### 1.1 Abnormal Uterine Bleeding (AUB)

#### 1.1.1 Clinical Overview

Abnormal uterine bleeding refers to bleeding from the uterus occurring outside pregnancy in reproductive-aged women that is abnormal in regularity, frequency, volume, or duration. Up to one-third of women will experience AUB in their lifetime, with irregularities most common at menarche and perimenopause [^387^].

#### 1.1.2 PALM-COEIN Classification (FIGO, ACOG-Endorsed)

The PALM-COEIN system categorizes AUB into structural and non-structural etiologies [^387^][^393^]:

**Structural Causes (PALM):**
| Letter | Condition | Key Features |
|--------|-----------|--------------|
| **P** | Polyps | Endometrial or cervical; often cause intermenstrual bleeding |
| **A** | Adenomyosis | Endometrial tissue within myometrium; diffuse uterine enlargement, tender |
| **L** | Leiomyoma (Fibroids) | Subclassified by location (submucosal, intramural, subserosal); most common structural cause |
| **M** | Malignancy/Hyperplasia | Endometrial cancer risk increases with age >45, obesity, PCOS, unopposed estrogen |

**Non-Structural Causes (COEIN):**
| Letter | Condition | Key Features |
|--------|-----------|--------------|
| **C** | Coagulopathy | vWD most common; heavy bleeding since menarche suggests bleeding disorder [^395^] |
| **O** | Ovulatory Dysfunction | Most common cause of AUB; PCOS, thyroid, hyperprolactinemia, perimenopause |
| **E** | Endometrial | Primary endometrial disorder; diagnosis of exclusion |
| **I** | Iatrogenic | Hormonal contraceptives, anticoagulants, IUDs, SSRIs |
| **N** | Not Otherwise Classified | Endometritis, arteriovenous malformations (AVM) |

#### 1.1.3 Diagnostic Workup

**Initial Evaluation:**
- Detailed menstrual history (cycle length, duration, volume, regularity)
- Pregnancy test (mandatory)
- CBC with ferritin (assess for anemia)
- TSH, prolactin (evaluate endocrine causes)
- Screening for coagulopathy in adolescents with heavy bleeding since menarche [^395^]
  - Structured history instrument is 90% sensitive for coagulopathy [^395^]
  - Positive screen: von Willebrand testing, ristocetin cofactor, factor VIII

**Imaging:**
- Transvaginal ultrasound (first-line imaging)
- Saline-infusion sonohysterography (evaluate endometrial cavity)
- Hysteroscopy with directed biopsy (gold standard for focal lesions)

**Endometrial Sampling Indications:**
- Age >= 45 with new-onset AUB
- Age < 45 with risk factors for endometrial cancer (obesity, PCOS, unopposed estrogen)
- Failed medical management
- Persistent intermenstrual bleeding
- Any age with abnormal ultrasound (thick/endometrial irregularity)

#### 1.1.4 Management

**Acute Heavy Bleeding (ACOG Committee Opinion #557):** [^393^]

| Treatment | Dosing | Notes |
|-----------|--------|-------|
| IV Conjugated Equine Estrogen | 25 mg IV every 4-6 hours | Stabilizes endometrium; most rapid option |
| Combined Oral Contraceptives | Multidose regimens (e.g., 3 pills daily x 7 days, taper) | Effective for hemodynamically stable patients |
| Oral Progestins | Medroxyprogesterone 20 mg TID or norethindrone 5 mg TID | Stabilizes endometrium |
| Tranexamic Acid | 1.3 g PO TID or 10 mg/kg IV | Antifibrinolytic; contraindicated with active VTE |

**Chronic AUB Management:**

| Etiology | Medical Options | Surgical Options |
|----------|----------------|------------------|
| Polyps | N/A | Hysteroscopic polypectomy |
| Adenomyosis | LNG-IUD, GnRH agonists with add-back, COCs, NSAIDs | Hysterectomy (definitive); adenomyomectomy (fertility-sparing, rare) |
| Leiomyomas | LNG-IUD, GnRH agonists, GnRH antagonists (elagolix, relugolix), tranexamic acid, NSAIDs | Myomectomy, uterine artery embolization, endometrial ablation, hysterectomy, radiofrequency ablation |
| Ovulatory Dysfunction | COCs, cyclic progestins, treat underlying cause | Endometrial ablation (if no fertility desires), hysterectomy |
| Coagulopathy | Tranexamic acid, DDAVP (vWD), hematology referral | - |

**Key Medical Therapies:**
- **LNG-IUD (52 mg):** First-line for heavy menstrual bleeding; reduces bleeding 80-90% by 12 months [^388^]
- **Tranexamic acid:** 1.3 g PO TID during menses; reduces bleeding 40-60% [^388^]
- **GnRH agonists (leuprolide):** Bridge to surgery; 3-6 months with add-back therapy
- **GnRH antagonists with add-back (elagolix, relugolix):** FDA-approved for AUB-L; up to 24 months [^394^]
- **NSAIDs:** Reduce bleeding 20-40%; start at menses onset

**Surgical Options:**
- Endometrial ablation: Alternative to hysterectomy; not for fertility preservation
- Uterine artery embolization: For fibroids; fertility data limited [^394^]
- Myomectomy: Fertility-preserving; laparoscopic/hysteroscopic approach preferred
- Hysterectomy: Definitive; vaginal approach preferred when feasible [^394^]

#### 1.1.5 Red Flags
- Hemodynamic instability (tachycardia, hypotension)
- Acute heavy bleeding with hemoglobin < 8 g/dL
- Postmenopausal bleeding (always requires evaluation)
- Fever with bleeding (possible infection/sepsis)

### 1.2 Dysmenorrhea

#### 1.2.1 Clinical Overview

Dysmenorrhea is the most common gynecologic condition affecting women of reproductive age, with prevalence estimates of 45-95% worldwide. It is a leading cause of school/work absenteeism [^441^][^450^].

**Primary Dysmenorrhea:** Painful menstruation in the absence of pelvic pathology. Begins 6-12 months after menarche (when ovulatory cycles establish). Pathophysiology driven by overproduction of prostaglandin F2a causing myometrial contraction and vasoconstriction [^445^].

**Secondary Dysmenorrhea:** Painful menstruation due to underlying pelvic pathology. Most common cause is endometriosis; others include adenomyosis, infection/PID, myomas, Mullerian anomalies, ovarian cysts [^445^].

**Key Distinguishing Features:**
| Feature | Primary | Secondary |
|---------|---------|-----------|
| Onset | Early menarche (6-12 months after) | Later onset; may develop years after menarche |
| Character | Cramping, colicky; starts with menses | May begin before menses; progressive worsening |
| Associated | Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, headache | Dyspareunia, abnormal bleeding, infertility |
| Physical Exam | Normal | May reveal masses, tenderness, nodularity |

#### 1.2.2 Management

**First-Line Treatment (NSAIDs):** [^441^][^450^]
- Ibuprofen: 400-800 mg every 6-8 hours (max 2400 mg/day)
- Naproxen: 500 mg loading, then 250-500 mg every 12 hours
- Mefenamic acid: 500 mg loading, then 250 mg every 6 hours
- All NSAIDs are superior to placebo; no significant differences between agents for pain relief [^441^]
- **Optimal use:** Start 1-2 days before menses onset and continue through first 2-3 days of bleeding
- Contraindications: Renal insufficiency, peptic ulcer disease, bleeding disorders

**First-Line Treatment (Hormonal Contraception):** [^441^][^444^]
- Combined oral contraceptives (COCs): Cyclic or continuous/extended regimens
- Levonorgestrel IUD: Highly effective; 20% amenorrhea rate
- Etonogestrel implant: May reduce menstrual pain
- Depot medroxyprogesterone acetate: 150 mg IM every 3 months
- Progestin-only pills: If estrogen contraindicated
- Mechanism: Suppress ovulation, reduce endometrial proliferation -> decreased prostaglandin production

**Second-Line Options:**
- Acetaminophen: Less effective than NSAIDs; useful for mild symptoms or as adjunct
- GnRH analogs (elagolix 400 mg daily, relugolix 20-40 mg daily): For endometriosis-related dysmenorrhea

**Non-Pharmacologic:** [^441^][^450^]
- Heat therapy: Strong evidence for efficacy
- Exercise: Substantial supporting data
- TENS: Moderate evidence
- Acupuncture: Some evidence; use as adjunct

**Surgical (Reserved for Refractory Cases):**
- Endometrial ablation
- Presacral neurectomy
- Laparoscopic uterosacral nerve ablation (LUNA)

#### 1.2.3 When to Suspect Secondary Dysmenorrhea
- Pain begins after years of painless periods
- Progressive worsening
- Dyspareunia
- Abnormal uterine bleeding
- Infertility
- Abnormal pelvic exam or imaging

### 1.3 Endometriosis

#### 1.3.1 Clinical Overview

Endometriosis is characterized by the presence of endometrial-like tissue outside the uterus, causing a chronic inflammatory reaction. It affects approximately 10% of reproductive-age women and is a leading cause of chronic pelvic pain and infertility.

**Common Presentations:**
- Dysmenorrhea (progressive)
- Chronic pelvic pain
- Dyspareunia (deep)
- Infertility
- Cyclical bowel/bladder symptoms
- Ovarian endometriomas ("chocolate cysts")

#### 1.3.2 Diagnosis

**Clinical Diagnosis:** Empiric treatment can be initiated based on history and examination without laparoscopic confirmation in most cases.

**Definitive Diagnosis:** Laparoscopy with histologic confirmation (gold standard).

**Imaging:**
- Transvaginal ultrasound: Detects ovarian endometriomas and deep infiltrating lesions
- MRI: For deep infiltrating endometriosis, especially rectovaginal lesions

**Biomarkers:** No reliable serum biomarker for routine clinical use.

#### 1.3.3 Medical Management (ESHRE 2022 Guidelines) [^390^]

**First-Line: Hormonal Contraceptives and Progestogens:**

| Class | Agents | Notes |
|-------|--------|-------|
| Combined hormonal contraceptives | COCs (oral, patch, ring) | Strong recommendation; continuous use preferred for dysmenorrhea |
| Progestogens | Dienogest, norethindrone, medroxyprogesterone | Strong recommendation; various formulations |
| LNG-IUS (Mirena) | 52 mg levonorgestrel IUD | Strong recommendation; excellent long-term option |
| Etonogestrel implant | Nexplanon | Strong recommendation |

**Second-Line: GnRH Agonists:** [^390^]
- Leuprolide, goserelin, nafarelin
- Highly effective for pain reduction
- **Must use with add-back therapy** (estrogen + progestin) to prevent bone loss and hypoestrogenic symptoms
- Recommended as second-line due to side effect profile
- Continuous add-back: 0.625 mg CEE + 2.5 mg medroxyprogesterone daily (or transdermal estradiol + progestin)

**GnRH Antagonists:**
- Elagolix (Orilissa): 150 mg daily (up to 24 months) or 200 mg BID (up to 6 months)
- Relugolix (Myfembree): 40 mg daily with estradiol 1.0 mg + norethindrone 0.5 mg (add-back)
- Significantly reduce VMS and pain scores
- Bone loss concern: Limit duration per labeling

**Other Options:**
- **Aromatase inhibitors** (letrozole, anastrozole): For refractory cases; use with OCP/progestin/GnRH agonist [^390^]
- **NSAIDs:** For pain adjunct; no disease-modifying effect

#### 1.3.4 Surgical Management

**Indications:**
- Diagnostic confirmation
- Failed medical management
- Endometriomas > 4 cm
- Bowel/bladder/ureteral involvement
- Fertility treatment optimization

**Approaches:**
- **Laparoscopic excision:** Preferred over ablation; superior pain outcomes
- **Cystectomy for endometriomas:** Reduces recurrence vs drainage alone; may reduce ovarian reserve
- **Hysterectomy with BSO:** Definitive for women not desiring fertility
- **Preservation of fertility:** Conservative excision; ovarian stimulation outcomes may be affected

#### 1.3.5 Fertility Considerations
- Endometriosis is associated with infertility
- Early-stage disease: Surgical excision may improve spontaneous pregnancy rates
- Advanced disease: IVF often required
- Endometrioma excision before IVF: Controversial; discuss individual risks/benefits
- Post-surgery: Consider 6 months of GnRH agonist before IVF for stage III-IV

### 1.4 Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS)

#### 1.4.1 Clinical Overview

PCOS is the most common endocrine disorder in reproductive-age women (prevalence 6-15%). It is a heterogeneous metabolic and reproductive syndrome with lifelong implications extending beyond ovarian dysfunction [^391^].

**Pathophysiology:** Insulin resistance is a key contributing factor, augmenting LH-stimulated ovarian theca cell androgen production and inhibiting hepatic SHBG synthesis [^391^].

#### 1.4.2 Diagnosis: Modified Rotterdam Criteria [^389^][^391^][^392^]

PCOS is diagnosed when **2 of 3 criteria are met** (with exclusion of other disorders):

| Criterion | Recommended Diagnostic Standard |
|-----------|--------------------------------|
| **1. Hyperandrogenism** (Clinical and/or Biochemical) | Clinical: Modified Ferriman-Gallwey score >= 4-8 (ethnicity-dependent); acne, androgenetic alopecia. Biochemical: Elevated total or free testosterone, FAI, or bioavailable testosterone |
| **2. Oligo-anovulation** | Cycles > 35 days apart or < 8 menses/year; amenorrhea > 90 days (if > 3 years post-menarche) |
| **3. Polycystic Ovarian Morphology** | >= 20 follicles per ovary OR ovarian volume >= 10 mL (transvaginal, >= 8 MHz probe) |

**Exclusion of Other Disorders:**
- Thyroid dysfunction (TSH)
- Hyperprolactinemia (prolactin)
- Non-classical congenital adrenal hyperplasia (17-OHP)
- Cushing syndrome
- Androgen-secreting tumor (markedly elevated androgens with virilization)

**PCOS Phenotypes:**
| Phenotype | Features | Notes |
|-----------|----------|-------|
| A (Classic) | Hyperandrogenism + oligo-anovulation + PCOM | Most severe metabolic features |
| B | Hyperandrogenism + oligo-anovulation | |
| C | Hyperandrogenism + PCOM | "Ovulatory PCOS" |
| D | Oligo-anovulation + PCOM | "Mild" or "non-hyperandrogenic" |

**Special Populations:**
- **Adolescents:** Diagnosis requires hyperandrogenism + oligo-anovulation; PCOM not used. Irregular cycles are normal up to 3 years post-menarche [^391^].
- **Perimenopause:** PCOM criteria unreliable; history of PCOS features + long-term metabolic consequences guide diagnosis.

#### 1.4.3 Evaluation

**Initial Labs:**
- Test in early follicular phase (days 2-5): LH, FSH, estradiol
- TSH, prolactin, 17-OHP
- Total testosterone, calculated free testosterone or FAI
- Consider DHEAS, androstenedione if initial testing normal
- Fasting glucose, HbA1c, fasting insulin
- Lipid panel
- AMH (optional; not for routine diagnosis but may substitute for ultrasound in adults)

**Metabolic Screening:**
- BMI calculation
- Oral glucose tolerance test (recommended over fasting glucose; detects impaired glucose tolerance)
- Blood pressure
- Lipid panel

#### 1.4.4 Management

**Goals-Based Treatment:**

| Goal | Treatment Options |
|------|-------------------|
| **Menstrual regulation** | Combined hormonal contraceptives, cyclic progestins (medroxyprogesterone 10 mg x 10-14 days/month or norethindrone 5 mg), metformin |
| **Hirsutism/acne** | COCs (antiandrogenic progestins: drospirenone, cyproterone, dienogest), spironolactone 50-200 mg/day, topical eflornithine, mechanical hair removal |
| **Metabolic management** | Lifestyle modification (weight loss 5-10%), metformin 500-2000 mg/day |
| **Fertility** | Weight loss, letrozole (first-line ovulation induction), clomiphene citrate, metformin, gonadotropins, IVF |
| **Endometrial protection** | Progestin exposure (COCs, cyclic progestins, LNG-IUD) to prevent hyperplasia/cancer |

**Key Medications:**
- **Metformin:** Improves insulin sensitivity, aids weight loss, restores ovulation, reduces progression to T2DM. Start 500 mg daily, titrate to 1500-2000 mg/day.
- **Letrozole:** First-line for ovulation induction (superior to clomiphene); 2.5-7.5 mg days 3-7 of cycle.
- **Spironolactone:** Antiandrogen for hirsutism; use with reliable contraception (teratogenicity concern).

**Long-Term Health Considerations:**
- Increased risk of T2DM (screen every 1-3 years with OGTT)
- Increased risk of cardiovascular disease (address modifiable risk factors)
- Endometrial cancer risk (ensure regular withdrawal bleeds/progestin exposure)
- Mood disorders (screen for depression/anxiety)
- Obstructive sleep apnea (especially with obesity)

### 1.5 Uterine Fibroids (Leiomyomas)

#### 1.5.1 Clinical Overview

Uterine fibroids are the most common pelvic tumors in women, affecting up to 70% of white women and >80% of black women by age 50. They are benign smooth muscle tumors that cause symptoms based on size, number, and location.

**FIGO Classification System:**
- Type 0: Pedunculated intracavitary
- Type 1: Submucosal (< 50% intramural)
- Type 2: Submucosal (>= 50% intramural)
- Types 3-5: Intramural (various degrees)
- Type 6: Subserosal
- Type 7: Subserosal pedunculated
- Type 8: Other (cervical, parasitic)

**Symptoms:** Heavy menstrual bleeding, bulk symptoms (pelvic pressure, urinary frequency, constipation), dysmenorrhea, infertility/recurrent pregnancy loss (especially submucosal).

#### 1.5.2 Management (ACOG Practice Bulletin, 2021) [^394^]

**Expectant Management:** Appropriate for asymptomatic patients or those with mild symptoms.

**Medical Management:**

| Therapy | Dosing | Duration | Notes |
|---------|--------|----------|-------|
| **LNG-IUD 52 mg** | Intrauterine | Long-term | First-line for AUB-L; reduces bleeding 70-90% |
| **Tranexamic acid** | 1.3 g PO TID during menses | Long-term | Non-hormonal; 40-60% bleeding reduction |
| **GnRH agonists** (leuprolide) | 3.75 mg IM monthly or 11.25 mg q3mo | Up to 6 months (bridge) | With add-back for extended use; 35-65% volume reduction |
| **GnRH antagonists + add-back** (elagolix, relugolix) | Per product labeling | Up to 24 months | FDA-approved for AUB-L [^394^] |
| **COCs** | Daily | Long-term | Moderate bleeding reduction |
| **NSAIDs** | During menses | Long-term | Modest benefit |

**Procedural/Surgical Options:** [^394^][^412^]

| Procedure | Best Candidates | Key Considerations |
|-----------|---------------|-------------------|
| **Hysteroscopic myomectomy** | Submucosal fibroids (Type 0-2) | Fertility-preserving; outpatient |
| **Laparoscopic/robotic myomectomy** | Intramural/subserosal; fertility desired | Morcellation concerns (use containment); recurrence 15-30% |
| **Uterine artery embolization (UAE)** | Women preserving uterus; no fertility desires | 1-2 day recovery; fertility data limited [^394^] |
| **Radiofrequency ablation** | Symptomatic fibroids; uterus preservation | Laparoscopic, transvaginal, or transcervical; limited fertility data [^394^] |
| **Endometrial ablation** | AUB-L only; no fertility desires | Minimally invasive; not for bulk symptoms |
| **Hysterectomy** | Definitive; failed other treatments; no fertility desires | Vaginal approach preferred; laparoscopic/vaginal > abdominal |

#### 1.5.3 Special Considerations
- **Fertility:** Submucosal fibroids (Types 0-2) clearly reduce fertility; removal recommended. Intramural fibroids > 4-5 cm may affect fertility.
- **Pregnancy:** Fibroids may grow during pregnancy; most managed expectantly. Red degeneration can cause acute pain.
- **Black women:** Higher prevalence, earlier onset, more severe symptoms; consider earlier intervention.

### 1.6 Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (PID)

#### 1.6.1 Clinical Overview

PID is an ascending infection of the upper female genital tract (endometrium, fallopian tubes, ovaries) typically caused by sexually transmitted pathogens (N. gonorrhoeae, C. trachomatis) and polymicrobial vaginal flora.

**Diagnostic Criteria (CDC STI Guidelines, 2021):** [^291^]
Minimum criteria for empiric treatment (in sexually active young women with pelvic/abdominal pain and one of the following):
- Cervical motion tenderness
- Uterine tenderness
- Adnexal tenderness

**Additional supportive criteria:** Fever > 38.3 C, abnormal cervical/vaginal discharge, elevated ESR/CRP, cervical N. gonorrhoeae or C. trachomatis infection.

#### 1.6.2 Treatment (CDC STI Guidelines, 2021) [^291^][^399^]

**Outpatient Regimen (Mild-Moderate):**

| Component | Dosing |
|-----------|--------|
| Ceftriaxone | 1 g IM single dose |
| PLUS Doxycycline | 100 mg PO BID x 14 days |
| PLUS Metronidazole | 500 mg PO BID x 14 days |

Alternative outpatient regimens:
- Cefoxitin 2 g IM + probenecid 1 g PO, plus doxycycline 100 mg BID x 14 days +/- metronidazole
- If cephalosporin allergy: Azithromycin or levofloxacin-based regimens

**Inpatient Regimen (Severe, Pregnant, Failed Outpatient, or Unable to Tolerate Oral):** [^291^][^399^]

| Regimen | Components |
|---------|------------|
| **A** | Cefotetan 2 g IV q12h OR cefoxitin 2 g IV q6h PLUS doxycycline 100 mg PO/IV q12h |
| **B** | Clindamycin 900 mg IV q8h PLUS gentamicin IV/IM (2 mg/kg load, then 1.5 mg/kg q8h) |
| **C** | Ampicillin/sulbactam 3 g IV q6h PLUS doxycycline 100 mg PO/IV q12h |

Transition to oral doxycycline + metronidazole to complete 14 days after clinical improvement (usually 24-48 hours).

**Indications for Hospitalization:** [^291^]
- Surgical emergencies cannot be excluded
- Tubo-ovarian abscess
- Pregnancy
- Severe illness, nausea/vomiting, temperature > 38.5 C (101 F)
- Unable to follow/tolerate outpatient oral regimen
- No clinical response to oral antimicrobial therapy

#### 1.6.3 Special Considerations
- **Test and treat partners:** Sexual partners within 60 days should be evaluated and treated empirically
- **Tubo-ovarian abscess:** Hospital admission; antibiotic therapy; percutaneous or surgical drainage if no response
- **Fertility counseling:** PID increases risk of infertility (10-15% after one episode, up to 50% after three), ectopic pregnancy, chronic pelvic pain
- **IUDs:** Can be left in place if clinically improving; remove if no improvement within 48-72 hours
- **Follow-up:** Re-examine within 72 hours for improvement

### 1.7 Vulvovaginal Candidiasis and Bacterial Vaginosis

#### 1.7.1 Bacterial Vaginosis (BV)

**Clinical Features:** Thin, homogeneous gray discharge; fishy odor; vaginal pH > 4.5; clue cells on microscopy. Amsel criteria (3 of 4): homogenous discharge, pH > 4.5, clue cells, positive whiff test. Nugent score >= 7 on Gram stain [^398^][^401^].

**Treatment (CDC STI Guidelines, 2021):** [^401^]

| Regimen | Dosing | Notes |
|---------|--------|-------|
| **Metronidazole** (recommended) | 500 mg PO BID x 7 days | No alcohol restriction needed (disulfiram-like reaction disproven) |
| **Metronidazole gel 0.75%** | 5 g intravaginally daily x 5 days | |
| **Clindamycin cream 2%** | 5 g intravaginally at bedtime x 7 days | Oil-based; may weaken latex condoms for 5 days |
| **Clindamycin** (oral) | 300 mg PO BID x 7 days | |
| **Clindamycin ovules** | 100 mg intravaginally at bedtime x 3 days | |
| **Secnidazole** | 2 g oral granules single dose | Alternative; higher cost |
| **Tinidazole** | 2 g PO daily x 2 days OR 1 g PO daily x 5 days | |

**Pregnancy:** Treat symptomatic BV in pregnancy; oral metronidazole or clindamycin [^401^].

**Recurrent BV (>= 3 episodes/year):** [^401^]
- Suppressive therapy: Metronidazole gel 0.75% twice weekly for 4-6 months
- Alternative: Oral nitroimidazole followed by intravaginal boric acid 600 mg daily x 21 days, then suppressive metronidazole gel
- Lactobacillus crispatus CTV-05 (Lactin-V) vaginal probiotic after metronidazole: Shows promise [^401^]
- Partner treatment: Evidence limited; may consider in recurrent cases

#### 1.7.2 Vulvovaginal Candidiasis (VVC)

**Clinical Features:** Vulvar pruritus, burning, soreness; thick white "cottage cheese" discharge; vulvar erythema/edema; vaginal pH typically normal (< 4.5).

**Uncomplicated VVC Treatment:** [^402^]

| Route | Agents | Duration |
|-------|--------|----------|
| Topical (OTC) | Butoconazole, clotrimazole, miconazole, tioconazole | 1-7 days |
| Oral | Fluconazole 150 mg | Single dose |

**Complicated VVC:** (Severe symptoms, non-albicans Candida, immunocompromised, recurrent, pregnancy)
- Severe: Fluconazole 150 mg every 72 hours x 2-3 doses OR topical azole 7-14 days
- Non-albicans: Fluconazole 800 mg x 3 doses OR boric acid 600 mg vaginal capsule daily x 14 days OR nystatin
- Pregnancy: Topical azole 7-day regimen (avoid oral fluconazole)

**Recurrent VVC (>= 3 episodes/year):** [^402^]
- Induction: Fluconazole 100-200 mg every 3rd day x 3 doses (days 1, 4, 7)
- Maintenance: Fluconazole 100-150-200 mg weekly x 6 months
- Cultures to confirm C. albicans vs non-albicans species
- Evaluate for underlying diabetes, immunosuppression

### 1.8 Ovarian Cysts

#### 1.8.1 Clinical Overview

Ovarian cysts are common and may be physiologic (functional) or pathologic. Most functional cysts resolve spontaneously.

**Functional Cysts:**
- Follicular cysts: Failed ovulation; usually < 3 cm; resolve in 1-2 cycles
- Corpus luteum cysts: Post-ovulatory; may cause pain, rupture, or hemorrhage; usually resolve in 4-8 weeks
- Theca lutein cysts: Associated with hCG stimulation (pregnancy, molar pregnancy, fertility treatments)

**Pathologic Cysts:**
- Endometriomas: "Chocolate cysts"; associated with endometriosis
- Dermoid cysts (mature cystic teratomas): Contain ectodermal elements; can cause torsion
- Cystadenomas: Serous or mucinous epithelial tumors
- Functional cysts persisting > 2-3 cycles

#### 1.8.2 Evaluation
- Transvaginal ultrasound: Characterize size, septations, solid components, papillary projections
- CA-125: Not routinely for premenopausal simple cysts; elevated in epithelial ovarian cancer and benign conditions
- IOTA criteria/ADNEX model: Risk stratification for malignancy
- O-RADS (Ovarian-Adnexal Reporting and Data System): Standardized risk stratification

#### 1.8.3 Management

**Premenopausal:**
| Type | Management |
|------|------------|
| Simple cyst < 5 cm | Expectant; repeat ultrasound in 6-12 weeks |
| Simple cyst 5-7 cm | Follow with serial ultrasounds; usually physiologic |
| Simple cyst > 7 cm | MRI for better characterization; may need surgical evaluation |
| Hemorrhagic cyst | Usually resolves spontaneously; follow-up if symptomatic |
| Endometrioma | Medical management for endometriosis; surgery if > 4 cm, symptomatic, or fertility concerns |
| Dermoid cyst | Surgical removal (risk of torsion) |

**Postmenopausal:**
| Type | Management |
|------|------------|
| Simple cyst < 3 cm | Normal ovarian morphology; no follow-up needed |
| Simple cyst 3-5 cm | Risk very low; follow-up optional |
| Simple cyst > 5 cm | Close follow-up or surgical evaluation |
| Complex features | Surgical evaluation warranted |

**Surgical Indications:**
- Persistent or enlarging cysts
- Symptomatic cysts (pain, torsion, rupture)
- Suspicious features (solid components, papillary projections, ascites)
- Large cysts (> 7-10 cm)
- Torsion (emergency)

**Ovarian Torsion (Emergency):** Acute onset unilateral pelvic pain with nausea/vomiting; ultrasound shows enlarged ovary with absent Doppler flow. Requires emergent surgical detorsion with ovarian preservation when possible; oophorectomy if nonviable.

### 2.1 Preconception Counseling

#### 2.1.1 Key Recommendations

**Folic Acid Supplementation:**
- All women of reproductive age: 400 mcg folic acid daily
- High-risk (prior NTD, on antiepileptics, pregestational diabetes, obesity): 4 mg daily starting 1 month before conception through first trimester
- Dietary sources: Fortified grains, leafy greens, legumes

**Chronic Disease Optimization:**
- Diabetes: Target HbA1c < 6.5% before conception; optimize medications (switch from ACE inhibitors, ARBs, statins)
- Hypertension: Optimize control; switch to pregnancy-safe agents (labetalol, nifedipine, methyldopa)
- Thyroid disease: Target TSH < 2.5; adjust levothyroxine (often needs 25-30% dose increase)
- Seizure disorders: Transition to lowest effective teratogenic regimen; folic acid 4 mg
- Obesity: Weight loss before conception improves outcomes

**Medication Safety Review:** [^435^]
- ACE inhibitors, ARBs (Category D/X): Switch before conception
- Statins (Category X): Discontinue
- Warfarin (Category X): Switch to LMWH
- Isotretinoin (Category X): Discontinue 1 month before conception; iPLEDGE program
- Valproic acid: Avoid if possible (NTD risk 1-2%)
- SSRIs: Generally safe; paroxetine may have small cardiac defect risk
- Preconception immunizations: MMR, varicella (live vaccines > 1 month before conception), Tdap, influenza

**Other Considerations:**
- Genetic carrier screening (cystic fibrosis, SMA, hemoglobinopathies, Ashkenazi Jewish panel)
- Rubella/varicella immunity testing
- Smoking, alcohol, substance use cessation
- Environmental toxin exposure assessment
- Dental care optimization

### 2.2 Antenatal Care Schedule

#### 2.2.1 Routine Prenatal Visits

**Initial Visit (8-10 weeks or first prenatal visit):**
- Comprehensive history (medical, obstetric, family, genetic, psychosocial)
- Physical examination including pelvic exam
- Confirm gestational age (LMP, early ultrasound)
- Baseline labs: CBC, blood type/Rh, antibody screen, urinalysis/urine culture
- Infectious disease screening: Hepatitis B, HIV, syphilis, gonorrhea/chlamydia
- Rubella/varicella immunity
- PAP smear if due
- First trimester aneuploidy screening options discussion
- Genetic carrier screening

**Subsequent Visits:**
| Gestational Age | Frequency | Key Components |
|-----------------|-----------|----------------|
| 10-28 weeks | Every 4 weeks | Weight, BP, urine dip, fundal height, FHR |
| 28-36 weeks | Every 2 weeks | Above + glucose screening (24-28 wks), Tdap (27-36 wks), Rhogam if Rh-negative (28 wks) |
| 36 weeks-delivery | Weekly | Above + GBS screening (36-37 wks), cervical exam as indicated |

**Ultrasound Schedule:**
- Dating ultrasound: 8-13 weeks (if uncertain dates)
- Anatomy scan: 18-22 weeks
- Growth ultrasound: As indicated (third trimester for high-risk)

**High-Risk Pregnancies:** May require additional visits, earlier/more frequent ultrasounds, specialist consultation (maternal-fetal medicine).

### 2.3 Common Pregnancy Complaints

#### 2.3.1 Nausea and Vomiting of Pregnancy (NVP) / Hyperemesis Gravidarum

**NVP:** Affects 70-80% of pregnancies; typically begins 4-6 weeks, peaks 9 weeks, resolves by 16-20 weeks.

**Treatment Algorithm:**
| Step | Intervention |
|------|-------------|
| 1 (First-line) | Dietary: Small frequent meals, avoid triggers, ginger 250 mg QID |
| 2 | Vitamin B6 (pyridoxine) 10-25 mg TID + doxylamine 12.5 mg TID |
| 3 | Doxylamine-pyridoxine combination (Diclegis/Diclectin) |
| 4 | Promethazine, metoclopramide, prochlorperazine |
| 5 | Ondansetron (though data on safety in first trimester evolving) |
| 6 | Corticosteroids (methylprednisolone) for refractory cases |

**Hyperemesis Gravidarum:** Severe NVP with weight loss (> 5% prepregnancy weight), ketonuria, electrolyte abnormalities. Requires hospitalization for IV fluids, electrolyte correction, antiemetics, thiamine supplementation before dextrose-containing IV fluids.

#### 2.3.2 Other Common Complaints

| Complaint | Management |
|-----------|------------|
| **Constipation** | Increased fiber/water, exercise; docusate, psyllium, PEG 3350 |
| **Heartburn/GERD** | Dietary modifications, antacids, H2 blockers (ranitidine, famotidine), PPIs (omeprazole) |
| **Back pain** | Exercise, physical therapy, support belts, acetaminophen, heat |
| **Leg cramps** | Magnesium supplementation, stretching, hydration |
| **Hemorrhoids** | Fiber, fluids, stool softeners, topical hydrocortisone, witch hazel |
| **Varicose veins** | Compression stockings, elevation, exercise |
| **Insomnia** | Sleep hygiene, relaxation techniques; doxylamine, diphenhydramine if needed |
| **Round ligament pain** | Rest, position changes, support belts, acetaminophen |

### 2.4 Pregnancy Complications

#### 2.4.1 Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (GDM) [^425^][^429^][^431^]

**Screening:** Universal screening at 24-28 weeks; earlier screening in high-risk (obesity, prior GDM, glycosuria, PCOS, strong family history).

**Two-Step Approach (US Standard):**
- Step 1: 50 g 1-hour glucose challenge test (GCT); >= 140 mg/dL (some use 130-135) is abnormal
- Step 2: 100 g 3-hour OGTT (Carpenter-Coustan criteria): >= 2 abnormal values:
  - Fasting: >= 95 mg/dL
  - 1-hour: >= 180 mg/dL
  - 2-hour: >= 155 mg/dL
  - 3-hour: >= 140 mg/dL

**One-Step Approach (IADPSG/WHO):**
- 75 g 2-hour OGTT: >= 1 abnormal value (fasting >= 92, 1-hour >= 180, 2-hour >= 153)

**Management:**
- Nutrition counseling (carbohydrate counting); moderate exercise
- Glucose monitoring: Fasting < 95 mg/dL; 1-hour postprandial < 140 mg/dL; 2-hour postprandial < 120 mg/dL
- **Insulin:** First-line pharmacologic therapy; does not cross placenta [^429^]
  - Starting total daily dose: 0.7-1.0 units/kg/day
  - Split: 50% basal (NPH or glargine/detemir), 50% prandial (lispro or aspart)
- **Metformin:** Alternative for patients declining insulin; crosses placenta; 50% may still need insulin [^431^]
- **Glyburide:** NOT recommended as first-line; crosses placenta; inferior outcomes [^431^]

**Obstetric Management:**
- Growth ultrasounds every 3-4 weeks in third trimester
- Antenatal testing starting 32-34 weeks (if on insulin or poorly controlled)
- Delivery timing: 39-40 weeks for well-controlled GDM on diet; 39 weeks if on medications; earlier for poor control
- Consider cesarean if EFW >= 4500 g

**Postpartum:**
- Screen with 75 g 2-hour OGTT at 4-12 weeks postpartum [^425^]
- 15-70% risk of developing T2DM; screen every 1-3 years thereafter
- Counsel on lifestyle modifications to reduce future diabetes risk

#### 2.4.2 Preeclampsia and HELLP Syndrome [^424^][^426^][^427^]

**Definitions:**
- **Gestational hypertension:** New-onset BP >= 140/90 after 20 weeks without proteinuria or severe features
- **Preeclampsia:** Gestational hypertension PLUS proteinuria (>= 300 mg/24h or protein/creatinine >= 0.3) OR hypertension with thrombocytopenia, renal insufficiency, impaired liver function, pulmonary edema, or cerebral/visual symptoms
- **Severe features:** [^426^][^427^]
  - SBP >= 160 or DBP >= 110 (twice, 4 hours apart)
  - Thrombocytopenia (platelets < 100,000)
  - Impaired liver function (transaminases 2x normal or RUQ/epigastric pain)
  - Progressive renal insufficiency (Cr > 1.1 or doubling)
  - Pulmonary edema
  - New-onset cerebral or visual disturbances
- **HELLP syndrome:** Hemolysis (schistocytes, LDH > 600, bilirubin > 1.2), Elevated Liver enzymes (2x normal), Low Platelets (< 100,000) [^428^]
- **Eclampsia:** New-onset seizures in preeclampsia

**Prevention:**
- Low-dose aspirin 81 mg daily for high-risk and multiple moderate-risk factors [^427^]
- Initiate between 12-28 weeks (optimally before 16 weeks)
- Continue until delivery

**Management:**

| Condition | Management |
|-----------|------------|
| Gestational HTN/Preeclampsia without severe features at >= 37 weeks | Delivery recommended |
| Preeclampsia with severe features at >= 34 weeks | Delivery after stabilization |
| Preeclampsia with severe features at < 34 weeks | Expectant management if stable; antenatal corticosteroids; deliver if maternal/fetal deterioration |
| Eclampsia | Magnesium sulfate + control BP + deliver after stabilization |

**Magnesium Sulfate Protocol:** [^427^][^428^]
- Loading: 4-6 g IV over 20-30 minutes
- Maintenance: 1-2 g/hour IV continuous
- Continue 24 hours postpartum or after last seizure
- Monitor: Deep tendon reflexes, urine output, respiratory rate
- Toxicity signs: Loss of DTR at 8-12 mg/dL; respiratory paralysis > 12 mg/dL; cardiac arrest > 24 mg/dL
- Antidote: Calcium gluconate 1 g (10 mL of 10%) slow IV push

**Antihypertensive Therapy in Pregnancy:**
- Acute severe hypertension (>= 160/110): Labetalol IV, hydralazine IV, or oral nifedipine
- Chronic: Labetalol, nifedipine, methyldopa
- **Contraindicated:** ACE inhibitors, ARBs, spironolactone (teratogenic)

**HELLP Management:** [^428^]
- Hospital admission to tertiary care
- Magnesium sulfate + BP control
- Immediate delivery if >= 34 weeks
- If < 34 weeks and stable: 48 hours of expectant management for corticosteroids
- Platelet transfusion if < 50,000 for surgery
- Monitor CBC, liver enzymes q12h until improving

#### 2.4.3 Anemia in Pregnancy
- Physiologic anemia: Dilutional (plasma volume expands more than red cell mass)
- Iron deficiency anemia: Hgb < 11 g/dL (1st/3rd trimester) or < 10.5 g/dL (2nd trimester)
- Treatment: Ferrous sulfate 325 mg (65 mg elemental iron) daily; increase to BID/TID if deficient
- IV iron: For malabsorption, intolerance, or moderate-severe anemia (3rd trimester)
- Folate deficiency: Folic acid 1-4 mg daily
- Sickle cell disease: Higher transfusion thresholds; multidisciplinary care

#### 2.4.4 Urinary Tract Infections
- Asymptomatic bacteriuria: Screen at first prenatal visit; treat if >= 10^5 CFU (reduces pyelonephritis risk)
- Cystitis: Nitrofurantoin, cephalexin, fosfomycin (avoid TMP-SMX in 1st trimester; avoid near term)
- Pyelonephritis: Hospital admission; IV ceftriaxone or ampicillin + gentamicin; 10-14 day course

#### 2.4.5 Group B Streptococcus (GBS)
- Universal screening at 36 0/7 - 37 6/7 weeks
- Vaginal-rectal culture
- Intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis (IAP) if GBS-positive:
  - Penicillin G 5 million units IV load, then 2.5-3 million units IV q4h until delivery
  - Alternative: Cefazolin, clindamycin (if susceptible), vancomycin (penicillin-allergic, high-risk)
- IAP also indicated if: Prior infant with invasive GBS disease, GBS bacteriuria this pregnancy, unknown status with risk factors (delivery < 37 weeks, ROM >= 18 hours, intrapartum fever)

### 2.5 Medication Safety in Pregnancy

#### 2.5.1 FDA Pregnancy Categories (Historical) [^435^]

| Category | Definition |
|----------|------------|
| **A** | Adequate studies in pregnant women show no risk (e.g., folic acid, levothyroxine) |
| **B** | Animal studies show no risk but human studies inadequate; OR animal studies show adverse effect but human studies show no risk (e.g., penicillins, cephalosporins, metformin, loratadine) |
| **C** | Animal studies show adverse effects; no adequate human studies; potential benefits may warrant use (e.g., SSRIs, fluconazole, glyburide) |
| **D** | Evidence of human fetal risk; benefits may be acceptable despite risk (e.g., ACE inhibitors [2nd/3rd trimester], phenytoin, carbamazepine, lithium) |
| **X** | Contraindicated; fetal abnormalities in animals and humans; risks clearly outweigh benefits (e.g., isotretinoin, methotrexate, warfarin, statins) |

**Note:** FDA replaced the A/B/C/D/X letter categories in 2015 with a narrative-based system (Pregnancy and Lactation Labeling Rule - PLLR), but the letter categories are still widely referenced.

#### 2.5.2 Known Teratogens

| Agent | Teratogenic Effect | Timing |
|-------|-------------------|--------|
| Isotretinoin | Craniofacial, cardiac, CNS defects | 1st trimester |
| Warfarin | Nasal hypoplasia, stippled epiphyses, CNS defects | 1st trimester (also 2nd/3rd) |
| Valproic acid | Neural tube defects (1-2%), craniofacial defects | 1st trimester |
| Methotrexate | Craniofacial, limb defects | 1st trimester |
| ACE inhibitors/ARBs | Renal agenesis, oligohydramnios, IUGR | 2nd/3rd trimester |
| Lithium | Ebstein anomaly (small absolute risk: 0.05-0.1%) | 1st trimester |
| Thalidomide | Limb defects | 1st trimester |
| Alcohol | Fetal alcohol spectrum disorders | Any; dose-dependent |

#### 2.5.3 Safe Medications in Pregnancy

| Category | Safe Options |
|----------|-------------|
| Analgesics | Acetaminophen (1st line); avoid NSAIDs in 3rd trimester |
| Antibiotics | Penicillins, cephalosporins, azithromycin, nitrofurantoin (avoid at term), metronidazole |
| Antihistamines | Loratadine, cetirizine, diphenhydramine, chlorpheniramine |
| Antacids | Calcium carbonate, H2 blockers, PPIs |
| Antihypertensives | Labetalol, nifedipine, methyldopa, hydralazine |
| Antiemetics | Vitamin B6 + doxylamine, metoclopramide, promethazine |
| Thyroid | Levothyroxine |
| Anticoagulants | LMWH (enoxaparin), unfractionated heparin |

### 2.6 Postpartum Care

#### 2.6.1 Postpartum Visit Timeline
- Early postpartum contact: Within 1-3 weeks (earlier for high-risk)
- Comprehensive visit: By 12 weeks postpartum
- Ongoing care as needed

#### 2.6.2 Postpartum Depression (PPD)
- Affects 10-20% of postpartum women
- Screen with EPDS (Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale) or PHQ-9 at postpartum visit
- Risk factors: Prior depression/anxiety, PPH, infant complications, lack of support, traumatic delivery
- **Treatment:**
  - Psychotherapy (CBT, IPT): First-line for mild-moderate
  - SSRIs (sertraline, paroxetine preferred for breastfeeding): Moderate-severe
  - Brexanolone (Zulresso): IV treatment for severe PPD (FDA-approved); requires monitored infusion setting
- **Red flags:** Postpartum psychosis (medical emergency; involves delusions/hallucinations/homicidal or suicidal ideation)

#### 2.6.3 Postpartum Thyroiditis
- Affects 5-10% of women; typically occurs 1-6 months postpartum
- **Phase 1:** Transient hyperthyroidism (2-4 months) - usually mild, often not treated
- **Phase 2:** Hypothyroidism (4-8 months) - treat if symptomatic or TSH > 10
- Most recover by 12 months; 20-40% develop permanent hypothyroidism
- Screen if symptoms suggestive; follow TSH patterns

#### 2.6.4 Contraception Postpartum
- **Lactational amenorrhea method (LAM):** Effective if < 6 months postpartum, amenorrheic, exclusively breastfeeding
- **Progestin-only methods:** Safe immediately postpartum; preferred for breastfeeding (POPs, DMPA, implant, LNG-IUD)
- **Combined hormonal contraceptives:** Delay until 21 days postpartum (if not breastfeeding) or 6 weeks (if breastfeeding) due to VTE risk
- **IUD placement:** Immediately postpartum (within 10 minutes of placental delivery), at 4+ weeks, or delayed; immediate placement has higher expulsion rate

#### 2.6.5 Breastfeeding
- Exclusive breastfeeding recommended for first 6 months, then continued with complementary foods for >= 1 year
- Contraindications: Maternal HIV (in developed countries), certain medications (chemotherapy, radioactive compounds), active untreated TB, HTLV-1/2, infant galactosemia
- Common issues: Sore nipples, mastitis, low supply, latch difficulties; lactation consultation support

## Section 3: Menopause

---

### 3.1 Perimenopause and Diagnosis

#### 3.1.1 Clinical Overview

The menopausal transition (perimenopause) is the period from the onset of menstrual cycle changes to 12 months after the final menstrual period. Average age of natural menopause is 51 years. The transition typically lasts 4-8 years.

**Stages (STRAW + 10):**
- Early perimenopause: Increasing cycle variability (> 7 days difference from normal)
- Late perimenopause: Amenorrhea >= 60 days; increased vasomotor symptoms
- Menopause: 12 months of amenorrhea (no other cause)
- Postmenopause: Begins after 12 months of amenorrhea

#### 3.1.2 Symptoms

| Category | Symptoms |
|----------|----------|
| **Vasomotor** | Hot flashes, night sweats (affect 60-80% of women) |
| **Genitourinary** | Vaginal dryness, dyspareunia, urinary urgency/frequency, recurrent UTIs |
| **Psychological** | Mood changes, anxiety, irritability, cognitive concerns ("brain fog") |
| **Sleep** | Insomnia, disrupted sleep |
| **Physical** | Joint aches, headaches, palpitations, skin changes |
| **Menstrual** | Irregular cycles, heavier or lighter bleeding |

#### 3.1.3 Diagnosis
- **Clinical diagnosis** in women > 45 with typical symptoms
- **FSH is NOT required** for diagnosis in women with typical age-appropriate symptoms
- FSH > 25-30 IU/L on two occasions (4-6 weeks apart) may help in:
  - Women < 45 with suspected premature ovarian insufficiency
  - Women who have had hysterectomy (no menstrual marker)
  - Diagnostic uncertainty
- AMH may reflect remaining ovarian reserve but is not diagnostic of menopause

---

### 3.2 Menopausal Hormone Therapy (MHT)

#### 3.2.1 Benefits and Indications [^440^]

MHT is the most effective treatment for vasomotor symptoms and genitourinary syndrome of menopause.

**Indications:**
- Moderate to severe vasomotor symptoms (first-line)
- Genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM)
- Prevention of bone loss/fractures (in select symptomatic women at high fracture risk)

**Timing Hypothesis:** [^440^]
- MHT initiated within 10 years of menopause onset or before age 60 shows the most favorable benefit-risk profile
- This is when MHT may have neutral or beneficial cardiovascular effects
- Initiation > 10 years after menopause or after age 60 may increase cardiovascular risk

#### 3.2.2 Transdermal vs Oral Estrogen [^440^]

| Feature | Oral Estrogen | Transdermal Estrogen |
|---------|--------------|----------------------|
| **First-pass metabolism** | Yes | No |
| **Effect on SHBG** | Increases significantly | Minimal increase |
| **Effect on lipids** | Increases HDL, triglycerides | Neutral/minimal |
| **VTE risk** | Higher (OR ~3-4) | Lower (OR ~1-2) |
| **Stroke risk** | Slightly increased | Lower |
| **Breast cancer risk** | May be higher with long-term CEE+MPA | Potentially lower |
| **Blood pressure** | May increase | Neutral |
| **Gallbladder disease** | Increased | Less risk |
| **Convenience** | Daily pill | Patch change 1-2x/week |
| **Dosing** | CEE 0.3-0.625 mg; estradiol 0.5-2 mg | Estradiol 0.025-0.1 mg/day |

**Recommendation:** Transdermal route preferred for women with: [^440^]
- Increased VTE risk (obesity, age > 60, smokers)
- Cardiovascular risk factors
- Hypertriglyceridemia
- Gallbladder disease
- Migraine without aura (avoid estrogen if migraine with aura)
- Liver disease

#### 3.2.3 Progestogen Component

**Required if uterus present:** To prevent endometrial hyperplasia/cancer.

| Regimen | Options |
|---------|---------|
| **Continuous combined** | Estrogen + progestin daily (no withdrawal bleeding) |
| **Cyclic/sequential** | Estrogen daily + progestin 12-14 days/month (predictable withdrawal bleeding) |

**Progestogen Options:**
- Micronized progesterone 100-200 mg: Favorable metabolic profile; may improve sleep; lower breast cancer risk
- Medroxyprogesterone acetate 2.5-5 mg: Most studied
- Norethindrone acetate 0.5-1 mg
- LNG-IUD (off-label for endometrial protection): For women who cannot tolerate systemic progestin

**Micronized progesterone advantages:** [^440^]
- No increase in VTE risk
- No adverse metabolic effects
- May have lower breast cancer risk than synthetic progestins
- Improves sleep quality

#### 3.2.4 Risks and Contraindications

**Absolute Contraindications:**
- Known or suspected breast cancer
- Known or suspected estrogen-dependent neoplasia
- Undiagnosed abnormal genital bleeding
- Active or history of VTE (DVT/PE)
- Active or recent arterial thromboembolic disease (stroke, MI)
- Severe hepatic disease
- Known hypersensitivity
- Pregnancy

**Relative Contraindications (individualize risk):**
- Migraine with aura (US MEC Category 4 for CHC; discuss carefully for MHT)
- History of breast cancer (case-by-case with oncology)
- Risk factors for VTE (use transdermal route)
- Cardiovascular risk factors
- Hypertriglyceridemia
- Gallbladder disease
- Endometriosis

#### 3.2.5 Duration of Therapy
- Individualize based on symptoms and risk factors
- Annual re-evaluation of benefits vs. risks
- No mandatory discontinuation at specific age
- If continuing > 60 or > 10 years since menopause, use lowest effective dose, transdermal preferred
- Taper rather than abrupt discontinuation to minimize symptom recurrence

### 3.3 Non-Hormonal Options for Vasomotor Symptoms

#### 3.3.1 FDA-Approved Non-Hormonal Therapy

**Fezolinetant (Veozah) - NK3 Receptor Antagonist:** [^417^][^418^][^420^]
- **FDA approved:** May 2023
- **Mechanism:** Neurokinin-3 receptor antagonist; blocks NKB signaling in hypothalamic KNDy neurons to normalize thermoregulation
- **Dosing:** 45 mg PO once daily
- **Efficacy:** 50-95% reduction in VMS frequency; onset within 1 week; sustained at 12 weeks
- **Side effects:** Headache (~9%), fatigue, nausea, abdominal pain, insomnia, elevated hepatic transaminases
- **Monitoring:** Baseline LFTs; repeat at 3, 6, and 9 months
- **Contraindications:** Cirrhosis, severe renal impairment (eGFR < 15), CYP1A2 inhibitors
- **Advantages:** No hormonal effects; safe for breast cancer survivors; no VTE risk

#### 3.3.2 Other Non-Hormonal Pharmacologic Options

| Agent | Dosing | Efficacy | Side Effects |
|-------|--------|----------|--------------|
| **SSRIs/SNRIs** | Paroxetine 7.5 mg (Brisdelle); venlafaxine 75 mg; escitalopram 10-20 mg | 40-60% reduction in VMS | Sexual dysfunction, GI upset, insomnia |
| **Gabapentin** | 300-900 mg at bedtime | 45-60% reduction | Drowsiness, dizziness, weight gain |
| **Clonidine** | 0.1 mg patch or 0.05-0.1 mg PO BID | 20-40% reduction | Dry mouth, hypotension, fatigue |
| **Oxybutynin** | 2.5-5 mg BID-TID | 50-70% reduction | Anticholinergic effects |

#### 3.3.3 Non-Pharmacologic Approaches
- Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT): Evidence for symptom perception improvement
- Clinical hypnosis: Moderate evidence for VMS reduction
- Stellate ganglion block: Emerging evidence
- Lifestyle modifications: Layered clothing, fans, trigger avoidance (alcohol, spicy foods, hot beverages), weight loss, exercise
- Supplements: Limited evidence (black cohosh, phytoestrogens, omega-3s)

### 3.4 Long-Term Health Considerations

#### 3.4.1 Bone Health
- Bone loss accelerates in first 3-5 years after menopause (estrogen deficiency increases osteoclast activity)
- DXA screening at age 65 (earlier if risk factors)
- Risk factors for earlier screening: Prior fracture, glucocorticoid use, low body weight, smoking, alcohol abuse, parental hip fracture
- **Prevention:** Weight-bearing exercise, calcium (1200 mg/day), vitamin D (800-1000 IU/day), smoking cessation, limit alcohol
- **MHT:** Reduces vertebral and hip fractures during use; benefit limited to duration of therapy
- **Pharmacologic therapy for osteoporosis:** Bisphosphonates, denosumab, raloxifene, teriparatide

#### 3.4.2 Cardiovascular Risk
- Cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of death in postmenopausal women
- Estrogen decline contributes to adverse lipid changes and vascular dysfunction
- MHT is NOT indicated for cardiovascular prevention
- Screen and treat modifiable risk factors: Blood pressure, lipids, diabetes, smoking, obesity, physical inactivity
- Timing hypothesis: MHT started < 60 or < 10 years from menopause may have neutral/beneficial CV effects

#### 3.4.3 Genitourinary Syndrome of Menopause (GSM)

**Definition:** Collection of symptoms due to estrogen deficiency affecting vulvovaginal and urinary tissues.

**Symptoms:**
- Vaginal dryness, burning, irritation
- Dyspareunia (pain with intercourse)
- Vaginal laxity/shortening
- Urinary urgency, frequency, dysuria, recurrent UTIs
- Urinary incontinence

**Treatment:**

| Treatment | Dosing | Notes |
|-----------|--------|-------|
| **Low-dose vaginal estrogen** (cream, tablet, ring) | Estradiol 10 mcg tablet 2x/week; cream 0.5 g 2x/week; ring (Estring) changed q3mo | First-line; minimal systemic absorption; safe for most women including breast cancer survivors (discuss with oncology) |
| **Vaginal DHEA** (prasterone) | 6.5 mg vaginal insert nightly | Converts to estrogen/testosterone locally; FDA-approved |
| **Oral ospemifene** | 60 mg daily | SERM; FDA-approved for moderate-severe dyspareunia; boxed warning for VTE |
| **Vaginal moisturizers** | Regular use (e.g., Replens) | Non-hormonal; symptomatic relief |
| **Vaginal lubricants** | With intercourse | Water/silicone-based for intercourse comfort |
| **Vaginal laser/RF** | Series of treatments | Emerging; limited long-term safety data |
| **Pelvic floor PT** | As indicated | For urinary incontinence |

## Section 4: Contraception

### 4.1 Combined Hormonal Contraceptives (CHCs)

#### 4.1.1 Methods
| Method | Components | Dosing |
|--------|-----------|--------|
| **Combined oral contraceptives (COCs)** | EE 10-50 mcg + progestin | Daily |
| **Transdermal patch** | Norelgestromin/EE (Xulane, Twirla) | Weekly x 3 weeks |
| **Vaginal ring** | Etonogestrel/EE (NuvaRing); segesterone/EE (Annovera) | 3 weeks in/1 week out; or continuous |

#### 4.1.2 Effectiveness
- Perfect use: 99%+ effective
- Typical use: ~91-93% effective (COCs); higher for patch/ring due to better adherence

#### 4.1.3 Non-Contraceptive Benefits
- Regulate menstrual cycles, reduce dysmenorrhea, reduce HMB
- Treatment of acne, hirsutism
- Reduce ovarian and endometrial cancer risk
- Reduce risk of colorectal cancer
- Improve PMS/PMDD (especially with drospirenone-containing COCs)

#### 4.1.4 Side Effects and Risks
- Common: Breakthrough bleeding (first 3-6 months), breast tenderness, nausea, mood changes
- Serious (rare): VTE, stroke, MI (dose and risk-factor dependent)
- **VTE risk with CHCs:** Absolute risk small (~3-9/10,000 women/year vs. 1-5/10,000 non-users); risk varies by progestin type and estrogen dose

### 4.2 Progestin-Only Contraceptives

#### 4.2.1 Methods
| Method | Components | Duration |
|--------|-----------|----------|
| **Progestin-only pills (POPs)** | Norethindrone 0.35 mg, norgestrel, drospirenone 4 mg | Daily |
| **Etonogestrel implant** (Nexplanon) | 68 mg etonogestrel | 3 years (FDA); effective up to 5 years |
| **DMPA injection** (Depo-Provera) | 150 mg medroxyprogesterone acetate IM or 104 mg SC | 3 months |

#### 4.2.2 Effectiveness
- POPs: 99% perfect use; 91% typical use
- Implant: > 99.9% effective; most effective reversible method
- DMPA: 99.8% perfect use; 94% typical use

#### 4.2.3 Side Effects
- Irregular bleeding/spotting (most common; often improves over time)
- Amenorrhea (common with DMPA and implant)
- Weight gain (DMPA most associated; average 5-10 lbs in first year)
- Mood changes
- DMPA: Delayed return to fertility (average 10 months); concern for bone density with long-term use

### 4.3 Intrauterine Devices (IUDs)

#### 4.3.1 Types
| Device | Hormone/Dose | Duration | Bleeding Pattern |
|--------|-------------|----------|-----------------|
| **Copper IUD** (Paragard) | None | 10-12 years | Heavier menses, cramping |
| **LNG-IUD 52 mg** (Mirena, Liletta) | Levonorgestrel 52 mg | 8 years | Lighter menses, spotting |
| **LNG-IUD 19.5 mg** (Kyleena) | Levonorgestrel 19.5 mg | 5 years | Lighter menses |
| **LNG-IUD 13.5 mg** (Skyla) | Levonorgestrel 13.5 mg | 3 years | Lighter menses |

#### 4.3.2 Effectiveness
- All IUDs: > 99% effective
- Most cost-effective long-term reversible contraception

#### 4.3.3 Key Considerations
- **Copper IUD:** Non-hormonal; can be used as emergency contraception; may worsen dysmenorrhea/HMB
- **LNG-IUDs:** Reduce menstrual bleeding 70-90% by 12 months; 20% amenorrhea rate; treats dysmenorrhea
- Both types: Fertility returns immediately after removal
- Expulsion rate: ~5% in first year; higher immediately postpartum

### 4.4 Emergency Contraception (EC) [^403^][^404^]

#### 4.4.1 Methods

| Method | Timing | Mechanism | Efficacy |
|--------|--------|-----------|----------|
| **Levonorgestrel 1.5 mg** (Plan B One-Step) | Within 72 hours of UPSI (effective up to 120 hours) | Delays/inhibits ovulation | ~85% if within 72h; decreases with time/BMI |
| **Ulipristal acetate 30 mg** (Ella) | Within 120 hours (5 days) | Selective progesterone receptor modulator; delays ovulation | ~98% within 120h; more effective than LNG at 72-120h and in overweight women |
| **Copper IUD** | Within 5 days of UPSI (or within 5 days of ovulation) | Prevents fertilization/implantation | > 99% effective; most effective EC method; provides ongoing contraception |

#### 4.4.2 Important Considerations
- EC pills do NOT terminate an existing pregnancy
- EC pills are less effective in women with BMI > 30; ulipristal > levonorgestrel in this group; copper IUD preferred [^403^]
- After ulipristal: Wait 5 days before starting/resuming hormonal contraception
- After levonorgestrel: Can start hormonal contraception immediately
- Vomiting within 2-3 hours of EC pill: Repeat dose
- CYP3A4 inducers (enzyme-inducing drugs): Reduce EC pill efficacy; copper IUD preferred
- No prescription needed for levonorgestrel EC (OTC in most places); ulipristal requires prescription in many countries

### 4.7 Permanent Contraception

#### 4.7.1 Tubal Surgery (Female)
- **Bilateral tubal ligation:** Laparoscopic clips, rings, or fulguration
- **Bilateral salpingectomy:** Preferred over ligation; may reduce ovarian cancer risk
- **Hysteroscopic tubal occlusion** (Essure): No longer available in many countries

- Effectiveness: > 99%
- Reversal possible but expensive and not always successful

- Mammographic breast density is an independent risk factor for breast cancer
- Dense tissue makes cancer detection harder on mammography
- About 50% of women have dense breasts
- **Screening considerations for dense breasts:**
  - Tomosynthesis (3D mammography): Improved detection in dense breasts
  - Supplemental ultrasound: May find additional cancers; higher false positive rate
  - Contrast-enhanced mammography: Emerging role
  - MRI: For high-risk patients (lifetime risk > 20%)

### 5.2 BRCA Testing and Risk Assessment

#### 5.2.1 Risk Assessment Models [^442^][^446^][^451^]

| Model | Risk Factors | Best Use |
|-------|-------------|----------|
| **Tyrer-Cuzick (IBIS)** | Age, menarche, parity, BMI, HRT, family history, breast density, biopsy history | Lifetime risk calculation; 20% threshold triggers MRI eligibility |
| **Gail (BCRAT)** | Age, menarche, first live birth, biopsies, first-degree relatives | Chemoprevention eligibility; limited family history |
| **BRCAPRO** | Detailed family history, genetic testing results | BRCA mutation probability |
| **CanRisk/BOADICEA** | Comprehensive family history, genetic testing, risk factors, PRS | Integrated risk assessment |

#### 5.2.2 BRCA Testing Criteria (NCCN Guidelines) [^442^][^446^]

**Personal History Indicating Testing:**
- Breast cancer diagnosed <= 45 years
- Breast cancer diagnosed <= 60 years with triple-negative subtype
- Breast cancer at any age with Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry
- Ovarian cancer (epithelial) at any age
- Male breast cancer
- Pancreatic cancer with >= 1 close relative with breast/ovarian/pancreatic cancer
- Metastatic prostate cancer

**Family History Indicating Testing:**
- First- or second-degree relative meeting above criteria
- Known familial mutation in BRCA1/2 or other high/moderate penetrance gene
- >= 2 breast cancers in close relatives on same side of family
- Ovarian cancer in close relative
- Triple-negative breast cancer in relative <= 60 years

#### 5.2.3 Management of BRCA Carriers
| Modality | Recommendation |
|----------|---------------|
| **Breast screening** | Annual MRI starting age 25-30 + annual mammogram starting age 30 |
| **Risk-reducing mastectomy** | Reduces breast cancer risk > 90%; individual decision |
| **Risk-reducing BSO** | Recommended age 35-40 (BRCA1) or 40-45 (BRCA2) upon completion of childbearing |
| **Chemoprevention** | Tamoxifen/raloxifene reduces risk ~50% |
| **Ovarian cancer screening** | Transvaginal ultrasound + CA-125 (limited effectiveness; risk-reducing surgery preferred) |

### 5.3 Common Breast Complaints

#### 5.3.1 Palpable Breast Mass - The "Triple Test"

| Component | Purpose |
|-----------|---------|
| **1. Clinical breast examination** | Characterize mass (size, location, consistency, mobility, skin/nipple changes, lymph nodes) |
| **2. Imaging** | Age < 30: Ultrasound first; Age >= 30: Diagnostic mammogram + targeted ultrasound |
| **3. Tissue sampling** | FNA or core needle biopsy for solid masses; cyst aspiration for simple cysts |

**Interpretation:**
- All three components concordant (all benign or all suspicious): 99.5% accuracy
- Discordant results: Excisional biopsy required

#### 5.3.2 Mastalgia (Breast Pain)

**Cyclical:** Related to menstrual cycle; most common; often bilateral, upper outer quadrants
- Management: Supportive bra, dietary changes (reduce caffeine, fat), evening primrose oil (limited evidence), COCs, danazol (severe cases), tamoxifen (refractory)

**Non-cyclical:** Not related to menstrual cycle
- Evaluate for extramammary causes (chest wall, Tietze syndrome, cervical radiculopathy)
- Caffeine reduction, NSAIDs, bromocriptine (if prolactin elevated)

**Worrisome features requiring evaluation:**
- Focal, persistent pain
- Associated mass
- Skin changes (erythema, peau d'orange)
- Nipple changes (discharge, retraction)

#### 5.3.3 Nipple Discharge

| Characteristic | Likely Etiology | Workup |
|----------------|----------------|--------|
| **Bilateral, multi-duct, non-spontaneous, various colors** | Physiologic, duct ectasia, medication-related | Reassurance; stop manipulation; evaluate medications |
| **Unilateral, single-duct, spontaneous, bloody/serosanguineous** | Intraductal papilloma (most common), ductal carcinoma | Surgical referral; ductography; excisional biopsy |
| **Purulent** | Infection/abscess | Culture, antibiotics, drainage |
| **Milky (galactorrhea)** | Hyperprolactinemia, pregnancy, medication | Pregnancy test, prolactin level, medication review |
| **Green/brown/black** | Duct ectasia, fibrocystic changes | Usually benign; excision if associated mass |

**Medications causing nipple discharge:** Antipsychotics, antidepressants, COCs, antihypertensives (verapamil, methyldopa), H2 blockers, opioids, hormones

#### 5.3.4 Fibrocystic Changes
- Most common benign breast condition
- Characterized by breast pain, lumpiness, nodularity (often bilateral, fluctuating)
- Worse before menses
- Management: Supportive measures, NSAIDs, evening primrose oil, vitamin E (limited evidence)
- Aspiration of symptomatic cysts for diagnosis and treatment
- Core biopsy for solid components or complex cysts
- Not associated with increased breast cancer risk (unless atypical hyperplasia present)

## Section 6: Prostate Health

---

### 6.1 Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH)

#### 6.1.1 Clinical Overview

BPH is the most common cause of lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) in aging men. Histologic prevalence increases with age: ~50% by age 60 and ~90% by age 85. Symptoms result from static (prostate enlargement causing obstruction) and dynamic (alpha-adrenergic tone) components [^408^][^412^].

#### 6.1.2 Assessment

**History:** Focus on LUTS (storage vs. voiding symptoms), impact on quality of life.

**International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS):**
| Severity | Score |
|----------|-------|
| Mild | 0-7 |
| Moderate | 8-19 |
| Severe | 20-35 |

**Physical Examination:** DRE to assess prostate size and consistency.

**Recommended Workup:**
- IPSS questionnaire
- DRE
- Urinalysis
- Serum creatinine (renal function)
- PSA (if clinically appropriate and patient desires testing)
- Post-void residual (PVR) urine volume
- Uroflowmetry (optional)

#### 6.1.3 Medical Management [^408^][^412^]

**Watchful Waiting:** For mild symptoms (IPSS < 8) not bothersome. Lifestyle modifications: fluid restriction before bed, limit alcohol/caffeine, timed voiding, bladder training, pelvic floor exercises.

**Alpha-Blockers:** Relax smooth muscle in prostate and bladder neck

| Agent | Dosing | Onset | Notes |
|-------|--------|-------|-------|
| **Tamsulosin** (Flomax) | 0.4 mg daily | Days | Selective for alpha-1A; retrograde ejaculation most common |
| **Alfuzosin** (Uroxatral) | 10 mg daily | Days | |
| **Silodosin** (Rapaflo) | 8 mg daily | Days | Highly selective alpha-1A; high rate of ejaculatory dysfunction |
| **Doxazosin** (Cardura) | 1-8 mg daily | Days | Also antihypertensive |
| **Terazosin** (Hytrin) | 1-10 mg daily | Days | Also antihypertensive; titration required |

**5-Alpha Reductase Inhibitors (5-ARIs):** Reduce prostate volume by blocking DHT conversion

| Agent | Dosing | Onset | Notes |
|-------|--------|-------|-------|
| **Finasteride** (Proscar) | 5 mg daily | 3-6 months | Type II 5-AR inhibitor; reduces volume ~25%; PSA decreases ~50% |
| **Dutasteride** (Avodart) | 0.5 mg daily | 3-6 months | Dual Type I/II 5-AR inhibitor; slightly greater volume reduction |

**5-ARI considerations:**
- Only effective in men with enlarged prostate (typically > 30-40 mL)
- PSA reduction by ~50% after 6 months; must double measured PSA for accurate cancer screening
- Sexual side effects: Decreased libido, erectile dysfunction, ejaculatory disorders
- Must use reliable contraception (teratogenicity risk to male fetus)
- Reduce risk of acute urinary retention and need for BPH-related surgery

**Combination Therapy:** Alpha-blocker + 5-ARI more effective than either alone for men with enlarged prostate and moderate-severe symptoms.

**PDE5 Inhibitors:**
- **Tadalafil 5 mg daily:** FDA-approved for BPH/LUTS; improves erectile function simultaneously

**Other Agents:**
- Anticholinergics (oxybutynin, tolterodine): For predominant storage symptoms with adequate bladder emptying
- Beta-3 agonists (mirabegron): For storage symptoms
- Tamsulosin + dutasteride combination (Jalyn)

#### 6.1.4 Surgical and Procedural Options [^411^][^412^]

| Procedure | Best Candidates | Pros | Cons |
|-----------|---------------|------|------|
| **TURP** (Transurethral resection) | Moderate-large prostates; gold standard | Highly effective | Bleeding, TUR syndrome, retrograde ejaculation, incontinence |
| **HoLEP** (Holmium laser enucleation) | All sizes including very large | Complete enucleation; durable | Steep learning curve |
| **GreenLight laser** (PVP) | Small-large prostates | Less bleeding; outpatient | Less tissue for pathology |
| **Aquablation** | Prostate 30-80 g | Robotically guided waterjet; preserves sexual function | Newer technology |
| **UroLift** (Prostatic urethral lift) | Prostate 30-80 g, no middle lobe | Preserves sexual function; rapid recovery | May not work for large prostates |
| **Rezum** (Water vapor thermal) | Prostate 30-80 g | Preserves sexual function; office-based | Longer time to symptom relief |
| **TUNA/TUMT** | Legacy technologies | - | Mostly replaced by newer MIST |
| **Simple prostatectomy** | Very large prostates (> 75-100 g) | Definitive | Open/laparoscopic; invasive |
| **PAE** (Prostatic artery embolization) | Select patients | Minimally invasive | Radiation exposure; limited long-term data |

### 6.2 Prostate Cancer Screening

#### 6.2.1 Guidelines (AUA/SUO 2023) [^410^][^413^][^415^]

**Key Recommendations:**

| Statement | Grade |
|-----------|-------|
| PSA-based screening + shared decision-making is recommended | Strong, Grade A |
| PSA should be used as the first screening test | Strong, Grade A |
| Repeat PSA if newly elevated before secondary biomarker/imaging/biopsy | Expert Opinion |
| Offer baseline PSA to ages 45-50 | Conditional, Grade B |
| Offer screening at age 40-45 for increased risk (Black ancestry, germline mutations, strong family history) | Strong, Grade B |
| Regular screening every 2-4 years for ages 50-69 | Strong, Grade A |
| Personalize re-screening interval based on age, PSA, risk, life expectancy | Conditional, Grade B |
| DRE may be used alongside PSA to establish risk | Conditional, Grade C |
| Do NOT use PSA velocity as sole indication for biopsy | Strong, Grade B |
| Use validated risk calculators to inform shared decision-making | Conditional, Grade B |
| May forgo near-term biopsy if risk sufficiently low | Clinical Principle |

**Biopsy Considerations:**
- MRI fusion biopsy when available
- At least 2 cores per MRI target
- Transrectal or transperineal route acceptable

#### 6.2.2 PSA Interpretation

| PSA Level | Interpretation |
|-----------|---------------|
| < 1.0 ng/mL | Low risk; extended screening interval |
| 1.0-2.5 ng/mL | Low-intermediate risk |
| 2.5-4.0 ng/mL | Consider additional risk factors |
| 4.0-10 ng/mL | Elevated; 25% cancer risk; use additional biomarkers |
| > 10 ng/mL | High suspicion; ~50% cancer risk |

**Factors affecting PSA:** Age, prostate volume, BPH, prostatitis, ejaculation, DRE, 5-ARIs (PSA reduced ~50%).

#### 6.2.3 Additional Biomarkers
- Free PSA (% free): Higher % suggests BPH; lower % suggests cancer
- PHI (Prostate Health Index): Combines total PSA, free PSA, and p2PSA
- 4Kscore: Risk calculator combining 4 kallikrein markers with clinical info
- SelectMDx: Urine test for HOXC6 and DLX1 mRNA
- ExoDx (EPI): Urine exosome RNA test
- MRI before biopsy: Increasingly standard; PI-RADS scoring

### 6.3 Prostatitis

#### 6.3.1 NIH Classification [^437^][^438^]

| Category | Name | Presentation |
|----------|------|--------------|
| **I** | Acute bacterial prostatitis | Fever, dysuria, perineal pain, UTI symptoms; acute illness |
| **II** | Chronic bacterial prostatitis | Recurrent UTIs; same organism; relatively asymptomatic between episodes |
| **IIIA** | CP/CPPS (inflammatory) | Chronic pelvic pain > 3 months; WBCs in EPS/semen/VB3 |
| **IIIB** | CP/CPPS (non-inflammatory) | Same as IIIA; no WBCs on microscopy |
| **IV** | Asymptomatic inflammatory prostatitis | No symptoms; incidental finding (e.g., biopsy) |

#### 6.3.2 Acute Bacterial Prostatitis (Category I)
- **Pathogens:** E. coli most common; other Enterobacteriaceae
- **Treatment:** Fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin) 2-4 weeks; TMP-SMX 4-6 weeks if susceptible
- **Severe illness:** Hospitalization; IV antibiotics (ceftriaxone + fluoroquinolone)
- **Complications:** Prostatic abscess (drain if present), urinary retention (suprapubic catheter preferred)

#### 6.3.3 Chronic Prostatitis/Chronic Pelvic Pain Syndrome (CP/CPPS) - AUA 2024 Guideline [^436^][^437^]

**Key Principle:** Multimodal, multidisciplinary approach. Not an infection - avoid repeated antibiotics.

**Treatment Algorithm:**

| Category | Treatment | Evidence |
|----------|-----------|----------|
| **Lifestyle** | Dietary changes, aerobic exercise, heat therapy | Conditional, Grade C |
| **Alpha-blockers** | Tamsulosin, alfuzosin (if voiding symptoms) | Moderate, Grade B |
| **NSAIDs** | Ibuprofen, naproxen | Conditional, Grade B |
| **PDE5 inhibitors** | Daily tadalafil | Conditional, Grade B |
| **Neuropathic medications** | TCAs (amitriptyline), gabapentinoids, SSRIs | Conditional, Grade C |
| **Phytotherapeutics** | Saw palmetto, quercetin, pollen extract | Conditional, Grade B |
| **Physical therapy** | Pelvic floor myofascial release (internal/external) | Conditional, Grade C |
| **Psychological** | Cognitive behavioral therapy | Conditional, Grade C |
| **ESWT** | Low-intensity extracorporeal shockwave therapy | Moderate, Grade A |
| **TENS** | Transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation | Conditional, Grade B |
| **Acupuncture** | Traditional acupuncture | Conditional, Grade B |

**What NOT to do:** [^437^]
- Do NOT send for psychological evaluation before medical evaluation
- Do NOT give repeated antibiotics with negative cultures
- Do NOT perform prostate surgery solely for pain
- Do NOT use long-term systemic glucocorticoids

## Section 7: Erectile Dysfunction

---

### 7.1 Clinical Overview

ED is defined as the consistent or recurrent inability to achieve or maintain an erection sufficient for satisfactory sexual performance. It affects approximately 30 million men in the US. Prevalence increases with age but is not a normal part of aging.

#### 7.1.1 Etiology

| Category | Causes |
|----------|--------|
| **Vascular** | Atherosclerosis, hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, smoking, pelvic trauma, Peyronie disease |
| **Neurologic** | Spinal cord injury, MS, Parkinson disease, stroke, pelvic surgery/radiation, diabetic neuropathy |
| **Hormonal** | Hypogonadism, hyperprolactinemia, thyroid disease, Cushing syndrome |
| **Psychogenic** | Performance anxiety, depression, relationship issues, stress |
| **Medication-related** | Antihypertensives (thiazides, beta-blockers), antidepressants (SSRIs), antipsychotics, finasteride, opioids, alcohol |
| **Structural** | Peyronie disease, penile trauma, congenital abnormalities |
| **Lifestyle** | Obesity, smoking, sedentary lifestyle, substance abuse |

#### 7.1.2 Cardiovascular Risk Assessment

**Critical Concept:** ED is often an early marker of cardiovascular disease [^414^].
- ED and CVD share common risk factors (endothelial dysfunction)
- ED often precedes coronary events by 2-5 years
- Men with ED have increased risk of MI, stroke, cardiovascular death
- **Recommendation:** All men with ED should be screened for cardiovascular risk factors
- **Princeton Consensus III:** Risk stratification before resuming sexual activity:
  - Low risk: Asymptomatic, < 3 cardiac risk factors; can proceed with sexual activity and ED treatment
  - Intermediate risk: Requires further cardiac evaluation (stress test)
  - High risk: Unstable angina, recent MI, heart failure, arrhythmias; defer sexual activity until cardiac condition stabilized

### 7.2 PDE5 Inhibitors (First-Line) [^414^]

| Agent | Onset | Duration | Dosing | Food Effect |
|-------|-------|----------|--------|-------------|
| **Sildenafil** (Viagra) | 30-60 min | 4-6 hours | 25-100 mg PRN | Delayed by high-fat meal |
| **Tadalafil** (Cialis) | 30-60 min | 24-36 hours | 10-20 mg PRN or 2.5-5 mg daily | No effect |
| **Vardenafil** (Levitra) | 30-60 min | 4-6 hours | 5-20 mg PRN | Delayed by high-fat meal |
| **Avanafil** (Stendra) | 15-30 min | 4-6 hours | 50-200 mg PRN | No effect |

**Common Side Effects:** Headache (10-16%), flushing (5-13%), dyspepsia (4-10%), nasal congestion (2-9%), dizziness (2-3%), myalgia (tadalafil 3-6%), visual changes (sildenafil 2-3% - blue tinge)

**Contraindications:**
- **ABSOLUTE:** Nitrates (nitroglycerin, isosorbide) in any form - risk of profound hypotension. Must wait: sildenafil/vardenafil 24 hours; tadalafil 48 hours after last dose before nitrates.
- **Relative:** Severe hepatic impairment, severe renal impairment, hereditary retinal disorders (retinitis pigmentosa), non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy (NAION) history

**Alpha-blocker precaution:** Risk of hypotension; if used together, ensure stable alpha-blocker dose first; separate dosing times.

### 7.3 Second-Line Therapy

| Therapy | Mechanism | Notes |
|---------|-----------|-------|
| **Vacuum erection device** | Negative pressure draws blood into penis + constriction ring | Non-invasive; high satisfaction with proper instruction; useful for penile rehabilitation |
| **Intracavernosal alprostadil** | Prostaglandin E1 injected directly into corpus cavernosum | Highly effective (85-90%); side effects: pain, priapism, fibrosis |
| **Intraurethral alprostadil** (MUSE) | Pellet placed in urethra | Less effective than injection; penile/urethral pain |

### 7.4 Third-Line Therapy

**Penile Prosthesis:**
- Indicated when other treatments fail or are contraindicated
- Types: Inflatable (2-piece, 3-piece) vs. malleable (semi-rigid)
- High patient/partner satisfaction (~90%)
- Risks: Infection (1-3%), mechanical failure, erosion

## Section 8: Male Hypogonadism

---

### 8.1 Clinical Overview

Male hypogonadism is defined as the failure of the testes to produce adequate testosterone and/or sperm. Can be primary (testicular failure) or secondary (hypothalamic-pituitary dysfunction).

#### 8.1.1 Symptoms

| Category | Symptoms |
|----------|----------|
| **Sexual** | Decreased libido, erectile dysfunction, reduced morning erections |
| **Physical** | Decreased muscle mass/strength, increased body fat, reduced bone density, decreased shaving frequency, gynecomastia, reduced testicular volume |
| **Energy/Mood** | Fatigue, decreased energy, irritability, depression, poor concentration |
| **Other** | Hot flashes, decreased work performance, sleep disturbance |

#### 8.1.2 Diagnosis

**Morning Total Testosterone:**
- Measure between 7-10 AM (circadian variation)
- Repeat to confirm: Low total testosterone on two separate mornings
- Threshold for treatment: Typically < 300 ng/dL (10.4 nmol/L) plus symptoms
- **Important:** SHBG increases with age and liver disease; may need free testosterone in these situations

**Additional Testing:**
- LH and FSH: Elevated in primary; low/normal in secondary
- Prolactin: If secondary hypogonadism suspected (elevated with pituitary adenoma)
- Ferritin: Hemochromatosis screening
- Estradiol: If gynecomastia present
- DHEAS: If adrenal source suspected
- Semen analysis: If fertility desired
- Bone density: If prolonged hypogonadism
- Pituitary MRI: If secondary hypogonadism with elevated prolactin or other pituitary signs

#### 8.1.3 Causes

**Primary (Hypergonadotropic):**
- Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY)
- Testicular trauma, torsion, infection (mumps orchitis)
- Chemotherapy/radiation
- Undescended testes history
- Aging (andropause/testicular senescence)
- Hemochromatosis
- Autoimmune testicular failure

**Secondary (Hypogonadotropic):**
- Kallmann syndrome (congenital GnRH deficiency + anosmia)
- Pituitary tumors (prolactinoma)
- Hyperprolactinemia
- Hemochromatosis
- Cushing syndrome
- Opioids, anabolic steroids (exogenous testosterone), GnRH analogs
- Severe obesity, uncontrolled diabetes
- Chronic illness, malnutrition

### 8.2 Testosterone Replacement Therapy (TRT)

#### 8.2.1 Pre-Treatment Requirements
- Confirmed low morning testosterone on 2 occasions
- Baseline PSA and DRE (age-appropriate prostate cancer screening)
- Hematocrit (screen for polycythemia risk)
- Fertility status assessed

#### 8.2.2 Delivery Methods

| Method | Dosing | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|--------|--------|------------|---------------|
| **Testosterone cypionate/enanthate** | 50-200 mg IM every 1-2 weeks or 100-200 mg every 2 weeks | Inexpensive; effective | Peaks/troughs; injections; polycythemia risk |
| **Testosterone undecanoate** (Aveed) | 750 mg IM, then 750 mg at 4 weeks, then q10 weeks | Longer interval | Requires REMS; rare risk of POME/anasphylaxis |
| **Testosterone gel** (AndroGel, Testim) | 1-2 packets (25-100 mg) daily | Steady levels; easy application | Skin transfer risk; daily application; cost |
| **Testosterone patch** (Androderm) | 2-4 mg patch daily | Steady levels | Skin irritation; adherence |
| **Testosterone pellets** (Testopel) | 150-450 mg SC every 3-6 months | Long-acting; convenient | In-office procedure; incision; extrusion risk |
| **Buccal tablet** (Striant) | 30 mg BID | Steady levels | Gum irritation; BID dosing |
| **Nasal gel** (Natesto) | 11 mg per nostril TID | Preserves some fertility; no polycythemia concern | TID dosing; nasal irritation |
| **Oral testosterone undecanoate** (Jatenzo, Kyzatrex, Tlando) | 225-400 mg BID with food | Oral; no liver toxicity | Variable absorption; BP monitoring; cost |

#### 8.2.3 Monitoring

| Parameter | Baseline | Follow-up |
|-----------|----------|-----------|
| Testosterone (trough) | Yes | 2-3 months after initiation, then annually |
| Hematocrit | Yes | 3-6 months, then annually; hold if > 54% |
| PSA | Yes | 3-6 months, then annually |
| DRE | Yes | Annually |
| Bone density | If indicated | If prolonged deficiency |
| Cardiovascular risk | Yes | Ongoing |

#### 8.2.4 Risks and Side Effects
- **Polycythemia:** Hematocrit > 54% requires dose reduction or therapeutic phlebotomy
- **Infertility:** Exogenous testosterone suppresses spermatogenesis via negative feedback
- **Acne, oily skin**
- **Gynecomastia** (if estradiol conversion significant)
- **Fluid retention**
- **Sleep apnea:** Screen and monitor; may worsen
- **Prostate:** Monitoring required; no clear evidence TRT causes prostate cancer but may promote growth of existing cancer
- **Cardiovascular:** Evidence mixed; caution in high-risk patients

### 8.3 Fertility Preservation [^419^][^421^]

**Critical:** Exogenous testosterone suppresses gonadotropins and impairs spermatogenesis. Do NOT prescribe TRT to men trying to conceive.

#### 8.3.1 Alternatives for Men Desiring Fertility

| Approach | Agents | Notes |
|----------|--------|-------|
| **SERM (Clomiphene citrate)** | 25-50 mg PO daily | Off-label; stimulates hypothalamus; increases LH, FSH, testosterone; preserves spermatogenesis |
| **Enclomiphene citrate** | 12.5-25 mg daily | Purified trans-isomer; not FDA-approved but available; preserves fertility |
| **hCG monotherapy** | 1,500-3,000 IU SC/IM 2-3x/week | Directly stimulates Leydig cells; maintains intratesticular testosterone |
| **hCG + TRT** | hCG 500 IU every other day + TRT | Simultaneous treatment; maintains spermatogenesis while on TRT [^419^] |
| **Aromatase inhibitors** | Anastrozole 1 mg daily | Off-label; for men with elevated estradiol; increases testosterone via reduced negative feedback |

**AUA Guidelines (2024):** [^421^]
- Discuss long-term impact of exogenous testosterone on spermatogenesis (Strong Recommendation, Grade A)
- Do NOT prescribe exogenous testosterone to men currently trying to conceive (Strong Recommendation, Grade A)
- May use AIs, hCG, SERMs, or combination in men desiring fertility (Conditional Recommendation, Grade C)

## Section 9: Testicular Disorders

---

### 9.1 Testicular Cancer

#### 9.1.1 Epidemiology
- Most common cancer in men ages 15-35
- Excellent prognosis with modern treatment (5-year survival > 95%)
- Two main types: Germ cell tumors (95%) and sex cord-stromal tumors (5%)

#### 9.1.2 Germ Cell Tumors

| Type | Features | Markers |
|------|----------|---------|
| **Seminoma** | Age 30-40; radiosensitive; pure histology; good prognosis | LDH elevated; AFP normal |
| **Non-seminomatous** | Age 20-30; more aggressive; includes embryonal, yolk sac, choriocarcinoma, teratoma | AFP, hCG elevated |
| **Mixed** | Combination of seminoma and non-seminomatous elements | Variable |

**Risk Factors:** Undescended testis (cryptorchidism - 5-10x risk), personal history, family history, Klinefelter syndrome, HIV, CIS (intratubular germ cell neoplasia)

#### 9.1.3 Screening
- **USPSTF/AAFP:** No routine screening recommended in asymptomatic men
- However, monthly testicular self-examination may be discussed with high-risk men
- Most cancers are found by the patient or partner

#### 9.1.4 Diagnosis
- Scrotal ultrasound: Nearly 100% sensitivity; hypoechoic intratesticular mass
- Tumor markers: AFP, hCG, LDH (pre- and post-orchiectomy)
- CT chest/abdomen/pelvis: Staging
- Orchiectomy (inguinal approach): Diagnostic and therapeutic

#### 9.1.5 Staging (AJCC TNMS)

| Stage | Features |
|-------|----------|
| **I** | Tumor confined to testis/epididymis; no nodal/distant metastases; markers normalizing |
| **IA** | pT1 (limited to testis), no vascular invasion |
| **IB** | pT2-4 (beyond testis or vascular invasion) |
| **IS** | Elevated and rising tumor markers post-orchiectomy |
| **II** | Retroperitoneal lymph node involvement |
| **III** | Distant metastases or non-retroperitoneal nodal involvement |

#### 9.1.6 Treatment

| Stage/Type | Treatment | Notes |
|------------|-----------|-------|
| **Stage I seminoma** | Surveillance (preferred), single-agent carboplatin x 1-2 cycles, or para-aortic RT | Excellent prognosis; 15-20% relapse with surveillance |
| **Stage I non-seminoma** | Surveillance (preferred), RPLND, or BEP x 1 cycle | 25-30% relapse with surveillance |
| **Stage II seminoma** | Chemotherapy (BEP x 3-4 cycles) or RT | |
| **Stage II non-seminoma** | Chemotherapy (BEP x 3-4 cycles) +/- RPLND | |
| **Stage III** | BEP x 3-4 cycles (good risk) or x 4 cycles (intermediate/poor risk) | |
| **Post-chemo residual mass** | RPLND or surveillance depending on size and markers | |

**Fertility:** Offer sperm banking before treatment. RPLND can cause retrograde ejaculation.

### 9.2 Testicular Torsion (EMERGENCY) [^433^]

**Definition:** Twisting of the spermatic cord cutting off blood supply to the testis. Most common in neonates and adolescents.

**Key Features:**
- Sudden onset severe unilateral scrotal pain
- Nausea/vomiting (common)
- Absent cremasteric reflex
- Elevated, horizontal "bell-clapper" testis
- Scrotal swelling, erythema
- Doppler ultrasound: Decreased/absent blood flow

**Management:**
- **EMERGENCY:** Time-critical; permanent damage begins at 6 hours
- > 12 hours of torsion: Orchiectomy rate ~75%
- Manual detorsion may be attempted if surgical delay ("open the book" - rotate testis outward)
- **Surgical exploration is mandatory** - scrotal orchiopexy if viable; orchiectomy if nonviable
- Contralateral orchiopexy at same surgery (prevents future torsion in other testis)

### 9.3 Epididymitis and Orchitis

| Feature | Acute Epididymitis | Chronic Epididymitis |
|---------|-------------------|---------------------|
| **Etiology** | Bacterial (E. coli, STI pathogens in < 35); viral | Often non-infectious; recurrent/chronic |
| **Symptoms** | Scrotal pain/swelling, fever, dysuria | Persistent/chronic scrotal pain |
| **Diagnosis** | Urinalysis, urine culture, STI testing, ultrasound | Clinical; rule out other causes |
| **Treatment** | Antibiotics (fluoroquinolone, doxycycline if < 35), NSAIDs, scrotal elevation, rest | Symptomatic; avoid repeated antibiotics |

**Orchitis:**
- Mumps orchitis: Most common viral cause; occurs 4-6 days after parotitis; 30% bilateral; may cause infertility
- Treatment: Supportive; mumps vaccination prevention

### 9.4 Varicocele and Hydrocele

**Varicocele:** Dilation of pampiniform plexus veins (left > right due to venous anatomy)
- Grading: I (palpable with Valsalva), II (palpable without Valsalva), III (visible)
- May cause: Infertility (impaired sperm quality), testicular atrophy, pain
- Treatment: Indicated for infertility with abnormal semen analysis, pain, or testicular growth arrest in adolescents; embolization or surgical ligation

**Hydrocele:** Fluid collection between tunica vaginalis layers
- Communicating (patent processus vaginalis): Common in infants; often resolves
- Non-communicating: Acquired; often idiopathic in adults
- Treatment: Surgery (hydrocelectomy) if symptomatic or cosmetically concerning; aspiration with sclerotherapy alternative

## Section 10: Male Infertility

---

### 10.1 Clinical Overview

Infertility affects approximately 15% of couples. Male factor contributes to ~40-50% of infertility cases. Defined as inability to achieve pregnancy after 12 months of unprotected intercourse (or 6 months if female partner > 35).

### 10.2 Evaluation

#### 10.2.1 Initial Assessment
- Comprehensive history: Medical (chronic illness, infections), surgical (hernia, orchiopexy, vasectomy), developmental (cryptorchidism, delayed puberty), sexual, family, environmental/occupational exposures, medications/substances
- Physical examination: Testicular volume (Prader orchidometer), consistency, varicocele, secondary sexual characteristics

#### 10.2.2 Semen Analysis (Two samples 2-3 weeks apart)

| Parameter | WHO 6th Edition (2021) Reference |
|-----------|---------------------------------|
| Volume | >= 1.4 mL |
| Sperm concentration | >= 16 million/mL |
| Total sperm count | >= 39 million per ejaculate |
| Total motility | >= 42% |
| Progressive motility | >= 30% |
| Normal morphology | >= 4% (strict criteria) |
| Vitality | >= 54% |

#### 10.2.3 Hormonal Testing
- **Indications:** Abnormal semen analysis, signs of hypogonadism, suspected endocrine disorder
- FSH: Elevated in primary testicular failure; low in secondary (hypogonadotropic)
- LH, testosterone: To differentiate primary vs. secondary
- Prolactin: If testosterone low with normal/low LH (possible prolactinoma)
- Estradiol: If gynecomastia

#### 10.2.4 Additional Testing
- Post-ejaculate urinalysis: Retrograde ejaculation
- Antisperm antibodies: Post-vasectomy, trauma, infection
- Genetic testing:
  - Karyotype: If severe oligospermia (< 5 million/mL) or non-obstructive azoospermia
  - Y-chromosome microdeletion: If severe oligospermia or NOA
  - CFTR mutation: If CBAVD (congenital bilateral absence of vas deferens)
- Scrotal ultrasound: Varicocele, testicular pathology
- Transrectal ultrasound: Ejaculatory duct obstruction

### 10.3 Causes and Treatment

| Category | Causes | Treatment |
|----------|--------|-----------|
| **Pretesticular (endocrine)** | Hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, hyperprolactinemia, thyroid disease | Hormonal treatment (hCG, FSH, SERMs); address underlying endocrinopathy |
| **Testicular** | Varicocele, cryptorchidism, orchitis, testicular cancer, genetic (Klinefelter, Y-microdeletion), chemotherapy/radiation | Varicocelectomy (if clinically significant + abnormal SA); sperm retrieval with ART |
| **Post-testicular (obstructive)** | Vasectomy, CBAVD, ejaculatory duct obstruction, epididymal obstruction | Vasectomy reversal, surgical correction, sperm retrieval with ICSI |
| **Sexual dysfunction** | ED, retrograde ejaculation, anejaculation, premature ejaculation | Treat underlying sexual dysfunction |
| **Idiopathic** | No identifiable cause | Empiric treatment or ART |

#### 10.3.1 Treatment Options

**Medical:**
- Varicocelectomy: For palpable varicocele with abnormal SA or symptoms; improves semen parameters in 60-80%
- Hormonal therapy: For hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (hCG + FSH)
- Antioxidants: Limited evidence (CoQ10, vitamin E, zinc, folic acid, L-carnitine)
- Treat infections, endocrine disorders

**Assisted Reproductive Technology (ART):**
| Procedure | Indication |
|-----------|------------|
| **Intrauterine insemination (IUI)** | Mild male factor; processed sperm placed in uterus |
| **In-vitro fertilization (IVF)** | Moderate male factor; fertilization in laboratory |
| **Intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI)** | Severe male factor; single sperm injected into egg; allows fertilization with very low sperm counts |
| **Testicular sperm extraction (TESE)** | Azoospermia; sperm retrieved directly from testis |
| **Microsurgical epididymal sperm aspiration (MESA)** | Obstructive azoospermia; sperm from epididymis |

#### 10.3.2 Azoospermia
- **Obstructive azoospermia (OA):** Normal testicular function; blockage prevents sperm in ejaculate. FSH normal, testicular volume normal. Treatment: Surgical correction if possible; sperm retrieval + ICSI.
- **Non-obstructive azoospermia (NOA):** Testicular failure. FSH elevated (usually), small testes. Genetic testing indicated. Treatment: Micro-TESE may find focal sperm production in 40-60%; if successful, ICSI used.

## RED FLAGS: EMERGENCY INDICATORS

### Women's Health Emergencies
| Condition | Red Flags |
|-----------|-----------|
| **Ectopic pregnancy** | Amenorrhea + abdominal pain + vaginal bleeding + hemodynamic instability; shoulder pain (referred) |
| **Ovarian torsion** | Sudden severe unilateral pelvic pain with nausea/vomiting |
| **Ruptured ectopic/hemorrhagic cyst** | Acute pelvic pain with hemodynamic instability |
| **Septic abortion/PID** | Fever, severe pelvic pain, purulent discharge, tachycardia |
| **Eclampsia** | Seizure in pregnant/postpartum woman with preeclampsia |
| **HELLP syndrome** | RUQ pain, thrombocytopenia, elevated LFTs in pregnancy |
| **Postpartum hemorrhage** | Blood loss > 500 mL (vaginal) or > 1000 mL (cesarean) |
| **Testicular torsion** | (In newborns/children) Acute scrotal pain - emergency |

### Men's Health Emergencies
| Condition | Red Flags |
|-----------|-----------|
| **Testicular torsion** | Acute severe scrotal pain, absent cremasteric reflex, elevated testis |
| **Acute urinary retention** | Painful bladder distension, inability to void |
| **Fournier gangrene** | Perineal/genital pain, swelling, crepitus, fever, necrosis |
| **Priapism** | Painful erection > 4 hours |
| **Acute bacterial prostatitis with sepsis** | Fever, dysuria, perineal pain, hypotension |
| **Paraphimosis** | Retracted foreskin causing painful glans swelling (emergency) |

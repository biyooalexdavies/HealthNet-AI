## 1. Eczema (Atopic Dermatitis)

### 1.1 Clinical Overview

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a chronic, relapsing inflammatory skin disease characterized by pruritus, eczematous lesions, and skin barrier dysfunction. It affects up to 20% of children and 7-10% of adults. The pathophysiology involves a complex interplay of type 2 inflammation (IL-4, IL-13, IL-31), skin barrier defects (filaggrin mutations), cutaneous microbiome dysbiosis, and immune dysregulation. [^1^]

**Diagnostic Features:**
- Pruritus (essential feature)
- Eczema (acute, subacute, chronic)
- Typical morphology and age-specific patterns
- Chronic or relapsing history
- Personal/family history of atopy (asthma, allergic rhinitis)

### 1.2 Guidelines & Evidence

**2024 AAD Guidelines for AD Management** strongly recommend: [^2^] [^3^]
- **Biologics:** Dupilumab, tralokinumab (strong recommendation, moderate certainty)
- **Oral JAK inhibitors:** Abrocitinib, upadacitinib, baricitinib (strong recommendation, moderate certainty)
- **Phototherapy, cyclosporine, methotrexate, azathioprine, mycophenolate:** Conditionally recommended
- **Systemic corticosteroids:** NOT recommended (except acute, severe exacerbations as bridge therapy)

### 1.3 Topical Corticosteroids — Potency Classes

| Class | Potency | Examples |
|-------|---------|----------|
| I (Super-high) | Most potent | Clobetasol propionate 0.05%, betamethasone dipropionate augmented 0.05% |
| II (High) | Potent | Fluocinonide 0.05%, betamethasone dipropionate 0.05% |
| III (High-Medium) | Potent | Triamcinolone acetonide 0.5%, fluticasone propionate 0.005% |
| IV (Medium) | Mid-strength | Triamcinolone acetonide 0.1%, fluocinolone acetonide 0.025% |
| V (Low-Medium) | Lower-mid | Fluocinolone acetonide 0.01%, hydrocortisone valerate 0.2% |
| VI (Low) | Mild | Desonide 0.05%, alclometasone dipropionate 0.05% |
| VII (Lowest) | Least potent | Hydrocortisone 1%, 2.5% |

**TCS Application Guidelines:**
- Use the lowest effective potency for the shortest duration
- Apply to active lesions 1-2x daily
- Avoid prolonged use of super-high/high potency on face, groin, skin folds
- TCS phobia is common; patient education on safe use is essential
- Proactive (maintenance) therapy with low/medium potency TCS on previously affected areas reduces flares

### 1.4 Topical Calcineurin Inhibitors

**Tacrolimus ointment** (0.03% for ages 2-15; 0.1% for ages >=16) and **pimecrolimus cream 1%** are FDA-approved non-steroidal anti-inflammatory agents for AD. [^4^]

- **Mechanism:** Inhibit calcineurin, blocking T-cell activation and inflammatory cytokine release
- **Advantages:** No skin atrophy; safe for face, eyelids, neck, skin folds; suitable for long-term maintenance
- **Black box warning:** Based on theoretical concern for malignancy; not observed in human studies
- **Common AEs:** Transient burning/stinging at application site

### 1.5 Biologics for Atopic Dermatitis

#### Dupilumab (Dupixent)
- **Target:** IL-4 receptor alpha (blocks IL-4 and IL-13)
- **Dosing:** 600 mg SC loading, then 300 mg Q2W (adults/adolescents); 300 mg Q4W (children 6-11 years, weight-based)
- **FDA Approval:** >=6 months for AD
- **Efficacy:** Phase 3 trials (SOLO1, SOLO2, CHRONOS): 48% EASI-75 at week 16 vs. 13% placebo; long-term data show 90.9% EASI-75 at 4 years [^5^]
- **Key AEs:** Conjunctivitis (10-20%), injection site reactions, upper respiratory infections, paradoxical head/neck erythema
- **Monitoring:** No laboratory monitoring required

#### Tralokinumab (Adbry)
- **Target:** IL-13
- **Dosing:** 600 mg SC loading, then 300 mg Q2W (may extend to Q4W in responders)
- **FDA Approval:** >=12 years for AD
- **Efficacy:** ECZTRA trials: EASI-75 in 25-33% (monotherapy) vs. 11-13% placebo; 56% with concomitant TCS [^5^]
- **Key AEs:** Upper respiratory infections, conjunctivitis (less frequent than dupilumab), injection site reactions

#### Lebrikizumab
- **Target:** IL-13
- **Dosing:** 500 mg SC loading (two 250 mg injections), then 250 mg Q2W or Q4W maintenance
- **FDA Approval:** Under review
- **Efficacy:** Demonstrated efficacy with both Q2W and Q4W dosing after 16-week induction [^5^]

### 1.6 JAK Inhibitors for Atopic Dermatitis

All JAK inhibitors carry a **black box warning** for serious heart-related events, cancer, blood clots, and death based on tofacitinib RA studies. They are **not first-line systemic therapy.** [^6^]

| Agent | Selectivity | Dosing (Adults) | Key AEs |
|-------|-------------|-----------------|---------|
| **Upadacitinib** (Rinvoq) | JAK1 selective | 15 mg daily (may increase to 30 mg) | Acne, URI, herpes zoster, CPK elevation |
| **Abrocitinib** (Cibinqo) | JAK1 selective | 100-200 mg daily | Nausea, headache, acne, herpes infections |
| **Baricitinib** (Olumiant) | JAK1/JAK2 | 2-4 mg daily | Increased LDL, URI, headache |
| **Ruxolitinib** (Opzelura) | Topical JAK1/JAK2 | 1.5% cream BID | Application site reactions, headache |

**Monitoring Requirements:** [^6^]
- Baseline: CBC with differential, liver enzymes, hepatitis B/C screening, TB screening, pregnancy test, lipid panel
- Follow-up: Lipids after initiation; ongoing CBC, liver function monitoring
- Vaccination: Shingles vaccine (recommended before starting, especially older patients); all live vaccines should be given prior to initiation

**Efficacy Comparison:**
- Upadacitinib 15 mg and abrocitinib 200 mg showed numerically higher response rates than dupilumab in head-to-head trials (Heads Up study)
- Abrocitinib 200 mg: faster itch relief (PP-NRS4 at week 2: 48.2% vs. 25.5%) and higher EASI-90 at week 4 (28.5% vs. 14.6%) compared to dupilumab [^5^]
- Baricitinib showed slightly less EASI reduction compared with dupilumab at 16 weeks

### 1.7 General Management Principles

**Skin Barrier Maintenance:**
- Daily liberal use of emollients/moisturizers (applied within 3 minutes of bathing)
- Lukewarm baths/showers; avoid hot water
- Fragrance-free, hypoallergenic products
- Wet wrap therapy for acute flares

**Trigger Avoidance:**
- Irritants: harsh soaps, detergents, wool, fragrances
- Allergens: consider patch testing for persistent/recalcitrant AD
- Environmental: temperature extremes, low humidity, stress
- Infections: S. aureus colonization common; bleach baths may help

**Stepwise Treatment Approach:**
1. **Mild AD:** Topical emollients + low-medium potency TCS or TCI as needed
2. **Moderate AD:** Proactive TCS/TCI maintenance + phototherapy; consider crisaborole or topical ruxolitinib
3. **Moderate-Severe AD:** Biologics (dupilumab, tralokinumab) or JAK inhibitors
4. **Severe/Refractory AD:** Oral JAK inhibitors, cyclosporine (short-term), methotrexate, azathioprine

### 1.8 Special Populations

**Pediatrics:**
- Mild-moderate: Low-mid potency TCS; tacrolimus 0.03% approved for >=2 years
- Dupilumab approved for >=6 months; upadacitinib for >=12 years (>=40 kg)
- JAK inhibitors: generally avoid in very young children when possible

**Pregnancy:**
- Topical TCS (low-mid potency) generally safe
- Tacrolimus/pimecrolimus: generally considered safe (Category C)
- Systemic therapies: avoid methotrexate, mycophenolate; hydroxychloroquine safe; limited data on biologics

## 2. Psoriasis

### 2.1 Clinical Overview

Psoriasis is a chronic, immune-mediated inflammatory skin disease affecting 2-3% of the global population. It is driven by T-cell-mediated inflammation with key cytokines including TNF-alpha, IL-17, IL-23, and IL-12. Psoriasis has significant comorbidity burden including psoriatic arthritis (PsA), metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular disease, depression, and inflammatory bowel disease. [^7^]

**Clinical Subtypes:**
| Subtype | Description | Key Features |
|---------|-------------|--------------|
| **Plaque psoriasis** | 80-90% of cases | Well-demarcated erythematous plaques with silvery scale; extensor surfaces, scalp, trunk |
| **Guttate psoriasis** | Often post-streptococcal | Small, drop-like papules; trunk and proximal extremities |
| **Pustular psoriasis** | Generalized (GPP) or localized | Sterile pustules; GPP is dermatologic emergency |
| **Erythrodermic psoriasis** | Generalized erythema | >90% BSA involvement; thermoregulatory compromise; emergency |
| **Inverse psoriasis** | Flexural involvement | Smooth, shiny, well-demarcated erythema; minimal scale |

**Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI):** Standard outcome measure; PASI 75 (75% improvement) and PASI 90 are common treatment targets.

### 2.2 Guidelines & Evidence

Current AAD/NPF guidelines recommend a treat-to-target approach with the goal of achieving PASI <=1 or BSA <=1%. Treatment selection is based on disease severity (mild: BSA <3%; moderate: 3-10%; severe: >10%), patient preferences, comorbidities, and impact on quality of life. [^8^]

### 2.3 Topical Therapy

**First-line for mild disease:**

| Agent | Mechanism | Notes |
|-------|-----------|-------|
| **Vitamin D analogs** (calcipotriene, calcitriol) | Inhibit keratinocyte proliferation, modulate immune response | First-line; combine with TCS for enhanced efficacy; calcipotriene 0.005% + betamethasone dipropionate 0.064% (Taclonex) is highly effective |
| **Topical corticosteroids** | Anti-inflammatory | By potency; limited by skin atrophy risk on long-term use |
| **Tazarotene** (Tazorac) | Topical retinoid | Modulates keratinocyte proliferation; may cause irritation |
| **Roflumilast cream 0.3%** (Zoryve) | PDE4 inhibitor | FDA-approved for plaque psoriasis; steroid-sparing |
| **Tapinarof cream 1%** (Vtama) | Aryl hydrocarbon receptor agonist | Steroid-free; once daily; remittive potential |

### 2.4 Phototherapy

- **Narrowband UVB (311-313 nm):** First-line phototherapy; 2-3x weekly; 20-30 treatments for response
- **PUVA (psoralen + UVA):** Highly effective; reserved for refractory cases; long-term skin cancer risk
- **Excimer laser (308 nm):** Targeted therapy for localized plaques
- **Home NB-UVB units:** Available for appropriate patients

### 2.5 Systemic Non-Biologic Therapy

| Agent | Dosing | Monitoring | Key Considerations |
|-------|--------|------------|-------------------|
| **Methotrexate** | 10-25 mg weekly (oral, SC, IM) | CBC, LFTs, creatinine at baseline, every 2-4 weeks initially, then q8-12 weeks | First-line systemic; folic acid supplementation; teratogenic; avoid in pregnancy, significant liver disease |
| **Cyclosporine** | 3-5 mg/kg/day divided BID | BP, creatinine, electrolytes, LFTs, lipids q2 weeks initially | Rapid onset; renal toxicity; hypertension; limit to <1 year continuous use |
| **Acitretin** | 25-50 mg daily | LFTs, lipids q2-4 weeks; pregnancy test | Teratogenic (must avoid pregnancy for 3 years after stopping); mucocutaneous side effects; hyperlipidemia |
| **Apremilast** (Otezla) | 10-30 mg BID (titration required) | Weight monitoring; LFTs at baseline | PDE4 inhibitor; diarrhea, nausea, headache common; weight loss; depression monitoring |

### 2.6 Biologic Therapy

Biologics are categorized by molecular target. All require TB screening before initiation and ongoing infection surveillance. [^9^]

#### TNF-alpha Inhibitors
| Agent | Dosing | Administration |
|-------|--------|----------------|
| **Adalimumab** (Humira) | 80 mg loading, then 40 mg Q2W | SC |
| **Etanercept** (Enbrel) | 50 mg QW or 25 mg BIW | SC |
| **Infliximab** (Remicade) | 5 mg/kg at weeks 0, 2, 6, then Q8W | IV |
| **Certolizumab pegol** (Cimzia) | 400 mg loading (weeks 0, 2, 4), then 200 mg Q2W or 400 mg Q4W | SC |

- **Efficacy:** Generally achieve PASI 75 in 60-80% of patients
- **Key AEs:** Increased infection risk (including TB reactivation), injection/infusion reactions, demyelinating disease, heart failure exacerbation, lymphoma risk (theoretical)

#### IL-17 Inhibitors
| Agent | Dosing | Administration |
|-------|--------|----------------|
| **Secukinumab** (Cosentyx) | 300 mg at weeks 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, then Q4W | SC |
| **Ixekizumab** (Taltz) | 160 mg loading, then 80 mg Q2W (may extend to Q4W) | SC |
| **Brodalumab** (Siliq) | 210 mg at weeks 0, 1, 2, then Q2W | SC |

- **Efficacy:** Generally achieve PASI 90 in 70-80% and PASI 100 in 40-60%
- **Key AEs:** IBD exacerbation (contraindicated in active Crohn's/UC), candida infections, neutropenia
- **IL-17 inhibitors have the highest rates of skin clearance among biologics** [^9^]

#### IL-23 Inhibitors
| Agent | Dosing | Administration |
|-------|--------|----------------|
| **Guselkumab** (Tremfya) | 100 mg at weeks 0, 4, then Q8W | SC |
| **Risankizumab** (Skyrizi) | 150 mg at weeks 0, 4, then Q12W | SC |
| **Tildrakizumab** (Ilumya) | 100 mg at weeks 0, 4, then Q12W | SC |
| **Tremfya IV** | 200 mg at weeks 0, 4, then Q8W | IV |

- **Efficacy:** PASI 90 in 70-85%; PASI 100 in 40-60%; excellent long-term data
- **Key AEs:** Low immunogenicity; well tolerated; nasopharyngitis, upper respiratory infections; minimal safety concerns
- **IL-23 inhibitors are increasingly preferred as first-line biologics** due to excellent efficacy and favorable safety profile [^9^]

#### IL-12/23 Inhibitor
| Agent | Dosing | Administration |
|-------|--------|----------------|
| **Ustekinumab** (Stelara) | 45 mg (<=100 kg) or 90 mg (>100 kg) at weeks 0, 4, then Q12W | SC |

- **Efficacy:** PASI 75 in 67-76%; PASI 90 in ~50%
- **Key AEs:** Generally well tolerated; fewer infections than TNF inhibitors

### 2.7 Treatment Selection Algorithm

**Mild psoriasis (BSA <3%):**
- Topical therapy (vitamin D analogs, TCS, roflumilast, tapinarof)
- Phototherapy for refractory cases

**Moderate psoriasis (BSA 3-10%):**
- Phototherapy (NB-UVB)
- Systemic therapy if phototherapy inadequate or impractical
- Biologics if quality of life significantly impacted

**Severe psoriasis (BSA >10%):**
- Systemic therapy (methotrexate, acitretin, apremilast) or biologics
- **Preferred first-line biologics:** IL-23 inhibitors or IL-17 inhibitors (highest efficacy)
- TNF inhibitors for patients with concomitant PsA, IBD

**Selection based on comorbidities:**
- **PsA:** TNF inhibitors, IL-17 inhibitors, IL-23 inhibitors, ustekinumab
- **IBD (Crohn's/UC):** TNF inhibitors, ustekinumab (avoid IL-17 inhibitors)
- **Cardiovascular disease:** All biologics appear relatively safe; some data suggest possible cardioprotective effects [^10^]
- **Demylenating disease:** Avoid TNF inhibitors
- **Pregnancy:** Limited data; TNF inhibitors (especially certolizumab) may have lowest placental transfer

### 2.8 Comorbidities

**Psoriatic Arthritis (PsA):**
- Affects 20-30% of psoriasis patients
- Screen all psoriasis patients annually for joint symptoms (PASE questionnaire)
- Treatment with biologics that address both skin and joints (TNF inhibitors, IL-17 inhibitors, IL-23 inhibitors)

**Metabolic Syndrome and CVD:**
- Increased risk of MI, stroke, and cardiovascular mortality
- Screen for metabolic syndrome components; manage cardiovascular risk factors
- Weight loss recommendations for overweight/obese patients

**Mental Health:**
- Depression and anxiety prevalence 2x higher
- Significant impact on quality of life; screen routinely

**Other:**
- IBD (Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis) risk increased
- Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease
- Type 2 diabetes mellitus
- Lymphoma (small increased risk)

## 3. Acne Vulgaris

### 3.1 Clinical Overview

Acne vulgaris is the most common skin condition in the United States, affecting nearly 50 million Americans annually. It affects 85% of adolescents and can persist or begin in adulthood, particularly in women. Pathophysiology involves four key factors: follicular hyperkeratinization, increased sebum production, *Cutibacterium acnes* (formerly *P. acnes*) colonization, and inflammation. [^11^] [^12^]

**Grading:**
- **Mild:** Comedones (open/closed) with few inflammatory papules/pustules
- **Moderate:** Multiple inflammatory papules/pustules with comedones
- **Severe (Nodulocystic):** Nodules, cysts, scarring risk, significant psychosocial impact

**AAD 2024 Updated Guidelines** provide 18 evidence-based recommendations and 5 good practice statements. [^11^] [^13^]

### 3.2 Topical Therapy

**Strongly Recommended (AAD 2024):**

| Agent | Mechanism | Key Points |
|-------|-----------|------------|
| **Benzoyl peroxide (BP)** | Antimicrobial, comedolytic | OTC; concentration 2.5-10%; antimicrobial resistance prevented; fabric bleaching |
| **Topical retinoids** (adapalene, tretinoin, tazarotene, trifarotene) | Normalize follicular keratinization, anti-inflammatory | Cornerstone of acne treatment; first-line for comedonal acne; maintenance therapy essential |
| **Topical antibiotics** (erythromycin, clindamycin, minocycline, dapsone) | Antibacterial, anti-inflammatory | **Never as monotherapy** — must combine with BP to prevent resistance |
| **Fixed-dose combinations** (BP + retinoid, BP + antibiotic, retinoid + antibiotic) | Multimodal | Recommended for improved adherence and efficacy |

**Conditionally Recommended:**
- **Clascoterone 1% cream** (Winlevi): Androgen receptor inhibitor; first new mechanism for acne in decades
- **Salicylic acid:** Keratolytic; OTC
- **Azelaic acid 15-20%:** Antibacterial, anti-inflammatory; safe in pregnancy; beneficial for post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation

### 3.3 Systemic Therapy

**Systemic Antibiotics:**
- **Doxycycline:** Strongly recommended; 50-100 mg daily-BID
- **Minocycline:** Conditionally recommended; 50-100 mg BID
- **Sarecycline:** Conditionally recommended; narrow-spectrum tetracycline; weight-based dosing
- **Limit duration to shortest possible, typically no more than 3-4 months**
- **Always combine with BP and topical retinoid**
- **Never use as monotherapy** to prevent resistance

**Hormonal Therapy:**
- **Combined oral contraceptives (COCs):** Conditionally recommended; beneficial for women with hormonal acne (jawline, premenstrual flares)
- **Spironolactone:** Conditionally recommended; 50-200 mg daily; anti-androgen; potassium monitoring not needed in healthy patients but consider for those with hyperkalemia risk

### 3.4 Isotretinoin

**Indications (strongly recommended by AAD 2024):**
- Severe acne (nodulocystic)
- Acne causing psychosocial burden or scarring
- Acne failing standard treatment with oral or topical therapy

**Dosing:**
- Traditional: 0.5-1 mg/kg/day for 15-20 weeks; cumulative dose 120-150 mg/kg
- Low-dose and intermittent regimens: Less effective; daily dosing preferred

**Monitoring:**
- Baseline: LFTs, fasting lipid panel, pregnancy test (all patients of childbearing potential)
- Follow-up: LFTs and lipids at follow-up visits; CBC not needed in healthy patients
- **iPLEDGE Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy (REMS):** Mandatory for all prescribers and patients; monthly pregnancy tests for females of childbearing potential; two forms of contraception required
- **Psychiatric monitoring:** Screen for depression, anxiety, suicidal ideation at each visit (population-based studies have not identified increased risk, but monitoring is prudent)

**Key Adverse Effects:**
- Mucocutaneous: Dry lips, dry skin, epistaxis (near universal)
- Ocular: Dry eyes, potential night vision changes
- Musculoskeletal: Myalgias, arthralgias; rare skeletal effects in children
- GI: Elevated LFTs, hypertriglyceridemia; IBD association controversial
- Teratogenicity: **Absolute contraindication in pregnancy**
- **Full-face dermabrasion and ablative laser treatments not recommended within 6 months of isotretinoin use** [^13^]

### 3.5 Acne Scars

**Prevention:** Early effective treatment of inflammatory acne is the best prevention.

**Treatment options for existing scars:**
- **Post-inflammatory erythema/hyperpigmentation:** Time, topical retinoids, azelaic acid, photoprotection
- **Atrophic scars:**
  - Ice pick: Punch excision, TCA CROSS
  - Boxcar: Microneedling, fractional laser, subcision, fillers
  - Rolling: Subcision, microneedling, fractional laser
- **Hypertrophic/keloid scars:** Intralesional corticosteroids, silicone sheets, laser
- **Microneedling and laser:** Evidence supports benefit; must be >6 months after isotretinoin completion

### 3.6 Special Populations

**Pregnancy:**
- Safe: Azelaic acid, BP, erythromycin, clindamycin (topical)
- Contraindicated: Tazarotene, oral tetracyclines, isotretinoin
- Use topical therapies other than retinoids as first-line

**Pediatrics (ages 9-12):**
- Topical retinoids + BP are first-line
- Systemic antibiotics: Limited use; sarecycline approved for >=9 years
- Isotretinoin for severe/recalcitrant cases

## 4. Skin Infections

### 4.1 Bacterial Skin Infections

#### Impetigo
- **Pathogen:** *S. aureus* (most common, including MRSA), Group A Streptococcus
- **Types:** Non-bullous (honey-colored crusts), bullous (flaccid bullae)
- **Treatment (IDSA Guidelines):** [^14^]
  - **Localized:** Mupirocin 2% or retapamulin 1% ointment BID x 5 days
  - **Extensive/systemic:** Oral cephalexin, dicloxacillin x 7 days
  - **MRSA suspected:** Doxycycline, clindamycin, or TMP-SMX x 7 days
  - **Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis outbreaks:** Systemic antibiotics to eliminate nephritogenic strains

#### Folliculitis
- **Pathogen:** *S. aureus* (most common), Pseudomonas (hot tub), Malassezia (fungal), Candida
- **Treatment:**
  - Bacterial: Topical mupirocin or clindamycin; oral antibiotics for extensive cases
  - Pseudomonas: Usually self-limited; ciprofloxacin for severe cases
  - Malassezia: Topical ketoconazole or selenium sulfide
  - Prevention: Warm compresses, proper hygiene, avoid occlusive clothing

#### Cellulitis/Erysipelas
- **Pathogen:** Group A Streptococcus, *S. aureus*
- **Treatment:**
  - Mild: Oral cephalexin, dicloxacillin; amoxicillin-clavulanate
  - Moderate-severe (inpatient): IV cefazolin, ceftriaxone; add vancomycin for MRSA risk
  - Recurrent cellulitis: Treat predisposing factors (tinea pedis, lymphedema); prophylactic penicillin VK or erythromycin
  - **Always examine for tinea pedis as portal of entry**

#### MRSA Skin and Soft Tissue Infections
- **Community-acquired MRSA:** Often presents as purulent skin/soft tissue infections, abscesses
- **Treatment:**
  - **Abscess:** Incision and drainage (primary treatment)
  - **Oral agents:** TMP-SMX, doxycycline, clindamycin (check local resistance patterns)
  - **Severe infections:** Vancomycin, daptomycin, linezolid (IV)
  - **Decolonization (recurrent infections):** Mupirocin to nares, chlorhexidine washes; consider dilute bleach baths

### 4.2 Fungal Infections

#### Tinea Corporis (Ringworm)
- **Pathogen:** *Trichophyton rubrum* (most common)
- **Clinical:** Annular, scaly, pruritic plaques with central clearing and raised borders
- **Treatment:** Topical antifungals (terbinafine 1%, clotrimazole 1%, miconazole 2%) BID x 2-4 weeks
- Extensive: Oral terbinafine or itraconazole

#### Tinea Capitis
- **Pathogen:** *Trichophyton tonsurans* (most common in US), *Microsporum* species
- **Clinical:** Scaly patches with alopecia, broken hairs, kerion (boggy, purulent swelling)
- **Must treat with systemic antifungals:**
  - **Griseofulvin** (microsize: 20-25 mg/kg/day; ultramicrosize: 10-15 mg/kg/day) x 6-8 weeks — still preferred in children
  - **Terbinafine** (weight-based dosing: <25 kg: 125 mg/day; 25-35 kg: 187.5 mg/day; >35 kg: 250 mg/day) x 6 weeks
  - **Itraconazole:** 5 mg/kg/day x 2-4 weeks
- **Adjunct:** Selenium sulfide or ketoconazole shampoo 2-3x weekly to reduce shedding/spread
- **Kerion:** May add oral corticosteroids short-term; culture before treatment

#### Tinea Pedis (Athlete's Foot)
- **Clinical:** Interdigital (maceration, fissuring), moccasin-type (dry scaling), vesicular
- **Treatment:** Topical antifungals (terbinafine 1%, butenafine 1%) BID x 1-4 weeks; oral terbinafine for moccasin-type or refractory
- **Preventive role:** Treating tinea pedis reduces risk of recurrent cellulitis

#### Onychomycosis (Tinea Unguium)
- **Pathogen:** *T. rubrum* (most common)
- **Treatment:**
  - **Oral terbinafine:** 250 mg daily x 12 weeks (toenails) or 6 weeks (fingernails) — highest cure rates (70-80%)
  - **Oral itraconazole:** Pulse dosing (200 mg BID x 1 week/month) x 3-4 months (toenails)
  - **Topical:** Efinaconazole 10%, tavaborole 5% (lower cure rates; suitable for mild disease or contraindications to oral therapy)
  - **Check baseline LFTs; monitor for drug interactions**

#### Candidiasis
- **Cutaneous candidiasis:** Intertriginous areas; beefy red erythema with satellite pustules
  - Treatment: Topical nystatin, clotrimazole, miconazole; keep area dry
- **Oral candidiasis (thrush):** White plaques that scrape off leaving erythematous base
  - Treatment: Nystatin suspension, clotrimazole troches, oral fluconazole
- **Chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis:** Consider immunodeficiency (especially in children)

### 4.3 Viral Exanthems

#### Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV)
- **HSV-1:** Oral herpes (cold sores); may cause facial/ocular infections
- **HSV-2:** Genital herpes
- **Treatment:**
  - First episode: Acyclovir 400 mg TID x 7-10 days, valacyclovir 1 g BID x 7-10 days, famciclovir 250 mg TID x 7-10 days
  - Recurrent (episodic): Valacyclovir 500 mg BID x 3 days or 2 g BID x 1 day
  - Suppressive: Valacyclovir 500-1000 mg daily; acyclovir 400 mg BID
- **Eczema herpeticum (Kaposi varicelliform eruption):** HSV superinfection in atopic dermatitis — emergency; treat with IV acyclovir

#### Varicella Zoster Virus (VZV)
- **Varicella (chickenpox):** Primary infection; self-limited in children
- **Herpes Zoster (shingles):** Reactivation; dermatomal distribution
  - **Treatment (within 72 hours of rash onset):** Valacyclovir 1 g TID x 7 days, famciclovir 500 mg TID x 7 days, acyclovir 800 mg 5x daily x 7-10 days
  - **Ophthalmic involvement (V1):** Urgent ophthalmology referral
  - **Ramsay Hunt syndrome (facial palsy + auricular vesicles):** Urgent ENT/neurology
  - **Post-herpetic neuralgia prevention:** Early antiviral treatment; vaccination (Shingrix) recommended for adults >=50 years

#### Molluscum Contagiosum
- **Pathogen:** Molluscum contagiosum virus (poxvirus)
- **Clinical:** Umbilicated, flesh-colored, pearly papules
- **Treatment:** Usually self-limited (6-12 months); treat for cosmetic reasons or to prevent spread:
  - Curettage, cryotherapy, cantharidin
  - Topical imiquimod (off-label), tretinoin
  - In immunocompromised: may be extensive and persistent

### 4.4 Infestations

#### Scabies
- **Pathogen:** *Sarcoptes scabiei var. hominis*
- **Clinical:** Intense nocturnal pruritus; burrows in web spaces, wrists, axillae, genitalia; papules and excoriations [^15^] [^16^]
- **Classic scabies treatment:**
  - **First-line:** Permethrin 5% cream applied neck-down, left 8-14 hours, repeat in 7 days
  - **Alternative:** Oral ivermectin 200 mcg/kg, repeat in 7-14 days
  - **All close contacts must be treated simultaneously**
  - **Bedding/clothing:** Wash/dry at >=50 degrees C for 10 minutes or seal in bag for >=72 hours
- **Crusted (Norwegian) scabies:** Highly contagious; occurs in immunocompromised
  - **Combination therapy:** Topical permethrin daily + oral ivermectin on days 1, 2, 8, 9, 15 (and possibly 22, 29)

#### Pediculosis (Lice)
- **Pediculus humanus capitis (head lice):**
  - First-line: Permethrin 1% lotion or pyrethrins with piperonyl butoxide; repeat in 7-10 days
  - Alternative: Oral ivermectin 200 mcg/kg (repeat in 7-10 days); topical ivermectin 0.5% lotion
  - Wet combing with fine-tooth comb (nit comb)
  - **Resistance to permethrin/pyrethrins is widespread** [^17^]
- **Phthirus pubis (pubic lice):**
  - Permethrin 1% cream rinse x 10 minutes; pyrethrin with piperonyl butoxide; malathion 0.5% lotion x 8-12 hours
  - Screen for other STIs (HIV, syphilis, hepatitis)
- **Pediculus humanus corporis (body lice):**
  - Improved hygiene; wash clothing/bedding in hot water
  - Topical permethrin if needed
  - **Can transmit epidemic typhus, trench fever, relapsing fever**

## 5. Skin Cancer

### 5.1 Melanoma

#### Clinical Overview
Melanoma is the most serious form of skin cancer, with increasing incidence globally. Early detection is critical — 5-year survival for localized melanoma is >99% but drops to 27% for distant metastatic disease. [^18^]

#### ABCDE Criteria for Detection
| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **A**symmetry | One half doesn't match the other |
| **B**order irregularity | Ragged, notched, or blurred edges |
| **C**olor variation | Multiple colors or uneven distribution |
| **D**iameter | >6 mm (pencil eraser) — though melanomas can be smaller |
| **E**volving | Changing in size, shape, color, elevation; new symptoms (itching, bleeding) |

**Additional warning signs:** "Ugly duckling" sign (lesion different from patient's other moles), elevation, firmness, growing (EFG for nodular melanoma)

#### Diagnosis
- **Full-thickness biopsy** with 1-3 mm clinical margins preferred (shave biopsy acceptable if full-thickness)
- **Dermoscopy** improves diagnostic accuracy
- **Sentinel lymph node biopsy** for melanomas >=0.8 mm Breslow thickness

#### Staging (AJCC 8th Edition, 2018) [^18^]
**T Classification (based on Breslow thickness and ulceration):**
| Stage | Breslow Thickness | Ulceration Status |
|-------|-------------------|-------------------|
| T1 | <=0.8 mm | Without ulceration (T1a) |
| T1 | <=0.8 mm | With ulceration (T1b) |
| T2 | 0.8-1.0 mm | Without (T2a) / With (T2b) ulceration |
| T3 | 1.01-2.0 mm | Without (T3a) / With (T3b) ulceration |
| T4 | 2.01-4.0 mm | Without (T4a) / With (T4b) ulceration |
| T5 | >4.0 mm | Without (T5a) / With (T5b) ulceration |

**Prognostic Stage Groups:**
| Stage | Features | 5-Year Survival |
|-------|----------|-----------------|
| 0 | Melanoma in situ | ~100% |
| IA | T1a N0 M0 | ~97% |
| IB-IIA | T1b-T2b N0 M0 | 92-95% |
| IIB-IIC | T3a-T4b N0 M0 | 77-87% |
| III | Node-positive (N1-N3) | 40-78% |
| IV | Distant metastasis (M1) | 15-27% |

#### Treatment
- **Stage 0 (in situ):** Wide local excision with 0.5-1 cm margins
- **Stage I-II:** Wide local excision (1-2 cm margins depending on thickness); sentinel lymph node biopsy for >=T1b/T2; adjuvant therapy (pembrolizumab, nivolumab) for high-risk Stage IIB-IIC
- **Stage III:** Lymphadenectomy if node-positive; adjuvant immunotherapy (pembrolizumab, nivolumab, dabrafenib + trametinib for BRAF V600E); radiation for high-risk nodal disease
- **Stage IV:** Systemic therapy — checkpoint inhibitors (pembrolizumab, nivolumab, ipilimumab+nivolumab) first-line; targeted therapy (BRAF + MEK inhibitors) for BRAF-mutant; clinical trials

**Adjuvant Immunotherapy:** Pembrolizumab and nivolumab significantly improve recurrence-free survival in Stage IIB-III melanoma.

### 5.2 Basal Cell Carcinoma (BCC)

- **Most common skin cancer** — rarely metastasizes but locally invasive
- **Risk factors:** UV exposure, fair skin, ionizing radiation, immunosuppression, genetic syndromes (Gorlin syndrome)
- **Subtypes:** Nodular (most common), superficial, morpheaform/sclerosing (more aggressive), micronodular, infiltrative
- **Treatment (NCCN Guidelines 2024):** [^19^]
  - **Low-risk:** Curettage and electrodesiccation (non-hair-bearing areas), standard excision (4 mm margins), Mohs surgery, topical therapies (imiquimod, 5-FU, PDT) for superficial BCC
  - **High-risk:** Mohs micrographic surgery (preferred for H-zone, recurrent, aggressive histology, >1 cm); standard excision with wider margins; radiation for non-surgical candidates
  - **Locally advanced/metastatic:** Hedgehog pathway inhibitors (vismodegib, sonidegib); cemiplimab (PD-1 inhibitor)
- **Follow-up:** Skin exam every 6-12 months for life; sun protection counseling

### 5.3 Squamous Cell Carcinoma (SCC)

- **Second most common skin cancer** — metastatic potential higher than BCC
- **Risk factors:** UV exposure, fair skin, HPV (anogenital), immunosuppression (organ transplant), chronic wounds, arsenic exposure
- **Subtypes:** Well-differentiated, moderately differentiated, poorly differentiated, desmoplastic (high risk), adenosquamous, spindle cell
- **Treatment:**
  - **Low-risk:** Standard excision (4-6 mm margins), ED&C, Mohs
  - **High-risk:** Mohs micrographic surgery (preferred); adjuvant radiation for perineural invasion, large tumors, incomplete excision
  - **Metastatic:** Cemiplimab; multidisciplinary care
- **Follow-up:** Every 3-12 months initially; every 6-12 months long-term

### 5.4 Actinic Keratosis (AK)

- **Premalignant lesions** (may progress to SCC — 0.025-16% per lesion per year)
- **Clinical:** Rough, scaly patches on sun-exposed skin (face, scalp, dorsal hands, forearms)
- **Field cancerization concept:** Multiple AKs suggest photodamaged skin at risk for SCC
- **Treatment options:**
  - **Lesion-directed:** Cryotherapy, curettage, shave excision
  - **Field-directed:** 5-fluorouracil cream, imiquimod cream, diclofenac gel, photodynamic therapy, ingenol mebutate

### 5.5 Skin Cancer Prevention

**Photoprotection (AAD Guidelines):** [^20^]
- **Broad-spectrum sunscreen SPF 30+** applied liberally (2 mg/cm2 = ~1 oz for full body) and reapplied every 2 hours and after swimming/sweating
- **Physical blockers:** Zinc oxide, titanium dioxide (especially for sensitive skin, children)
- **Chemical blockers:** Oxybenzone, avobenzone, octinoxate, octisalate, homosalate
- **Protective clothing:** UPF-rated clothing, wide-brimmed hats, UV-blocking sunglasses
- **Seek shade:** Especially 10 AM - 4 PM
- **Avoid tanning beds:** Increase melanoma risk by 75% when used before age 30
- **Regular self-examination:** Monthly; professional skin examination annually (more frequently for high-risk patients)

## 6. Other Common Dermatoses

### 6.1 Contact Dermatitis

**Irritant Contact Dermatitis (ICD):** Direct chemical/physical damage to skin barrier; most common type; non-immunologic; anyone can develop with sufficient exposure

**Allergic Contact Dermatitis (ACD):** Type IV delayed hypersensitivity (T-cell mediated); requires prior sensitization

**Common Allergens:**
- Nickel (jewelry, snaps, zippers) — most common contact allergen
- Fragrances, preservatives (formaldehyde, MI/MCI)
- Topical antibiotics (neomycin, bacitracin)
- Rubber accelerators (thiurams, carbamates)
- Poison ivy/oak (urushiol)
- Hair dyes (PPD)

**Diagnosis:**
- **Clinical:** Pruritic, eczematous eruption at site of contact; may have sharp demarcation
- **Patch testing:** Gold standard for ACD; performed by experienced clinician; apply panels of allergens, read at 48 and 72-96 hours [^21^]

**Treatment:**
- **First-line:** Avoidance of trigger; topical corticosteroids (medium-high potency for acute, low for maintenance)
- **Severe:** Oral corticosteroids (prednisone taper over 2-3 weeks — shorter tapers cause rebound)
- **Chronic/refractory:** Phototherapy, systemic immunosuppressants
- **Barrier repair:** Emollients, protective creams

### 6.2 Urticaria (Hives)

**Acute Urticaria:** <6 weeks duration; often triggered by infection, medications (NSAIDs, antibiotics), foods, insect stings

**Chronic Urticaria (CU):** >=6 weeks duration; includes chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU) and chronic inducible urticaria (CIndU — physical urticarias)

**Pathophysiology:** Mast cell degranulation -> histamine release -> vascular permeability, edema, pruritus

**Diagnosis:** Clinical; limited testing (CBC, ESR/CRP, thyroid function tests if chronic); physical challenge tests for CIndU

**Treatment Guidelines (EAACI/GA2LEN/EDF/WAO 2017 Update):** [^22^] [^23^]
1. **First-line:** Second-generation H1-antihistamines (cetirizine, loratadine, fexofenadine, levocetirizine, desloratadine) at standard doses daily
2. **Step 2:** Up-titrate H1-antihistamine up to 4x standard dose (strongest evidence for levocetirizine, desloratadine, bilastine)
3. **Step 3:** Add omalizumab (Xolair) 300 mg SC every 4 weeks — **FDA-approved for CSU >=12 years**
4. **Step 4:** Add cyclosporine 3-5 mg/kg/day (reserved for refractory cases due to toxicity)

**Systemic corticosteroids:** Short courses only (up to 10 days for acute exacerbations); NOT for long-term use

**Special considerations:**
- **Angioedema:** Occurs in ~40% of CSU patients; can affect lips, periorbital area, genitals; omalizumab effective for prevention
- **Anaphylaxis risk:** If associated with systemic symptoms (hypotension, bronchospasm, laryngeal edema) — requires epinephrine auto-injector
- **Thyroid autoimmunity association:** Check TSH in chronic urticaria

### 6.3 Rosacea

- **Chronic inflammatory condition** affecting central face
- **Subtypes:** Erythematotelangiectatic (flushing, persistent erythema), papulopustular, phymatous (rhinophyma), ocular
- **Triggers:** Sun exposure, heat, alcohol, spicy foods, hot beverages, emotional stress, certain skincare products
- **Treatment:**
  - **General:** Sun protection, gentle skincare, trigger avoidance
  - **Erythema/telangiectasia:** Topical brimonidine 0.33% gel or oxymetazoline 1% cream (vasoconstrictors); laser (PDL, Nd:YAG) for telangiectasia
  - **Papulopustular:** Topical metronidazole, azelaic acid 15%, ivermectin 1% cream; oral doxycycline 40 mg (sub-antimicrobial dose) or minocycline
  - **Phymatous:** Oral isotretinoin; surgical/laser debulking
  - **Ocular:** Lid hygiene, artificial tears, oral doxycycline; ophthalmology referral if severe
- **Avoid topical corticosteroids** on face (may cause steroid-induced rosacea/perioral dermatitis)

### 6.4 Vitiligo

- **Autoimmune destruction of melanocytes** causing depigmented patches
- **Types:** Segmental (unilateral, dermatomal, earlier onset, stable), non-segmental (generalized, acrofacial, universal)
- **Associations:** Other autoimmune diseases (thyroid disease, pernicious anemia, type 1 diabetes, alopecia areata)
- **Treatment:**
  - **Limited (<10% BSA):** Potent topical corticosteroids (short-term), topical calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus, pimecrolimus — especially for face), topical ruxolitinib 1.5% cream (Opzelura — FDA-approved for non-segmental vitiligo)
  - **Moderate-severe:** Phototherapy (NB-UVB); oral mini-pulse steroids for rapidly progressive disease
  - **Stable/refractory:** Autologous melanocyte transplantation
  - **Cosmetic:** Cover-up makeup, self-tanners, depigmentation (monobenzone for extensive disease)
- **Psychosocial impact:** Significant; provide counseling and support

### 6.5 Alopecia Areata

- **Autoimmune T-cell-mediated attack** on hair follicles
- **Clinical:** Well-circumscribed patches of non-scarring hair loss; exclamation mark hairs; nail pitting
- **Severity:** Patchy, alopecia totalis (entire scalp), alopecia universalis (entire body)
- **Treatment:** [^24^]
  - **Limited (<50% scalp):** Intralesional corticosteroids (triamcinolone acetonide 2.5-10 mg/mL); topical corticosteroids; minoxidil; topical immunotherapy (DPCP, squaric acid)
  - **Extensive:** Oral JAK inhibitors (baricitinib [Olumiant] — FDA-approved for severe AA; tofacitinib, ruxolitinib — off-label); systemic corticosteroids (pulse therapy)
  - **Support:** Wigs, hairpieces, psychological support
- **Baricitinib (Olumiant):** 2-4 mg daily; FDA-approved for adults with severe alopecia areata
- **Prognosis:** Variable; spontaneous regrowth common in limited disease; worse prognosis with early onset, extensive involvement, atopy, nail changes

### 6.6 Seborrheic Dermatitis

- **Chronic, relapsing inflammatory condition** affecting sebaceous gland-rich areas (scalp, face, chest)
- **Pathophysiology:** *Malassezia* yeast overgrowth, sebum composition, immune response, inflammation
- **Associated with:** HIV, Parkinson's disease, stroke, heart failure, mood disorders
- **Treatment:**
  - **Scalp:** Ketoconazole 2% shampoo, selenium sulfide 2.5%, zinc pyrithione shampoo, coal tar shampoo, salicylic acid shampoo; topical corticosteroid solutions/foams for flares
  - **Face:** Ketoconazole 2% cream, mild topical corticosteroids (hydrocortisone 1-2.5%), topical calcineurin inhibitors
  - **Refractory:** Consider oral fluconazole or itraconazole pulse therapy
  - **Infantile (cradle cap):** Mineral oil, gentle shampooing; usually resolves spontaneously

### 6.7 Hidradenitis Suppurativa (HS)

- **Chronic, recurrent inflammatory disease** of follicular occlusion in apocrine gland-bearing areas (axillae, groin, perianal, inframammary)
- **Clinical:** Painful nodules, abscesses, draining sinus tracts, scarring; Hurley stages I-III
- **Associated with:** Obesity, smoking, metabolic syndrome, depression, Crohn's disease, acne conglobata, pilonidal disease (follicular occlusion tetrad)
- **Treatment Guidelines (AAD/European S2k 2024):** [^25^] [^26^]
  - **General:** Smoking cessation, weight loss, loose clothing, wound care
  - **Mild (Hurley I):** Topical clindamycin 1%; oral tetracyclines (doxycycline) x >=12 weeks; intralesional corticosteroids
  - **Moderate (Hurley II):** Oral tetracyclines -> clindamycin + rifampicin combination x 10-12 weeks; if refractory -> adalimumab
  - **Severe (Hurley III):** Adalimumab (first-line biologic); infliximab; secukinumab; bimekizumab; surgical excision
  - **Adalimumab dosing:** 160 mg (week 0), 80 mg (week 2), then 40 mg weekly (may increase to 80 mg weekly in non-responders)
  - **Pain management:** Essential component; consider multimodal analgesia
  - **Screen for:** Depression, anxiety, diabetes, cardiovascular risk factors

## 1. Osteoarthritis (OA)

### 1.1 Clinical Overview

Osteoarthritis is the most common form of arthritis, affecting an estimated 32.5 million adults in the United States. It is a degenerative joint disease characterized by progressive loss of articular cartilage, subchondral bone remodeling, osteophyte formation, and synovial inflammation. The "wear and tear" model has evolved to recognize OA as a disease involving the whole joint organ, with contributions from mechanical, inflammatory, and metabolic factors. [^27^]

**Commonly Affected Joints:** Knee, hip, hand (DIP, PIP, first CMC), spine, foot

**Risk Factors:**
- Non-modifiable: Age, female sex, genetics, joint shape/alignment
- Modifiable: Obesity (strongest modifiable risk factor), prior joint injury, repetitive joint loading, muscle weakness

### 1.2 Guidelines & Evidence

**ACR/Arthritis Foundation Guidelines (2019/2024):** Recommendations vary by joint but share core principles [^27^] [^28^]

**Strongly Recommended:**
- Exercise (aerobic, resistance, neuromuscular)
- Weight loss for overweight/obese patients
- Self-efficacy and self-management programs
- Oral NSAIDs (shortest duration, lowest effective dose)
- Total joint replacement for end-stage disease

**Conditionally Recommended:**
- Topical NSAIDs (especially hand/knee OA; first-line pharmacologic for hand OA)
- Intra-articular corticosteroid injections
- Acetaminophen (limited benefit; use lowest effective dose)
- Duloxetine
- Tai chi, yoga, aquatic therapy
- Cane use for unilateral lower extremity OA
- Cognitive behavioral therapy

**Strongly Recommended AGAINST:**
- Glucosamine, chondroitin sulfate
- Hyaluronic acid injections (knee)
- TNF inhibitors, IL-1 antagonists
- Platelet-rich plasma, stem cell injections
- Opioids (non-tramadol)
- Bisphosphonates
- Hydroxychloroquine, methotrexate (for hand OA)

### 1.3 Non-Pharmacologic Management

**Exercise:** Core treatment for all OA — strongest evidence-based recommendation
- Aerobic exercise (walking, cycling, swimming)
- Resistance/strengthening exercises
- Range of motion/flexibility exercises
- Neuromuscular/balance training
- Aquatic therapy for patients with significant pain
- Physical therapy referral for individualized program

**Weight Management:**
- 5-10% weight loss significantly improves symptoms and function in knee/hip OA
- Greater weight loss associated with greater benefit
- Combined with exercise for optimal outcomes

**Assistive Devices:**
- Canes, walkers, orthotics, braces, patellar taping
- Shoe modifications (lateral wedged insoles for medial knee OA — evidence mixed)
- Assistive devices for activities of daily living

**Self-Management:**
- Patient education (arthritis self-management programs)
- Cognitive behavioral therapy for pain coping
- Heat/cold therapy
- Acupuncture (conditionally recommended)

### 1.4 Pharmacologic Management

| Agent | Recommendation | Notes |
|-------|----------------|-------|
| **Topical NSAIDs** (diclofenac gel) | First-line for hand/knee OA | Lower systemic exposure; preferred over oral NSAIDs when feasible; minimal GI/renal/cardiovascular risk |
| **Oral NSAIDs** | Strongly recommended | Shortest duration, lowest dose; COX-2 selective (celecoxib) for lower GI risk; monitor for CV/renal/GI toxicity; avoid in CKD, PUD, anticoagulation, cardiovascular disease |
| **Acetaminophen** | Conditionally recommended | Up to 3 g/day (previously 4 g — reduced due to liver toxicity); modest efficacy |
| **Duloxetine** (Cymbalta) | Conditionally recommended | 60 mg daily; especially useful for patients with concomitant widespread pain, depression; may take 2-4 weeks for benefit |
| **Intra-articular corticosteroids** | Conditionally recommended | Rapid short-term relief (1-4 weeks); repeat every 3-4 months if needed; avoid more frequent injections due to cartilage damage risk |
| **Tramadol** | Conditionally recommended | Weakest opioid; reserve for refractory cases; monitor for sedation, constipation |
| **Topical capsaicin** | Conditionally recommended AGAINST (hand OA) | Burning sensation limits adherence |
| **Intra-articular hyaluronic acid** | Not recommended (AAOS) | Mixed evidence; expensive; not recommended by most guidelines for knee OA |

### 1.5 Surgical Options

**Total Joint Replacement (Arthroplasty):**
- Indicated for end-stage OA with intractable pain and functional limitation despite conservative treatment
- **Total knee arthroplasty (TKA)** and **total hip arthroplasty (THA):** Highly successful; 90-95% patient satisfaction
- Prehabilitation (exercise, weight loss, education) improves outcomes
- **Partial knee replacement:** Select patients with unicompartmental disease

**Other Surgical Options:**
- **Osteotomy:** Realignment for younger patients with unicompartmental disease and malalignment
- **Arthroscopy:** Limited role; not recommended for OA without mechanical symptoms (meniscal tear, loose body)
- **Joint fusion (arthrodesis):** For severe ankle, wrist, thumb CMC OA

### 1.6 Emerging Therapies

- **Platelet-rich plasma (PRP):** Not recommended by ACR due to insufficient evidence
- **Stem cell therapy:** Not recommended; no high-quality evidence
- **Biologics targeting inflammatory pathways:** Under investigation; not standard of care

## 2. Rheumatoid Arthritis (RA)

### 2.1 Clinical Overview

Rheumatoid arthritis is a chronic, systemic autoimmune disease characterized by symmetric, inflammatory polyarthritis. It affects ~1% of the adult population worldwide, with a female predominance (3:1). Untreated RA causes progressive joint destruction, disability, and increased mortality (primarily cardiovascular). Early diagnosis and treatment are critical — most joint damage occurs within the first 6-12 months. [^29^] [^30^]

**Diagnostic Features:**
- Symmetric small joint arthritis (MCP, PIP, MTP) — hallmark
- Morning stiffness >60 minutes
- Elevated inflammatory markers (ESR, CRP)
- Positive rheumatoid factor (RF) and/or anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide (anti-CCP) antibodies
- Radiographic erosions (especially at MCP, MTP, wrist)
- Extra-articular manifestations: rheumatoid nodules, interstitial lung disease, vasculitis, Sjogren's syndrome, Felty syndrome

**2010 ACR/EULAR Classification Criteria** (for early diagnosis): Score-based algorithm including joint involvement, serology, acute-phase reactants, and duration. Score >=6/10 = definite RA.

### 2.2 Principles of Management: Treat-to-Target

**ACR 2024 Guidelines** emphasize: [^30^] [^31^]
- **Early DMARD initiation** within 3 months of symptom onset
- **Treat-to-target:** Adjust therapy every 1-3 months to achieve remission or low disease activity
- **Methotrexate as first-line** for moderate-high disease activity
- **Avoid long-term glucocorticoids** (>3 months) when possible

### 2.3 Conventional Synthetic DMARDs (csDMARDs)

| Agent | Dosing | Monitoring | Key Considerations |
|-------|--------|------------|-------------------|
| **Methotrexate (MTX)** | 7.5-25 mg weekly (oral, SC, IM); start 7.5-15 mg | CBC, LFTs, creatinine every 2-4 weeks initially, then q8-12 weeks | First-line for RA; folic acid 1-5 mg daily (except MTX day); teratogenic; avoid in pregnancy, breastfeeding, significant liver disease; caution in renal impairment |
| **Hydroxychloroquine (HCQ)** | 200-400 mg daily | Ophthalmologic exam annually after 5 years (retinal toxicity screening) | Safe in pregnancy; slow onset (3-6 months); used for mild disease or combination therapy |
| **Sulfasalazine (SSZ)** | 500 mg daily, titrate to 2-3 g/day in divided doses | CBC, LFTs at baseline and periodically | Sulfonamide component; caution with sulfa allergy; may cause GI upset; compatible with pregnancy |
| **Leflunomide** | 10-20 mg daily | CBC, LFTs at baseline and periodically | Teratogenic; requires cholestyramine washout before conception (for both men and women); avoid in pregnancy |

**Strongly Recommended by ACR 2024:** [^31^]
- MTX monotherapy over biologic or JAK inhibitor monotherapy for DMARD-naive patients with moderate-high disease activity
- MTX over hydroxychloroquine or sulfasalazine for moderate-high disease activity
- csDMARD initiation WITHOUT longer-term glucocorticoids over initiation with glucocorticoids
- Treat-to-target approach over usual care

### 2.4 Biologic DMARDs (bDMARDs)

All require **TB screening before initiation** and **hepatitis B screening**. Live vaccines contraindicated during use.

#### TNF-alpha Inhibitors
| Agent | Dosing | Notes |
|-------|--------|-------|
| **Adalimumab** (Humira) | 40 mg SC Q2W | Available biosimilars |
| **Etanercept** (Enbrel) | 25 mg SC BIW or 50 mg QW | Less effective for IBD, uveitis |
| **Infliximab** (Remicade) | 3-5 mg/kg IV at weeks 0, 2, 6, then Q8W | Infusion reactions; need premedication |
| **Golimumab** (Simponi) | 50 mg SC QW | Once monthly option |
| **Certolizumab pegol** (Cimzia) | 200 mg SC Q2W or 400 mg Q4W | Minimal placental transfer; preferred in pregnancy |

**AEs:** Increased infection risk (including TB reactivation), injection site/infusion reactions, new/worsening heart failure (avoid NYHA III-IV), demyelinating disease (avoid in MS), lymphoma (small increased risk)

#### Non-TNF Biologics
| Agent | Target | Dosing | Notes |
|-------|--------|--------|-------|
| **Abatacept** (Orencia) | CTLA-4-Ig (T-cell costimulation inhibitor) | IV or SC monthly | Less immunogenic; may have lower infection risk; safe in elderly |
| **Rituximab** (Rituxan) | CD20 (B-cell depletion) | 1000 mg IV x 2 doses, 2 weeks apart; repeat as needed (typically 6-12 months) | First dose reactions; hepatitis B reactivation risk; monitor IgG levels |
| **Tocilizumab** (Actemra) | IL-6 receptor | IV monthly or SC Q2W | Elevation in LFTs, lipids; neutropenia; diverticular perforation risk |
| **Sarilumab** (Kevzara) | IL-6 receptor | 200 mg SC Q2W | Similar to tocilizumab |

### 2.5 Targeted Synthetic DMARDs (JAK Inhibitors)

| Agent | Selectivity | Dosing | Notes |
|-------|-------------|--------|-------|
| **Tofacitinib** (Xeljanz) | JAK1/JAK3 | 5 mg BID or 11 mg XR daily | First approved JAK for RA |
| **Baricitinib** (Olumiant) | JAK1/JAK2 | 2 mg daily | Approved in Europe for AD |
| **Upadacitinib** (Rinvoq) | JAK1 selective | 15 mg daily | Higher efficacy in some trials |

**Black Box Warning:** Increased risk of serious cardiovascular events, malignancy, thrombosis (VTE, PE), and death based on tofacitinib ORAL Surveillance study. [^29^]

**ACR/EULAR Position:** Reserve JAK inhibitors for patients with inadequate response/intolerance to at least one bDMARD, particularly those with increased cardiovascular risk or malignancy risk. [^30^]

**Monitoring:**
- Baseline: CBC with differential, LFTs, lipid panel, hepatitis B/C, TB screening
- Follow-up: CBC, LFTs q4 weeks initially then q12 weeks; lipids at 12 weeks
- Shingles vaccination before starting; all live vaccines prior to initiation

### 2.6 Treatment Algorithm

1. **DMARD-naive, moderate-high disease activity:** Methotrexate first-line
2. **Inadequate response to MTX after 3 months:** Add or switch
   - Triple therapy (MTX + SSZ + HCQ) — cost-effective option
   - Add bDMARD (TNF inhibitor or non-TNF)
   - Switch to JAK inhibitor (after bDMARD failure per current guidance)
3. **Low disease activity/remission achieved:** Gradual taper of glucocorticoids first; consider tapering DMARDs after sustained remission (>6-12 months)
4. **Refractory disease:** Consider rituximab, combination biologic + MTX

### 2.7 Glucocorticoids in RA

- **Bridge therapy:** Short-term (<=3 months) low-dose prednisone (5-10 mg) while waiting for DMARD onset
- **Intra-articular:** For single or few active joints
- **Avoid long-term systemic use** due to toxicity (osteoporosis, diabetes, cardiovascular, infection)
- Taper to <5 mg/day as soon as possible

### 2.8 Monitoring and Complications

**Disease Activity Assessment:**
- DAS28-CRP (Disease Activity Score) — most commonly used
- CDAI (Clinical Disease Activity Index) — no labs required
- SDAI (Simplified Disease Activity Index)
- ACR20/50/70 response criteria

**Complications:**
- **CVD:** Leading cause of death in RA; aggressive risk factor modification; statins as indicated
- **Osteoporosis:** Screen with DEXA; bisphosphonate prophylaxis if on glucocorticoids
- **Infection:** Increased risk from disease and immunosuppression; keep vaccinations current (influenza, pneumococcal, shingles)
- **Interstitial lung disease:** Screen with HRCT and PFTs if respiratory symptoms

## 3. Gout and Pseudogout

### 3.1 Clinical Overview

**Gout** is an inflammatory arthritis caused by deposition of monosodium urate (MSU) crystals in joints and tissues, resulting from chronic hyperuricemia. It affects 3.9% of US adults (male predominance) and is the most common inflammatory arthritis in men. [^32^]

**Risk Factors:** Hyperuricemia (>6.8 mg/dL), male sex, postmenopausal women, obesity, alcohol (especially beer and spirits), purine-rich diet (red meat, seafood), fructose-sweetened beverages, CKD, diuretics (thiazides, loop), low-dose aspirin, cyclosporine, lead exposure, psoriasis, myeloproliferative disorders

**Pathophysiology:** Hyperuricemia -> MSU crystal formation in joint/tissues -> phagocytosis by neutrophils -> NLRP3 inflammasome activation -> IL-1beta release -> acute inflammation

### 3.2 Diagnosis

**Definitive Diagnosis:** Synovial fluid analysis demonstrating **negatively birefringent, needle-shaped MSU crystals** under compensated polarized light microscopy. [^32^]

**Clinical Diagnosis (when aspiration not feasible):**
- Serum urate >=6 mg/dL (may be normal during acute attack)
- Typical presentation: Acute onset of severe pain, erythema, swelling, warmth (often first MTP — podagra)
- Rapid peak (<24 hours), self-limited (3-14 days), complete resolution between attacks
- Tophi (chronic deposits in joints, helix, olecranon bursae)

**Imaging:** X-rays may show erosions with "overhanging edge" in chronic gout; ultrasound shows "double contour sign"; DECT can detect urate deposits

**Classification:** ACR/EULAR 2015 gout classification criteria (score-based)

### 3.3 Acute Gout Management

Goal: Rapid pain relief and inflammation reduction. **Start anti-inflammatory as soon as possible.**

| Agent | Dosing | Key Considerations |
|-------|--------|-------------------|
| **NSAIDs** | Naproxen 500 mg BID, indomethacin 50 mg TID; full dose until attack resolves, then taper | Avoid in CKD, PUD, anticoagulation, significant CVD; GI protection with PPI |
| **Colchicine** | 1.2 mg at first sign, then 0.6 mg 1 hour later (2-tablet regimen); OR 0.6 mg TID until resolved | Start within 36 hours; dose adjust for renal impairment; CYP3A4/P-gp interactions (avoid with clarithromycin, diltiazem, verapamil, cyclosporine); GI side effects common |
| **Corticosteroids** | Prednisone 30-35 mg daily x 5 days (or 3-day burst); intra-articular triamcinolone for monoarticular | Preferred if NSAIDs and colchicine contraindicated; may cause transient rebound; avoid if concern for septic arthritis without aspiration |
| **IL-1 inhibitors** (off-label) | Anakinra, canakinumab | For refractory gout; canakinumab expensive but highly effective |

**General principles:**
- Continue urate-lowering therapy (ULT) during acute attacks — do not stop
- Rest, ice, elevation of affected limb
- Monarticular gout: Consider joint aspiration to exclude septic arthritis
- Do NOT start ULT during acute attack (unless already on it or prophylaxis provided)

### 3.4 Urate-Lowering Therapy (ULT)

**Indications for ULT:** [^32^]
- Recurrent flares (>=2/year)
- Tophi or chronic gouty arthritis
- Stage 3+ CKD
- Urolithiasis history
- Serum urate >9 mg/dL

**Treat-to-Target:** Goal serum urate <6 mg/dL (<5 mg/dL if tophaceous gout)

| Agent | Dosing | Notes |
|-------|--------|-------|
| **Allopurinol** | Start 100 mg daily (50 mg if CKD); titrate every 2-5 weeks to target; max 800 mg/day | First-line ULT; HLA-B*5801 testing before starting in high-risk ethnicities (Han Chinese, Thai, Koreans, African Americans) due to severe cutaneous adverse reactions risk; safe to start during acute attack with anti-inflammatory prophylaxis |
| **Febuxostat** (Uloric) | 40-80 mg daily | Second-line if allopurinol intolerance; **black box warning** for increased cardiovascular death — avoid in patients with CVD; no need for dose adjustment in mild-moderate renal impairment |
| **Probenecid** | 500 mg BID, titrate to 2 g/day | Uricosuric; promote renal excretion; avoid if urolithiasis history or CKD (eGFR <30); requires high fluid intake; less effective as monotherapy |
| **Pegloticase** (Krystexxa) | 8 mg IV every 2 weeks | Recombinant uricase; for refractory, tophaceous gout; risk of infusion reactions (pretreat with antihistamine, corticosteroid); check uric acid before each infusion; stop if >6 mg/dL |

**Prophylaxis Against Flares When Initiating ULT:**
- Colchicine 0.6 mg daily or BID for 3-6 months
- OR low-dose NSAID with PPI
- OR low-dose prednisone (<=10 mg daily) if colchicine/NSAIDs contraindicated
- **Essential to provide flare prophylaxis when starting ULT** — flares are common as urate shifts

### 3.5 Chronic Gout Management

- **Patient education:** Dietary modification (limit purines, alcohol, fructose), weight loss, hydration
- **Screen for comorbidities:** Hypertension, CKD, metabolic syndrome, CVD
- **Manage cardiovascular risk:** Statins, blood pressure control, diabetes management
- **Medication review:** Minimize thiazide diuretics when possible
- **Lifestyle:** Low-fat dairy, coffee, vitamin C may have protective effects; cherries may reduce flare risk

### 3.6 Pseudogout (Calcium Pyrophosphate Deposition Disease — CPPD)

- **Pathophysiology:** Calcium pyrophosphate dihydrate (CPPD) crystal deposition in joints
- **Diagnosis:** Synovial fluid analysis showing **weakly positively birefringent, rhomboid-shaped crystals**
- **Clinical:** Acute attacks (pseudogout) or chronic degenerative arthritis; commonly affects knee, wrist, MCP, shoulder
- **Associations:** Advanced age, hemochromatosis, hyperparathyroidism, hypothyroidism, hypomagnesemia, hypophosphatasia
- **X-rays:** Chondrocalcinosis (calcification of cartilage)
- **Treatment:** Similar to gout — NSAIDs, colchicine, corticosteroids (intra-articular preferred)
- **Chronic management:** Low-dose colchicine prophylaxis for recurrent attacks; treat underlying metabolic disorders

## 4. Low Back Pain

### 4.1 Clinical Overview

Low back pain is the leading cause of disability worldwide. Most episodes are acute (6 weeks) and resolve spontaneously. Key priorities are identifying serious pathology (red flags), managing symptoms, preventing chronicity, and maintaining activity. [^33^] [^34^]

**Categories:**
- **Non-specific low back pain:** Most common (~85%); no identifiable cause
- **Radicular pain (sciatica):** Nerve root compression (herniated disc, stenosis)
- **Specific causes:** Fracture, infection, malignancy, inflammatory spondyloarthropathy, cauda equina syndrome

### 4.2 Red Flags — "Can't Miss" Diagnoses

**Absolute Red Flags (urgent/emergency workup):** [^33^] [^35^]

| Red Flag | Condition to Rule Out | Action |
|----------|----------------------|--------|
| Cauda equina symptoms (saddle anesthesia, urinary retention, fecal incontinence, bilateral leg weakness/numbness) | Cauda equina syndrome | **EMERGENCY MRI + urgent neurosurgical referral** |
| Fever + severe back pain + risk factors | Spinal infection (discitis, osteomyelitis, epidural abscess) | Urgent MRI + infectious disease consultation |
| History of cancer + new back pain | Metastatic spinal disease | Urgent MRI + oncology referral |
| Significant trauma + osteoporosis risk | Vertebral compression fracture | X-ray/CT + pain management + consider osteoporosis workup |
| Progressive neurological deficit | Cord compression, tumor | Urgent MRI + appropriate specialist referral |
| IV drug use + back pain + fever | Spinal epidural abscess | Emergency MRI + surgical evaluation |

**Relative Red Flags (consider imaging/referral):**
- Unexplained weight loss, night sweats
- Age >50 with new-onset back pain
- Pain at rest or at night
- Failure to improve after 4-6 weeks of conservative treatment
- Long-term glucocorticoid use
- Inflammatory back pain features (morning stiffness >30 min, improves with exercise, night pain)

### 4.3 Cauda Equina Syndrome (CES) — Emergency

- **Compression of cauda equina** (nerve roots below L2)
- **Triad:** Saddle anesthesia (S2-S5 dermatomes), bladder/bowel dysfunction (urinary retention > incontinence), decreased rectal tone
- **Other features:** Bilateral leg pain/weakness, severe radiculopathy
- **Urgent MRI** is diagnostic gold standard
- **Surgical decompression within 48 hours** improves outcomes — time-sensitive emergency
- **Refer immediately** if any suspicion

### 4.4 Imaging Guidelines

**Do NOT image in the first 6 weeks** for uncomplicated acute low back pain without red flags. [^34^]

**When to Image:**
- Red flags present (MRI preferred)
- Progressive neurological deficit
- Suspected malignancy, infection, fracture
- Cauda equina syndrome (emergency MRI)
- Failure to improve after 4-6 weeks of conservative treatment
- Before invasive interventions (epidural injection, surgery)

**Modality:**
- **X-ray:** Vertebral fracture suspected (trauma, osteoporosis)
- **CT:** Fracture assessment, contraindication to MRI, surgical planning
- **MRI:** Gold standard for disc herniation, spinal stenosis, infection, malignancy, CES

### 4.5 Management

**Acute Low Back Pain (<6 weeks):**
- **Stay active:** Avoid prolonged bed rest; encourage gentle activity and walking
- **Heat therapy:** More effective than cold
- **Pharmacologic:**
  - Acetaminophen or NSAIDs first-line (shortest duration)
  - Muscle relaxants (short-term) for severe spasms — sedation risk
  - Opioids: **Not first-line**; short-term only if severe, refractory pain and alternatives inadequate
  - Gabapentinoids: Limited evidence for acute LBP
- **Education:** Reassurance that most cases improve; prognosis is favorable
- **Physical therapy:** Consider if not improving after 2-3 weeks

**Subacute/Chronic Low Back Pain (>12 weeks):**
- **Multidisciplinary approach:** Physical therapy, psychological support, pharmacologic
- **Exercise therapy:** Core strengthening, aerobic exercise, yoga, tai chi — strongest evidence
- **Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT):** For pain coping, fear-avoidance behaviors
- **Pharmacologic:**
  - NSAIDs (if not contraindicated)
  - Duloxetine (SNRI) — best evidence for chronic LBP
  - Tramadol (short-term)
  - Gabapentin/pregabalin — limited evidence for non-radicular chronic LBP
- **Interventional:**
  - Epidural steroid injection: For radicular pain (short-term benefit, 2-6 weeks); not for axial LBP
  - Facet joint injections, medial branch blocks, radiofrequency ablation: For suspected facet-mediated pain
  - Spinal cord stimulation: For refractory cases
- **Surgical:** Indicated for progressive neurologic deficit, CES, confirmed disc herniation with persistent radiculopathy, spinal stenosis with neurogenic claudication

**Radiculopathy:**
- 90% of disc herniations improve spontaneously within 6-12 weeks
- MRI if severe/progressive neurologic deficit, failure to improve, or before surgery
- Conservative management similar to acute LBP
- Epidural steroid injection may provide short-term relief
- Surgery (microdiscectomy) for persistent, disabling radiculopathy after 6-12 weeks of failed conservative treatment

## 5. Osteoporosis

### 5.1 Clinical Overview

Osteoporosis is a skeletal disorder characterized by compromised bone strength predisposing to increased fracture risk. It affects 10.2 million adults in the US, with another 43.4 million having low bone mass (osteopenia). One in two women and one in four men >50 years will experience an osteoporotic fracture. [^36^]

**Risk Factors:**
- Non-modifiable: Female sex, advanced age, Caucasian/Asian race, family history of hip fracture, prior fragility fracture, menopause (especially early)
- Modifiable: Low BMI, smoking, excessive alcohol (>3 drinks/day), glucocorticoid use, low calcium/vitamin D intake, physical inactivity, falls
- Secondary causes: Hyperthyroidism, hyperparathyroidism, Cushing's, celiac disease, CKD, multiple myeloma, medications (glucocorticoids, aromatase inhibitors, ADT, PPIs, anticonvulsants, SSRIs, methotrexate)

### 5.2 Diagnosis

**FRAX Tool:** 10-year probability of major osteoporotic fracture or hip fracture; integrates clinical risk factors with or without BMD. Available at: https://www.sheffield.ac.uk/FRAX/

**DEXA (Dual-Energy X-ray Absorptiometry):**
- **T-score** (standard deviations from young adult mean):
  - Normal: T-score >= -1.0
  - Osteopenia: T-score -1.0 to -2.5
  - Osteoporosis: T-score <= -2.5
- **Indications for DEXA:**
  - Women >=65 years, men >=70 years
  - Younger postmenopausal women with risk factors
  - Adults with fragility fracture
  - Adults with conditions/medications associated with bone loss
  - Anyone being considered for pharmacologic therapy
  - Monitoring response to therapy (every 1-2 years)

**Vertebral Fracture Assessment (VFA):** Recommended when T-score <= -1.0 with height loss, kyphosis, glucocorticoid use

### 5.3 Pharmacologic Therapy

**Indications for Treatment:** [^36^] [^37^]
- T-score <= -2.5 (spine, femoral neck, or total hip)
- T-score -1.0 to -2.5 with FRAX 10-year major osteoporotic fracture risk >=20% or hip fracture risk >=3%
- Prior hip or vertebral fracture (clinical or morphometric)
- Glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis (see below)

| Class | Agent | Dosing | Key Considerations |
|-------|-------|--------|-------------------|
| **Bisphosphonates (oral)** | Alendronate (Fosamax) | 70 mg weekly | First-line; take on empty stomach with full glass plain water; remain upright 30-60 min; GI side effects |
| | Risedronate (Actonel) | 35 mg weekly or 150 mg monthly | Similar to alendronate; potentially lower GI risk |
| | Ibandronate (Boniva) | 150 mg monthly | Less potent; also available IV |
| **Bisphosphonates (IV)** | Zoledronic acid (Reclast) | 5 mg IV annually | Ensure vitamin D repletion before infusion; acute phase reaction (fever, myalgia); renal function monitoring (CrCl >35 required); once every 2 years for zoledronic acid after 3 years |
| **RANKL inhibitor** | Denosumab (Prolia) | 60 mg SC every 6 months | Alternative first-line; no GI/renal concerns; must NOT stop without bisphosphonate transition (rebound fractures risk); check calcium/vitamin D before each dose; higher hypocalcemia risk with CKD |
| **Anabolic (PTH analog)** | Teriparatide (Forteo) | 20 mcg SC daily x 2 years | For very high fracture risk (T-score < -3.5, multiple fractures, glucocorticoid-induced); sequential therapy with antiresorptive after completion |
| | Abaloparatide (Tymlos) | 80 mcg SC daily x 2 years | Similar to teriparatide; possibly lower hypercalcemia risk |
| **Sclerostin inhibitor** | Romosozumab (Evenity) | 210 mg SC monthly x 12 months | Dual action (increases bone formation, decreases resorption); very high fracture risk; must follow with antiresorptive; AV block/cardiiovascular risk consideration |

**Treatment Duration and Drug Holidays:**
- Oral bisphosphonates: Reassess after 3-5 years; if fracture risk no longer high, consider drug holiday (1-2 years) with monitoring; resume if BMD declines or fracture occurs
- IV zoledronic acid: Reassess after 3 years
- Denosumab: Do NOT stop without planning transition to bisphosphonate (rebound vertebral fractures within 7-18 months)
- Teriparatide/abaloparatide: Maximum 2 years; must follow with antiresorptive
- Romosozumab: 12 months; must follow with antiresorptive

**Sequential Therapy:** After anabolic therapy (teriparatide, romosozumab), always transition to antiresorptive therapy (bisphosphonate or denosumab) to maintain gains.

### 5.4 Glucocorticoid-Induced Osteoporosis (GIOP)

- Significant bone loss occurs within first 3-6 months of glucocorticoid use
- Fracture risk increases at doses >=2.5 mg prednisone equivalent/day
- **Start bone protection** when: planned glucocorticoids >=7.5 mg/day for >=3 months; or any dose if existing high fracture risk
- **Agents:** Oral bisphosphonates or IV zoledronic acid first-line; teriparatide for highest risk
- Ensure adequate calcium and vitamin D

### 5.5 Non-Pharmacologic Management

**Calcium:**
- Total intake 1000-1200 mg/day (diet + supplements)
- 1200 mg/day for postmenopausal women, men >70 years
- Calcium carbonate: Take with meals; cheapest; requires acid for absorption
- Calcium citrate: Can take without food; better absorption with PPI use

**Vitamin D:**
- 800-1000 IU daily for adults >=50 years
- Goal 25(OH)D level: >30 ng/mL (adequate)
- Higher doses may be needed for deficiency replacement

**Exercise:**
- Weight-bearing and resistance exercises
- Balance training to reduce falls
- Core strengthening

**Fall Prevention:** (See comprehensive section below)

**Lifestyle:**
- Smoking cessation
- Limit alcohol (<=2 drinks/day men, <=1 drink/day women)
- Maintain healthy weight

### 5.6 Fall Prevention

Falls are the leading cause of fractures in older adults. Multifactorial fall risk assessment and intervention is recommended. [^36^]

**Risk Assessment:**
- History of falls in past year
- Gait, balance, and mobility assessment (Timed Up and Go test)
- Vision assessment
- Medication review (psychotropics, sedatives, antihypertensives)
- Home environment assessment
- Orthostatic blood pressure
- Footwear assessment

**Interventions:**
- Exercise programs focusing on strength and balance (Tai Chi highly effective)
- Home modification (remove loose rugs, install grab bars, improve lighting)
- Vision correction
- Medication optimization (reduce sedatives, psychotropics)
- Management of orthostatic hypotension
- Proper footwear
- Vitamin D supplementation (800 IU/day minimum)
- Hip protectors for very high-risk patients
- Environmental hazard reduction

## 6. Common Sports Injuries

### 6.1 Sprains vs. Strains

| Feature | Sprain | Strain |
|---------|--------|--------|
| **Tissue injured** | Ligament | Muscle or tendon |
| **Mechanism** | Stretching/tearing of ligament | Stretching/tearing of muscle/tendon |
| **Grading** | Grade 1 (mild stretch), Grade 2 (partial tear), Grade 3 (complete rupture) | Grade 1 (mild), Grade 2 (moderate), Grade 3 (severe/complete) |
| **Examples** | Ankle sprain (lateral), ACL/MCL tear | Hamstring strain, groin pull |

### 6.2 RICE Protocol

**Immediate management of acute soft tissue injuries:** [^38^]

| Component | Action | Duration |
|-----------|--------|----------|
| **R**est | Protect injured area; avoid activities that cause pain; consider immobilization if severe | First 24-72 hours |
| **I**ce | Apply ice pack (wrapped in thin towel) for 15-20 minutes every 2-3 hours | First 48-72 hours |
| **C**ompression | Elastic bandage or compression sleeve; firm but not tight (should not cause numbness/tingling) | First 48-72 hours |
| **E**levation | Elevate above heart level to reduce swelling | As much as possible, first 48-72 hours |

**Modified/additional considerations:**
- **Protection and optimal loading** are increasingly emphasized over strict rest
- Early gentle range of motion prevents stiffness
- Avoid HARM in first 48-72 hours: Heat, Alcohol, Running, Massage
- Gradual return to activity as symptoms allow

### 6.3 Rotator Cuff Injuries

**Anatomy:** Four muscles (supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor, subscapularis) stabilize and move the shoulder

**Types:**
- **Tendinopathy/impingement:** Overuse, degeneration; pain with overhead activity
- **Partial tear:** More significant pain, weakness
- **Full-thickness tear:** Significant weakness, functional limitation

**Treatment:**
- **Conservative (most cases):** Rest, NSAIDs, physical therapy (rotator cuff strengthening, scapular stabilization), subacromial corticosteroid injection
- **Surgical repair:** Indicated for full-thickness tears in active patients, failed conservative management (3-6 months), acute traumatic tears in younger patients
- **Arthroscopic vs. open repair:** Most performed arthroscopically

### 6.4 Meniscus Tears

**Mechanism:** Acute (twisting injury in younger patients) or degenerative (older patients with minimal trauma)

**Symptoms:** Joint line pain, swelling, locking, catching, difficulty with deep flexion

**Treatment:**
- **Conservative:** RICE, NSAIDs, physical therapy (quadriceps/hamstring strengthening)
- **Indications for surgery:** Mechanical symptoms (locking), failure of conservative management, repairable tears in younger patients
- **Meniscectomy vs. repair:** Repair preferred for peripheral tears in younger patients (better long-term outcomes); partial meniscectomy for irreparable tears
- **Avoid total meniscectomy** when possible (increases OA risk)

### 6.5 ACL Injuries

**Mechanism:** Non-contact pivoting, deceleration, landing (common in soccer, basketball, skiing)

**Symptoms:** "Pop" sensation, immediate swelling (hemarthrosis), instability

**Diagnosis:** Lachman test (most sensitive), anterior drawer, pivot shift; MRI confirms

**Treatment:**
- **Non-surgical:** Physical therapy (quadriceps/hamstring strengthening, proprioception), activity modification, bracing; suitable for older, less active patients
- **Surgical reconstruction:** For younger, active patients; restores stability; PT-guided rehabilitation (6-12 months return to sport)
- **ACL prevention programs:** Neuromuscular training reduces injury risk by 50-80% in female athletes

### 6.6 Tennis Elbow (Lateral Epicondylitis)

- **Overuse injury** of extensor carpi radialis brevis tendon origin
- **Symptoms:** Lateral elbow pain with gripping, wrist extension
- **Treatment:**
  - Conservative: Activity modification, counterforce bracing, physical therapy (eccentric exercises), NSAIDs
  - Corticosteroid injection: Short-term benefit but may delay healing; use with caution
  - Platelet-rich plasma: Mixed evidence
  - Surgery: Rarely needed; for refractory cases (>12 months)
- **Golfer's elbow (medial epicondylitis):** Similar but affects flexor/pronator tendons

### 6.7 Plantar Fasciitis

- **Most common cause of heel pain** in adults
- **Pathophysiology:** Degenerative changes in plantar fascia origin at medial calcaneal tubercle
- **Symptoms:** Sharp, stabbing heel pain with first steps in morning or after rest; improves with activity
- **Risk factors:** Obesity, prolonged standing, flat feet/high arches, tight Achilles/ calf muscles, running

**Treatment:** [^39^]
- **Conservative (90% resolve within 6-12 months):**
  - Plantar fascia-specific stretching exercises (most evidence-based)
  - Calf/Achilles stretching
  - Arch supports/orthotics
  - Heel cups/cushions
  - Night splints (maintains stretch during sleep)
  - NSAIDs for pain
  - Activity modification, weight loss if overweight
- **Refractory cases:**
  - Corticosteroid injection (short-term benefit; risk of fascia rupture, fat pad atrophy)
  - Extracorporeal shockwave therapy
  - Platelet-rich plasma injections
  - Surgery (plantar fascia release): Last resort; <5% need surgery

### 6.8 Achilles Tendinopathy

- **Most common in runners and middle-aged "weekend warriors"**
- **Types:** Mid-substance (2-6 cm above insertion) or insertional
- **Symptoms:** Morning stiffness, pain with activity, swelling, tenderness
- **Risk factors:** Sudden increase in training, tight calf muscles, flat feet, fluoroquinolone antibiotics, systemic corticosteroids

**Treatment:**
- **Conservative (mainstay):**
  - Relative rest (avoid aggravating activities but maintain gentle activity)
  - Eccentric strengthening exercises (Alfredson protocol — gold standard)
  - Heel raises in shoes
  - Calf stretching
  - NSAIDs for pain (not for healing)
  - Night splints
- **Refractory:**
  - Extracorporeal shockwave therapy
  - Platelet-rich plasma
  - Corticosteroid injection: **Generally avoided** due to tendon rupture risk (especially for intratendinous injection)
  - Surgery: For refractory cases >6 months

**Achilles Tendon Rupture:**
- Sudden "pop" with immediate pain, weakness, difficulty pushing off
- Thompson test (no plantarflexion with calf squeeze) — diagnostic
- Treatment: Surgical repair (younger, active patients) vs. functional bracing (older patients); both followed by structured rehabilitation

## 7. Fibromyalgia

### 7.1 Clinical Overview

Fibromyalgia is a chronic pain syndrome characterized by widespread musculoskeletal pain, fatigue, sleep disturbances, cognitive difficulties ("fibro fog"), and various somatic symptoms. It affects 2-4% of the population, predominantly women. The pathophysiology involves central sensitization (abnormal pain processing in the CNS) with alterations in neurotransmitters (serotonin, norepinephrine, dopamine, substance P). [^40^] [^41^]

**2016 ACR Revised Criteria:** [^40^]
- **Widespread pain index (WPI):** Count of painful areas (0-19)
- **Symptom severity (SS) scale:** Fatigue, waking unrefreshed, cognitive symptoms (0-3 each)
- **Diagnosis:** WPI >=7 AND SS >=5 OR WPI 4-6 AND SS >=9
- Symptoms present for >=3 months
- No other disorder that would explain the pain

### 7.2 Multimodal Treatment Approach

**No single treatment is effective alone; combination therapy is essential.** [^40^] [^41^]

### 7.3 Non-Pharmacologic Therapy

**Education and Self-Management:**
- Patient education about fibromyalgia as a real condition
- Self-management programs (arthritis self-management)
- Pacing activities; avoid "push-crash" cycles

**Exercise:** (Strong evidence — cornerstone of treatment)
- Low-impact aerobic exercise (walking, swimming, cycling)
- Gradual progression; start low, go slow
- Tai chi (strong evidence for benefit)
- Yoga, stretching
- Aquatic therapy

**Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT):** [^41^]
- Strong evidence (Grade A) for improvement in pain and disability
- Short and medium-term benefits
- Addresses catastrophizing, fear-avoidance, pain coping

**Sleep Hygiene:**
- Regular sleep schedule
- Sleep hygiene optimization
- Treat underlying sleep disorders (sleep apnea, restless legs)

**Other:**
- Physical therapy
- Occupational therapy
- Mindfulness-based stress reduction
- Biofeedback
- Acupuncture (some evidence)

### 7.4 Pharmacologic Therapy

**FDA-Approved Agents for Fibromyalgia:**

| Agent | Class | Dosing | Key AEs | Notes |
|-------|-------|--------|---------|-------|
| **Duloxetine** (Cymbalta) | SNRI | 30-60 mg daily | Nausea, dry mouth, constipation, dizziness, insomnia | Also treats comorbid depression/anxiety |
| **Milnacipran** (Savella) | SNRI | 12.5-100 mg BID | Nausea, headache, constipation, sweating | Titrate slowly |
| **Pregabalin** (Lyrica) | Alpha-2-delta ligand | 150-450 mg/day in 2-3 divided doses | Dizziness, somnolence, weight gain, peripheral edema | Also improves sleep; schedule V controlled substance |

**Other Commonly Used Agents (evidence-based):**

| Agent | Evidence | Notes |
|-------|----------|-------|
| **Amitriptyline** (tricyclic antidepressant) | Strong (Grade A) | 10-50 mg at bedtime; improves sleep and pain; anticholinergic side effects |
| **Cyclobenzaprine** (muscle relaxant) | Moderate | Similar to amitriptyline; 5-10 mg at bedtime |
| **Gabapentin** | Moderate | 300-2400 mg/day; similar to pregabalin but less studied |
| **Tramadol** | Limited | Short-term use for flares; avoid long-term |

**Not Recommended:**
- Opioids: Limited evidence; significant risks; not recommended for fibromyalgia
- NSAIDs: Not effective for fibromyalgia pain
- Corticosteroids: Not effective

### 7.5 Treatment Principles

- Start with lowest effective dose; titrate slowly
- Combine non-pharmacologic with pharmacologic therapy
- Address comorbid conditions (depression, anxiety, sleep disorders, IBS)
- Set realistic goals (pain reduction, improved function, not cure)
- Regular follow-up; adjust treatment as needed
- Consider referral to pain management or rheumatology for refractory cases

## 8. Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE)

### 8.1 Clinical Overview

SLE is a chronic, multisystem autoimmune disease characterized by immune dysregulation, autoantibody production, and immune complex deposition. It predominantly affects women (9:1 female:male ratio), especially during reproductive years. Disease course is characterized by flares and remissions. [^42^] [^43^]

**Key Autoantibodies:**
- **ANA:** Present in >95% (screening test; low specificity)
- **Anti-dsDNA:** Relatively specific; correlates with disease activity and lupus nephritis
- **Anti-Smith (Sm):** Highly specific for SLE (20-30% sensitivity)
- **Anti-Ro/SSA and Anti-La/SSB:** Associated with neonatal lupus, Sjogren's overlap
- **Antiphospholipid antibodies (aPL):** Lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin, anti-beta2-GPI; associated with thrombosis, pregnancy loss
- **Low complement (C3, C4):** Indicates active disease/consumption

### 8.2 Classification Criteria

**2019 EULAR/ACR Classification Criteria:** [^44^] [^45^]

**Entry Criterion:** Positive ANA (titer >=1:80 on HEp-2 cells) at least once

**Weighted Criteria (7 clinical + 3 immunologic domains):**

| Domain | Criteria | Weight |
|--------|----------|--------|
| **Constitutional** | Fever | 2 |
| **Hematologic** | Leukopenia | 3 |
| | Thrombocytopenia | 4 |
| | Autoimmune hemolysis | 4 |
| **Neuropsychiatric** | Delirium | 2 |
| | Psychosis | 3 |
| | Seizure | 5 |
| **Mucocutaneous** | Non-scarring alopecia | 2 |
| | Oral ulcers | 2 |
| | Subacute cutaneous/discoid lupus | 4 |
| | Acute cutaneous lupus | 6 |
| **Serosal** | Pleural/pericardial effusion | 5 |
| | Acute pericarditis | 6 |
| **Musculoskeletal** | Joint involvement | 6 |
| **Renal** | Proteinuria >0.5 g/day | 4 |
| | Active sediment | 4 |
| | Class II/V lupus nephritis | 8 |
| | Class III/IV lupus nephritis | 10 |
| **Antiphospholipid antibodies** | Positive | 2 |
| **Complement proteins** | Low C3 or C4 | 3 |
| | Low C3 AND C4 | 4 |
| **SLE-specific antibodies** | Anti-dsDNA | 6 |
| | Anti-Sm | 6 |

**Classification:** ANA positive + total score >=10

**Operating characteristics:** Sensitivity 96.1%, Specificity 93.4% (superior to ACR 1997 and SLICC 2012) [^44^]

### 8.3 Organ Involvement and Management

**Lupus Nephritis (LN):**
- Occurs in 50% of SLE patients; major cause of morbidity/mortality
- **Diagnosis:** Urinalysis, urine protein/creatinine ratio, serum creatinine, complement levels, anti-dsDNA; **renal biopsy for classification and treatment guidance**
- **ISN/RPS Classification:** Class I (minimal mesangial) to Class VI (advanced sclerosing)
- **Treatment (based on 2024 ACR Guidelines):** [^42^]
  - **Class III/IV (proliferative):** High-dose glucocorticoids + mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) or cyclophosphamide (CYC); consider belimumab, voclosporin addition
  - **Class V (membranous):** MMF + glucocorticoids
  - **Maintenance:** MMF or azathioprine + low-dose glucocorticoids
  - **Refractory:** Rituximab
  - **SGLT2 inhibitors** now recommended for proteinuria reduction in LN
  - **Monitor:** Blood pressure (goal <130/80), proteinuria, renal function, complement, anti-dsDNA

**Cutaneous Lupus:**
- Acute (malar rash), subacute cutaneous (SCLE — annular/papulosquamous), discoid (DLE — scarring)
- Treatment: Sun protection, topical corticosteroids, topical calcineurin inhibitors, antimalarials (first-line systemic), systemic corticosteroids for acute flares

**Neuropsychiatric SLE (NPSLE):**
- Headache, seizures, psychosis, cerebrovascular disease, cognitive dysfunction, mood disorders
- Exclude other causes; MRI, CSF analysis as indicated
- Treatment: Corticosteroids, immunosuppressants (cyclophosphamide for severe cases), anticoagulation if aPL-associated

**Hematologic:**
- Anemia (anemia of chronic disease, autoimmune hemolytic), leukopenia, thrombocytopenia
- Treatment: Immunosuppression for autoimmune cytopenias; thrombopoietin agonists for ITP

**Serositis:**
- Pleuritis, pericarditis
- Treatment: NSAIDs for mild; corticosteroids for severe/refractory

### 8.4 General SLE Management

**Hydroxychloroquine (HCQ):** [^42^] [^43^]
- **Recommended for ALL SLE patients unless contraindicated**
- Dose: 200-400 mg daily (<=5 mg/kg/day to minimize retinal toxicity)
- Benefits: Reduces flares, improves long-term survival, decreases organ damage, reduces thrombosis risk, improves lipid profile
- Monitoring: Baseline eye exam; annual screening after 5 years (earlier if high risk)
- **KEY drug for SLE — virtually all patients should be on HCQ**

**Glucocorticoids:**
- Minimize dose and duration; aim for <5-7.5 mg/day prednisone equivalent
- Use as bridge until immunosuppressants take effect
- High-dose pulses (methylprednisolone 500-1000 mg IV x 3 days) for severe organ involvement

**Immunosuppressants:**
- **Mycophenolate mofetil (MMF):** First-line for moderate-severe disease, lupus nephritis
- **Azathioprine:** Maintenance therapy, mild-moderate disease; safer in pregnancy
- **Cyclophosphamide:** Severe organ involvement (LN, CNS); gonadotoxic
- **Methotrexate:** Arthritis, cutaneous lupus
- **Belimumab (Benlysta):** Anti-BLyS; FDA-approved for SLE and LN; reduces flares; IV or SC
- **Anifrolumab (Saphnelo):** Anti-type I IFN receptor; FDA-approved for moderate-severe SLE
- **Rituximab:** Refractory disease (off-label)
- **Voclosporin:** For lupus nephritis (in combination with MMF)

**Anticoagulation:**
- APS with thrombosis: Warfarin (INR 2-3) or DOACs (controversial; warfarin preferred for arterial thrombosis)
- APS with pregnancy: LMWH + low-dose aspirin

### 8.5 Disease Activity Monitoring

- **Clinical assessment:** SLEDAI, BILAG, PGA (Physician Global Assessment)
- **Laboratory:** Complement levels, anti-dsDNA, CBC, creatinine, urinalysis
- **Target:** Remission or low disease activity; treat-to-target approach
- **Flare identification and management:** Early intervention

### 8.6 Pregnancy Considerations

**2024 ACR Guidelines and EULAR 2025 Recommendations:** [^42^] [^46^] [^47^]

**Preconception:**
- **Plan pregnancy during remission or low disease activity** (>=6 months preferred)
- **HCQ:** Continue — strongly recommended; reduces flares and improves pregnancy outcomes
- **Stop teratogenic medications:** Methotrexate (stop 3 months before), mycophenolate (stop 6 weeks before), cyclophosphamide
- **Transition to pregnancy-safe agents:** Azathioprine, tacrolimus, cyclosporine
- **Prednisone:** Keep <10-20 mg/day if possible

**Medications in Pregnancy:**

| Medication | Recommendation |
|------------|---------------|
| **Hydroxychloroquine** | Strongly recommended — continue |
| **Azathioprine** | Compatible — safe |
| **Tacrolimus/Cyclosporine** | Compatible — safe |
| **Prednisone** | Compatible — minimize dose |
| **Belimumab** | Limited data; may be used if needed for disease control |
| **Rituximab** | May be used for organ/life-threatening disease; avoid 2nd/3rd trimester if possible |
| **Methotrexate** | Contraindicated — stop before pregnancy |
| **Mycophenolate** | Contraindicated — stop 6 weeks before |
| **Cyclophosphamide** | Contraindicated — avoid |
| **Warfarin** | Contraindicated in 1st trimester; may be used in 2nd/3rd |
| **LMWH** | Safe — preferred for APS in pregnancy |
| **NSAIDs** | Avoid in 3rd trimester; may use earlier with caution |

**Pregnancy Complications:**
- Higher risk of preeclampsia, preterm birth, fetal loss, IUGR
- Risk stratification: aPL positivity, lupus nephritis, active disease increase risk
- **Low-dose aspirin** for preeclampsia prevention in high-risk patients (start preconception or by 16 weeks)
- Fetal echocardiography at 18-24 weeks if anti-Ro/SSA positive (neonatal lupus/CHB risk)
- Multidisciplinary care: Rheumatology, high-risk obstetrics, neonatology

**Neonatal Lupus:**
- Transient cutaneous rash, cytopenias, hepatitis
- **Congenital heart block (CHB):** Irreversible; risk 1-2% (higher if prior affected child); associated with anti-Ro/SSA and anti-La/SSB
- **HCQ reduces CHB risk** — continue throughout pregnancy

### 8.7 Cardiovascular Risk Management

- SLE patients have 2-10x increased risk of cardiovascular disease
- **Aggressive CV risk factor modification:** Blood pressure control, statins for hyperlipidemia, diabetes management, smoking cessation
- Annual CV risk assessment

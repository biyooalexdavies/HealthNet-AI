## 1. GERD (Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease) {#1-gerd}

### Clinical Overview

Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) is a chronic condition where stomach acid or contents flow backward into the esophagus due to transient lower esophageal sphincter (LES) relaxation or hypotensive LES. The refluxate irritates the esophageal lining, causing inflammation (esophagitis), and potentially leading to complications including Barrett's esophagus and esophageal adenocarcinoma. GERD is one of the most common gastrointestinal disorders, affecting approximately 20% of the Western population. [^91^]

**Pathophysiology**:
- Transient LES relaxation (primary mechanism)
- Hypotensive resting LES pressure
- Impaired esophageal acid clearance
- Delayed gastric emptying
- Hiatal hernia
- Obesity (increased intra-abdominal pressure)

### Guidelines & Evidence

**ACG Clinical Guidelines for GERD** recommend:
- Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) as the most effective therapy for healing esophagitis and relieving symptoms [^91^]
- Lifestyle modifications as adjunctive therapy
- Twice-daily PPI before meals for patients with incomplete response to once-daily therapy
- Surgical fundoplication for select patients with documented GERD who are intolerant of medical therapy or wish to discontinue long-term PPI use

**Diagnosis**:
- Empiric PPI trial (standard dose once daily for 4-8 weeks) in patients with classic symptoms
- Upper endoscopy (EGD) indicated for alarm features or patients unresponsive to empiric therapy
- 24-hour pH monitoring for patients with refractory symptoms to document acid reflux
- Impedance-pH testing to evaluate for non-acid reflux

### Differential Diagnosis

| Condition | Key Distinguishing Features |
|-----------|---------------------------|
| Eosinophilic esophagitis | Young male, dysphagia to solids, food impaction, normal pH testing |
| Achalasia | Progressive dysphagia to solids and liquids, regurgitation of undigested food, absent peristalsis on manometry |
| Gastric ulcer | Epigastric pain related to meals, may improve with antacids, diagnosed by endoscopy |
| Coronary artery disease | Chest pain with exertion, ECG changes, elevated troponins |
| Functional dyspepsia | Postprandial fullness, early satiety, epigastric pain, no retrosternal burning |
| Pill esophagitis | Sudden-onset odynophagia, history of medication ingestion (bisphosphonates, NSAIDs, doxycycline) |

### Red Flags (Alarm Features)

**These require immediate endoscopic evaluation:**
- Dysphagia or odynophagia
- Unintentional weight loss (>5% body weight)
- Gastrointestinal bleeding (melena, hematemesis, hematochezia)
- Iron-deficiency anemia
- Persistent vomiting
- New-onset symptoms in patients >60 years old
- Family history of upper GI malignancy or Barrett's esophagus
- Chest pain not clearly cardiac (after cardiac evaluation)

### Management

**Step-up approach:**
1. **Lifestyle modifications** (all patients):
   - Weight loss if overweight/obese (strongest evidence)
   - Elevate head of bed 6-8 inches
   - Avoid late meals (eat >3 hours before lying down)
   - Avoid trigger foods (fatty/fried foods, chocolate, peppermint, alcohol, coffee, citrus, tomato-based products)
   - Smoking cessation
   - Avoid tight clothing
   - Sleep on left side [^91^]

2. **Mild/intermittent symptoms**: Antacids or H2 blockers (famotidine, ranitidine alternatives)

3. **Moderate-severe symptoms**: PPI once daily 30-60 minutes before breakfast for 8 weeks
   - Omeprazole 20 mg, esomeprazole 20-40 mg, lansoprazole 30 mg, pantoprazole 40 mg, rabeprazole 20 mg

4. **Refractory GERD** (symptoms persist on once-daily PPI):
   - Switch to different PPI
   - Twice-daily PPI (before breakfast and dinner)
   - Evaluate for non-GERD causes (functional heartburn, rumination syndrome)
   - Impedance-pH testing on PPI therapy
   - Consider surgical evaluation (fundoplication, magnetic sphincter augmentation)

### Medications

| Drug Class | Examples | Dosing | Notes |
|-----------|----------|--------|-------|
| PPIs | Omeprazole | 20-40 mg daily | Most effective; take 30 min before meals |
| | Esomeprazole | 20-40 mg daily | |
| | Pantoprazole | 40 mg daily | Lower drug interaction potential |
| H2 blockers | Famotidine | 20-40 mg BID | Tolerance develops; use for mild symptoms |
| Antacids | Calcium carbonate | PRN | Rapid relief, short duration |
| Prokinetics | Metoclopramide | 5-10 mg QID | Limited efficacy; side effect concerns |
| Potassium-competitive acid blocker | Vonoprazan | 20 mg daily | Faster onset, longer duration |

**PPI Safety**: Long-term PPI use is generally safe. Associations with C. difficile infection, hypomagnesemia, vitamin B12 deficiency, and bone fractures exist but causality is uncertain. PPIs should be used at the lowest effective dose. [^89^]

### Special Populations

**Pregnancy**: Lifestyle modifications first-line; antacids and sucralfate are safe; H2 blockers (famotidine) preferred if pharmacotherapy needed; PPIs (omeprazole, lansoprazole) considered safe in pregnancy

**Pediatrics**: GERD common in infants but usually resolves by 12 months; thickened feeds, positioning; PPIs for erosive esophagitis only

**Elderly**: Higher risk of complications; maintain low threshold for endoscopy; PPIs generally well-tolerated

### Prevention

- Weight management
- Avoidance of trigger foods and late eating
- Smoking cessation
- Regular exercise
- Avoid excessive alcohol

## 2. Peptic Ulcer Disease {#2-peptic-ulcer-disease}

### Clinical Overview

Peptic ulcer disease (PUD) is characterized by discontinuation in the inner lining of the GI tract due to gastric acid secretion or pepsin, extending into the muscularis propria. It most commonly occurs in the stomach (gastric ulcer) and proximal duodenum (duodenal ulcer). The two major causes are *Helicobacter pylori* infection and NSAID use. [^88^]

**Key clinical features**:
- Gastric ulcer: epigastric pain within 15-30 minutes following a meal
- Duodenal ulcer: pain 2-3 hours after a meal, nocturnal pain, relief with food or antacids

### Guidelines & Evidence

**ACG Guidelines for PUD Management**:
- Testing for *H. pylori* is recommended in all patients with PUD [^88^]
- Eradication of *H. pylori* cures ulcer disease and prevents recurrence
- PPI-based triple or quadruple therapy is first-line
- Endoscopy is indicated for patients with alarm symptoms

**H. pylori Test-and-Treat Strategy**:
- Non-invasive testing: urea breath test or stool antigen test (preferred)
- Endoscopy with biopsy for patients with alarm features or those requiring EGD for other reasons
- Test of cure should be performed in all patients at least 4 weeks after completing therapy using urea breath test or stool antigen

### Differential Diagnosis

| Condition | Key Distinguishing Features |
|-----------|---------------------------|
| GERD | Retrosternal burning, regurgitation, worse with recumbency |
| Functional dyspepsia | Chronic epigastric pain without structural lesion, normal endoscopy |
| Gastric malignancy | Progressive symptoms, weight loss, persistent vomiting, anemia |
| Biliary colic | Right upper quadrant pain, radiates to back, after fatty meals |
| Pancreatitis | Severe epigastric pain radiating to back, elevated amylase/lipase |
| Mesenteric ischemia | Postprandial pain, food fear, weight loss, vascular risk factors |
| Cardiac ischemia | Chest pain with exertion, dyspnea, diaphoresis |

### Red Flags

- Severe abdominal pain with rigid abdomen (perforation)
- Hematemesis or melena (bleeding ulcer)
- Persistent vomiting (gastric outlet obstruction)
- Unexplained weight loss
- Severe anemia
- Syncope or orthostatic hypotension

### Management

**H. pylori Eradication Regimens** (14-day preferred): [^88^] [^94^]

| Regimen | Components | Duration |
|---------|-----------|----------|
| Bismuth quadruple therapy | Bismuth subsalicylate + tetracycline + metronidazole + PPI | 14 days |
| Concomitant therapy | PPI + amoxicillin + clarithromycin + metronidazole/tinidazole | 14 days |
| Clarithromycin triple | PPI + amoxicillin + clarithromycin | 14 days (only if clarithromycin resistance <15% and no prior macrolide) |
| Vonoprazan-based | Vonoprazan + amoxicillin | 14 days |

**NSAID-Related Ulcers**:
- Discontinue NSAIDs if possible
- PPI therapy (omeprazole 20-40 mg daily)
- If NSAIDs must be continued: use lowest effective dose + PPI or misoprostol
- Consider COX-2 selective NSAIDs with lower GI risk
- Test for *H. pylori* and eradicate if positive

**Bleeding Ulcer Management**:
- Endoscopic evaluation within 24 hours
- Endoscopic hemostasis: epinephrine injection + thermal coagulation or mechanical clips
- High-dose IV PPI (omeprazole 80 mg bolus + 8 mg/hr infusion or intermittent dosing)
- Forrest classification guides management:
  - IA (spurting vessel), IB (oozing): endoscopic therapy required
  - IIA (non-bleeding visible vessel): endoscopic therapy recommended
  - IIB (adherent clot): consider endoscopic therapy
  - IIC (flat pigmented spot): no endoscopic therapy
  - III (clean base): no endoscopic therapy

### Complications

**Bleeding** (most common): Hematemesis, melena, hematochezia; managed with endoscopic hemostasis + IV PPI [^89^]

**Perforation**: Sudden severe abdominal pain, rigid abdomen, free air on imaging; requires urgent surgical repair (usually laparoscopic) [^94^]

**Gastric outlet obstruction**: Early satiety, abdominal distention, vomiting; may require balloon dilatation or surgery [^94^]

**Penetration**: Ulcer extends into adjacent organ (pancreas most common)

### Medications

| Drug | Dosing | Indication |
|------|--------|------------|
| Omeprazole | 20-40 mg daily | Healing, maintenance |
| Esomeprazole | 20-40 mg daily | Healing, maintenance |
| Misoprostol | 200 mcg QID | Prevention of NSAID ulcers |
| Sucralfate | 1g QID | Ulcer healing |

### Special Populations

**Pregnancy**: Avoid bismuth subsalicylate; test-and-treat if *H. pylori* positive; PPIs and amoxicillin generally safe

**Elderly**: Higher bleeding risk with NSAIDs; avoid NSAIDs when possible; use PPI prophylaxis when NSAIDs necessary

**Pediatrics**: *H. pylori* testing only when PUD or gastric MALT lymphoma present

### Prevention

- Avoid unnecessary NSAIDs
- Eradicate *H. pylori* in all infected patients
- PPI prophylaxis for patients on chronic NSAIDs or dual antiplatelet therapy
- Smoking cessation
- Limit alcohol consumption

## 3. Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS) {#3-ibs}

### Clinical Overview

Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) is a functional gastrointestinal disorder characterized by recurrent abdominal pain associated with defecation or a change in bowel habits. It is diagnosed based on the **Rome IV criteria**: recurrent abdominal pain on average at least 1 day/week in the last 3 months, associated with two or more of: (1) related to defecation, (2) associated with change in stool frequency, (3) associated with change in stool form (appearance).

**Subtypes**:
- **IBS-C**: Constipation-predominant (>25% hard/lumpy stools, <25% loose/watery)
- **IBS-D**: Diarrhea-predominant (>25% loose/watery stools, <25% hard/lumpy)
- **IBS-M**: Mixed (both constipation and diarrhea)
- **IBS-U**: Unclassified

### Guidelines & Evidence

**ACG/AGA Guidelines for IBS Management**:
- Diagnosis based on Rome IV criteria after excluding alarm features
- Limited testing in patients without alarm features (CBC, CRP or fecal calprotectin, celiac serology in IBS-D)
- First-line: dietary modifications and lifestyle changes
- Second-line: pharmacotherapy based on IBS subtype
- Psychologic therapies (CBT, gut-directed hypnotherapy) effective

**Low FODMAP Diet**:
- Evidence supports a 2-6 week trial of low FODMAP (fermentable oligosaccharides, disaccharides, monosaccharides, and polyols) diet
- Reduction in IBS symptoms in approximately 50-80% of patients
- Should be guided by a trained dietitian
- Followed by systematic reintroduction to identify triggers

### Differential Diagnosis

| Condition | Key Distinguishing Features |
|-----------|---------------------------|
| Celiac disease | Positive tissue transglutaminase IgA, villous atrophy on biopsy |
| Inflammatory bowel disease | Bloody diarrhea, weight loss, elevated inflammatory markers, endoscopic findings |
| Microscopic colitis | Watery diarrhea in older women, diagnosed by biopsy |
| Small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO) | Positive breath test, bloating, response to antibiotics |
| Bile acid diarrhea | Post-cholecystectomy, positive SeHCAT test |
| Colorectal cancer | Age >50, weight loss, anemia, rectal bleeding, change in bowel habits |
| Thyroid dysfunction | Abnormal TSH, other systemic symptoms |
| Lactose intolerance | Positive breath test, symptoms with dairy |

### Red Flags

- Onset after age 50
- Unintentional weight loss
- Rectal bleeding or melena
- Iron-deficiency anemia
- Nocturnal symptoms (waking from sleep)
- Family history of colorectal cancer, IBD, or celiac disease
- Recent antibiotic use (consider C. difficile)
- Fever
- Progressive symptoms

### Management

**Stepwise approach**:

**Step 1 - Dietary and Lifestyle**:
- Low FODMAP diet trial (2-6 weeks with dietitian)
- Fiber supplementation (soluble fiber/psyllium preferred)
- Regular exercise
- Stress management
- Adequate hydration
- Avoid trigger foods

**Step 2 - Pharmacotherapy by Subtype**:

**IBS-C**:
- Osmotic laxatives (PEG)
- Guanylate cyclase-C agonists: linaclotide (290 mcg daily), plecanatide (3 mg daily)
- Chloride channel activator: lubiprostone (8 mcg BID)
- 5-HT4 agonist: prucalopride (2 mg daily)

**IBS-D**:
- Loperamide (2-4 mg PRN, max 16 mg/day)
- Rifaximin (550 mg TID for 14 days; non-absorbable antibiotic)
- Eluxadoline (75-100 mg BID) - avoid in patients without gallbladder
- Alosetron (0.5-1 mg BID) - restricted access due to ischemic colitis risk
- Bile acid sequestrants (cholestyramine) for bile acid diarrhea

**All IBS Subtypes**:
- Peppermint oil (IBS with predominant pain/bloating)
- Antispasmodics (hyoscyamine, dicyclomine) for cramping
- Low-dose tricyclic antidepressants (amitriptyline 10-50 mg QHS) for pain
- SSRIs (fluoxetine) for pain and associated anxiety/depression
- Psychological therapies (CBT, gut-directed hypnotherapy)

**Step 3 - Referral**:
- Gastroenterology for refractory symptoms
- Consider SIBO testing, anorectal manometry
- Pain management for central sensitization

### Medications

| Drug | Dose | Indication | Notes |
|------|------|------------|-------|
| Linaclotide | 290 mcg daily | IBS-C | Secretagogue; take 30 min before first meal |
| Plecanatide | 3 mg daily | IBS-C | Once daily |
| Lubiprostone | 8 mcg BID | IBS-C | Take with food |
| Rifaximin | 550 mg TID x 14 days | IBS-D | Non-absorbable antibiotic |
| Eluxadoline | 75-100 mg BID | IBS-D | Contraindicated without gallbladder |
| Loperamide | 2 mg PRN | IBS-D | Do not exceed 16 mg/day |
| Amitriptyline | 10-50 mg QHS | Pain | Start low, titrate slowly |
| PSyllium | 3.5 g daily | All types | Soluble fiber preferred |

### Special Populations

**Pregnancy**: Dietary management first; avoid most IBS-specific medications; loperamide may be used short-term

**Pediatrics**: Rome IV criteria apply with age-appropriate modifications; emphasize dietary and psychological approaches

**Elderly**: Consider medication side effects; avoid anticholinergics; monitor for medication interactions

### Prevention

- Regular physical activity
- Stress management techniques
- Consistent sleep schedule
- Dietary trigger identification and avoidance
- Maintaining healthy gut microbiome

## 4. Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD) {#4-ibd}

### Clinical Overview

Inflammatory bowel disease encompasses two main conditions: Crohn's disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC). Both are chronic, immune-mediated inflammatory conditions of the gastrointestinal tract with periods of active disease (flares) and remission.

**Crohn's Disease vs. Ulcerative Colitis**:

| Feature | Crohn's Disease | Ulcerative Colitis |
|---------|-----------------|-------------------|
| Distribution | Anywhere from mouth to anus; skip lesions | Colon only; continuous from rectum |
| Depth | Transmural (full thickness) | Mucosal and submucosal |
| Rectal sparing | Common | Rare (always involves rectum) |
| Granulomas | Present in ~50% | Absent |
| Fistulas/strictures | Common | Rare |
| Perianal disease | Common | Rare |
| Toxic megacolon | Less common | More common |
| Colorectal cancer risk | Increased (with colonic involvement) | Increased |

**Montreal Classification for CD**:
- Age at diagnosis: A1 (<17 years), A2 (17-40 years), A3 (>40 years)
- Location: L1 (ileal), L2 (colonic), L3 (ileocolonic), L4 (isolated upper GI)
- Behavior: B1 (non-stricturing, non-penetrating), B2 (stricturing), B3 (penetrating/fistulizing), p (perianal modifier)

**Montreal Classification for UC**:
- Extent: E1 (ulcerative proctitis), E2 (left-sided), E3 (extensive/pancolitis)
- Severity: S0-S3 (remission to severe)

### Guidelines & Evidence

**AGA 2024 Living Guideline for Moderate-to-Severe UC**: [^87^] [^90^] [^92^]
- Recommends advanced therapies (biologics, small molecules) over step-up approach after 5-ASA failure
- Categorizes drugs by efficacy buckets:
  - **Higher efficacy**: Infliximab, vedolizumab, ozanimod, etrasimod, upadacitinib, risankizumab, guselkumab
  - **Intermediate efficacy**: Golimumab, ustekinumab, tofacitinib, filgotinib, mirikizumab
  - **Lower efficacy**: Adalimumab
- Recommends combination therapy (TNF + immunomodulator) over monotherapy for TNF agents
- Recommends against thiopurine monotherapy for induction
- Suggests stopping 5-ASA when escalating to advanced therapies in moderate-severe UC

**ACG 2024 Clinical Guideline for Crohn's Disease**: [^93^]
- Against requiring failure of conventional therapy before initiating advanced therapy
- Oral mesalamine NOT recommended for induction or maintenance in CD
- Budesonide 9 mg daily for induction of ileocecal CD
- Anti-TNF agents (infliximab, adalimumab, certolizumab) for moderate-severe CD
- Combination infliximab + immunomodulator superior to monotherapy
- Mirikizumab and guselkumab now recommended for CD
- Biosimilar infliximab, adalimumab, and ustekinumab are effective
- Upadacitinib recommended for anti-TNF-exposed patients

### Differential Diagnosis

| Condition | Key Distinguishing Features |
|-----------|---------------------------|
| Infectious colitis | Acute onset, positive stool culture, resolves with treatment |
| C. difficile infection | Recent antibiotic use, positive C. difficile toxin |
| Ischemic colitis | Acute onset in elderly/vascular disease, segmental colitis |
| Microscopic colitis | Watery diarrhea, normal endoscopy, biopsy diagnosis |
| Celiac disease | Positive celiac serology, villous atrophy |
| Colon cancer | Focal lesion, older age, weight loss, anemia |
| Behcet's disease | Oral/genital ulcers, pathergy, systemic vasculitis |
| NSAID enteropathy | History of NSAID use, diaphragm-like strictures |

### Red Flags

- Severe abdominal pain with bloody diarrhea >6 stools/day
- Fever, tachycardia, hypotension
- Toxic megacolon (colonic dilation >6 cm with systemic toxicity)
- Severe dehydration
- Bowel obstruction symptoms
- Perianal abscess/fistula
- Massive GI bleeding
- Perforation (free air)
- Deep vein thrombosis (IBD is prothrombotic)

### Management

**Mild UC**:
- 5-ASA (mesalamine) enemas for proctitis
- Oral 5-ASA for left-sided or extensive UC
- Topical corticosteroids (budesonide foam) if 5-ASA intolerant

**Moderate-Severe UC**:
- Systemic corticosteroids (prednisone 40-60 mg) for induction
- Advanced therapies for steroid-sparing maintenance:
  - Anti-TNF: Infliximab, adalimumab, golimumab
  - Anti-integrin: Vedolizumab (gut-selective)
  - Anti-IL-12/23: Ustekinumab
  - Anti-IL-23: Risankizumab, guselkumab, mirikizumab
  - JAK inhibitors: Tofacitinib, upadacitinib, filgotinib
  - S1P modulators: Ozanimod, etrasimod

**Crohn's Disease Management**:
- Mild ileocecal: Budesonide 9 mg
- Moderate-severe: Anti-TNF (infliximab, adalimumab) + immunomodulator
- Fistulizing CD: Infliximab (most evidence), may add antibiotics
- Postoperative prophylaxis: Anti-TNF in high-risk patients

**Monitoring**:
- Fecal calprotectin to differentiate inflammatory from non-inflammatory disease [^93^]
- Routine colonoscopic surveillance for CRC in colonic CD
- Drug levels and anti-drug antibodies for biologic optimization
- CRP, ESR for disease activity monitoring
- Endoscopic assessment at 6-12 months post-surgery

### Medications

| Drug | Class | Dosing | Notes |
|------|-------|--------|-------|
| Infliximab | Anti-TNF | IV at weeks 0, 2, 6, then q8wk | First-line for moderate-severe |
| Adalimumab | Anti-TNF | SC every 2 weeks | Maintenance |
| Vedolizumab | Anti-integrin | IV at weeks 0, 2, 6, then q8wk | Gut-selective |
| Ustekinumab | Anti-IL-12/23 | IV induction, SC q8wk | Effective post-TNF failure |
| Risankizumab | Anti-IL-23 | IV induction, SC maintenance | Higher efficacy |
| Guselkumab | Anti-IL-23 | SC | New for CD |
| Mirikizumab | Anti-IL-23 | SC | New for CD |
| Upadacitinib | JAK inhibitor | 45 mg induction, 15-30 mg maintenance | Oral |
| Tofacitinib | JAK inhibitor | 10 mg BID induction, 5 mg BID maintenance | For UC |
| Azathioprine | Immunomodulator | 2-2.5 mg/kg/day | TPMT testing required |
| Methotrexate | Immunomodulator | 25 mg SC weekly | Folate supplementation |
| Prednisone | Corticosteroid | 40-60 mg daily | Not for long-term |
| Budesonide | Corticosteroid | 9 mg daily | Ileal release |

### Special Populations

**Pregnancy**: Most biologics (anti-TNF, vedolizumab, ustekinumab) considered safe; active disease poses greater risk than medications; discontinue methotrexate; plan delivery timing with drug levels

**Pediatrics**: Similar treatment principles; growth monitoring essential; earlier use of biologics in severe disease

**Elderly**: Higher infection risk; lower threshold for PJP prophylaxis; avoid thiopurines in elderly

### Prevention

- Smoking cessation (especially for CD - smoking worsens CD)
- Regular CRC surveillance in colonic disease
- Bone health monitoring (vitamin D, calcium)
- Vaccination before starting immunosuppression (pneumococcal, influenza, hepatitis B, shingles)
- Thromboprophylaxis during flares
- Nutritional optimization

## 5. Acute and Chronic Diarrhea {#5-diarrhea}

### Clinical Overview

Diarrhea is defined as passage of loose or watery stools, typically >3 times per day. Acute diarrhea lasts <14 days, persistent 14-30 days, and chronic >4 weeks. The differential is broad, encompassing infectious, inflammatory, malabsorptive, secretory, motility-related, and drug-induced causes.

### Guidelines & Evidence

**Infectious Diarrhea**: [^98^]
- Most infectious diarrhea is self-limited and does not require antibiotics
- Stool culture for bloody diarrhea, severe illness, immunocompromised, or recent travel
- Ova and parasites for travel to endemic areas or persistent diarrhea

**IDSA/SHEA Guidelines for C. difficile Infection**: [^95^] [^102^]
- Fidaxomicin now preferred first-line over vancomycin for initial episode
- Metronidazole only if fidaxomicin/vancomycin unavailable
- Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) for recurrent CDI after failure of antibiotics
- Bezlotoxumab (anti-toxin B monoclonal antibody) adjunctive for recurrence prevention

**Classification**:
- **Non-severe CDI**: WBC <15,000 and Cr <1.5 mg/dL
- **Severe CDI**: WBC >15,000 or Cr >1.5 mg/dL
- **Severe-complicated/fulminant**: Hypotension, shock, ileus, toxic megacolon

### Differential Diagnosis

**Acute Diarrhea**:
| Condition | Key Features |
|-----------|-------------|
| Viral (norovirus, rotavirus) | Acute, self-limited, vomiting prominent, community outbreaks |
| Bacterial (Campylobacter, Salmonella, Shigella, E. coli) | Bloody diarrhea, fever, foodborne, travel-related |
| C. difficile | Recent antibiotics, healthcare-associated, recurrent |
| Protozoal (Giardia, Cryptosporidium) | Persistent >2 weeks, travel-related, immunocompromised |
| Food poisoning | Short incubation, multiple affected individuals |
| Medication-related | Recent medication change, antibiotics, metformin, magnesium |

**Chronic Diarrhea**:
| Condition | Key Features |
|-----------|-------------|
| IBS-D | Rome IV criteria, no alarm features |
| IBD | Bloody diarrhea, weight loss, elevated inflammatory markers |
| Celiac disease | Positive serology, villous atrophy |
| Microscopic colitis | Watery diarrhea, normal endoscopy, biopsy diagnosis |
| SIBO | Bloating, positive breath test |
| Bile acid diarrhea | Post-cholecystectomy, watery diarrhea |
| Chronic pancreatitis | Steatorrhea, history of pancreatitis |
| Hyperthyroidism | Elevated TSH, other thyrotoxic symptoms |
| Lactose intolerance | Symptoms with dairy, positive breath test |
| Colorectal cancer | Age >50, weight loss, anemia, change in bowel habits |

### Red Flags

- Bloody diarrhea
- High fever (>38.5 C)
- Severe dehydration (orthostasis, syncope)
- Severe abdominal pain
- Altered mental status
- Immunocompromised state
- Recent antibiotics (C. difficile risk)
- Travel to high-risk areas
- Persistent diarrhea >4 weeks
- Nocturnal diarrhea (suggests organic cause)
- Weight loss

### Management

**Acute Infectious Diarrhea**:
- Oral rehydration is primary therapy
- Loperamide (4 mg then 2 mg after each loose stool, max 16 mg/day) for non-bloody, non-febrile diarrhea
- Bismuth subsalicylate (Pepto-Bismol) as alternative
- Avoid antimotility agents in bloody diarrhea or suspected C. difficile
- Antibiotics NOT routinely indicated for uncomplicated acute diarrhea

**When to use antibiotics for acute diarrhea**:
- Severe traveler's diarrhea
- Dysentery (bloody diarrhea with fever)
- Cholera
- Immunocompromised patients
- Sepsis or severe illness

**Traveler's Diarrhea Treatment**: [^120^]

| Severity | Treatment |
|----------|-----------|
| Mild | No antibiotics; loperamide or bismuth subsalicylate |
| Moderate | Azithromycin 500 mg daily x 3 days OR 1000 mg single dose; fluoroquinolone alternative |
| Severe/Dysentery | Azithromycin preferred; add loperamide |

**Specific Pathogens**:
- **Campylobacter**: Clarithromycin 250-500 mg BID for 5-7 days if severe [^98^]
- **Giardia**: Metronidazole 400 mg TID for 7-10 days [^98^]
- **Entamoeba histolytica**: Metronidazole 800 mg q8h for 5 days, then luminal agent
- **Cryptosporidium**: Nitazoxanide for immunocompromised

**C. difficile Management**: [^95^] [^102^]

| Classification | First-Line Treatment |
|---------------|---------------------|
| Initial episode (non-severe or severe) | Fidaxomicin 200 mg BID x 10 days (preferred) OR Vancomycin 125 mg QID x 10 days |
| First recurrence | Fidaxomicin 200 mg BID x 10 days OR Extended-pulsed fidaxomicin |
| Multiple recurrences | Fidaxomicin OR Vancomycin taper/pulse + FMT |
| Severe-complicated | Vancomycin 500 mg Q6H PO/NG +/- rectal + IV metronidazole 500 mg TID |

**Chronic Diarrhea Workup**:
1. CBC, CMP, CRP/ESR, TSH, celiac serology (tTG-IgA with total IgA)
2. Stool studies: calprotectin, lactoferrin, C. difficile toxin, O&P, culture
3. Fecal fat or elastase if malabsorption suspected
4. Colonoscopy with biopsies if red flags or abnormal screening
5. Breath testing for SIBO or lactose intolerance
6. SeHCAT scan or serum C4 for bile acid diarrhea
7. CT abdomen if structural cause suspected

### Medications

| Drug | Dose | Indication |
|------|------|------------|
| Fidaxomicin | 200 mg BID x 10 days | C. difficile (first-line) |
| Vancomycin (oral) | 125 mg QID x 10 days | C. difficile (alternative) |
| Metronidazole | 500 mg TID x 10-14 days | C. difficile (if others unavailable) |
| Azithromycin | 500 mg daily x 3 days | Traveler's diarrhea |
| Loperamide | 4 mg, then 2 mg PRN | Symptomatic control |
| Bismuth subsalicylate | 524 mg q30-60 min | Symptomatic control |
| Nitazoxanide | 500 mg BID x 3 days | Protozoal infections |

### Special Populations

**Pregnancy**: Avoid bismuth subsalicylate in 3rd trimester; avoid fluoroquinolones; azithromycin preferred for traveler's diarrhea; oral rehydration is primary therapy

**Pediatrics**: Higher dehydration risk; ORS essential; avoid antimotility agents in young children; avoid bismuth in viral illness (Reye's syndrome risk)

**Elderly**: Higher C. difficile risk; increased dehydration risk; lower threshold for hospitalization

**Immunocompromised**: Broader differential; consider opportunistic infections; lower threshold for diagnostic testing and treatment

### Prevention

- Hand hygiene (soap and water for C. difficile)
- Safe food and water practices when traveling
- Vaccination (rotavirus for infants, cholera for high-risk travel)
- Probiotics may reduce antibiotic-associated diarrhea (modest evidence)
- Judicious antibiotic use
- Avoid unnecessary PPI use (C. difficile risk factor)

## 6. Constipation {#6-constipation}

### Clinical Overview

Constipation is defined by infrequent bowel movements (<3 per week), hard or lumpy stools, straining, sensation of incomplete evacuation, or anorectal obstruction. It is classified as primary (functional/idiopathic) or secondary (due to medications, metabolic disorders, neurologic disease, or structural abnormalities).

**Primary Constipation Subtypes**:
- Normal transit constipation
- Slow transit constipation
- Defecatory disorders (pelvic floor dysfunction)

**Common Secondary Causes**:
- Medications: opioids, anticholinergics, antidepressants, calcium channel blockers, iron supplements, antacids
- Metabolic: hypothyroidism, hypercalcemia, diabetes mellitus, pregnancy
- Neurologic: Parkinson's disease, multiple sclerosis, spinal cord injury
- Structural: colorectal cancer, strictures, rectocele

### Guidelines & Evidence

**2023 AGA/ACG Joint Guideline for Chronic Idiopathic Constipation (CIC)**: [^96^] [^97^] [^101^]
- Systematic review of 10 pharmacologic treatments
- Strong recommendations (moderate certainty): PEG, sodium picosulfate, linaclotide, plecanatide, prucalopride
- Conditional recommendations (low certainty): fiber, lactulose, senna, magnesium oxide, lubiprostone

**Stepwise treatment approach**:
1. Lifestyle modifications + fiber
2. Osmotic laxatives (PEG, lactulose, magnesium oxide)
3. Stimulant laxatives as rescue or short-term (bisacodyl, senna, sodium picosulfate)
4. Prescription medications (secretagogues or 5-HT4 agonists)

### Differential Diagnosis

| Condition | Key Distinguishing Features |
|-----------|---------------------------|
| IBS-C | Abdominal pain, Rome IV criteria, bloating |
| Slow transit constipation | Marked infrequency, no urge to defecate, delayed colonic transit study |
| Pelvic floor dysfunction (dyssynergic defecation) | Excessive straining, digital evacuation, paradoxical anal contraction on manometry |
| Hypothyroidism | Fatigue, weight gain, cold intolerance, elevated TSH |
| Hypercalcemia | Polyuria, polydipsia, confusion, elevated calcium |
| Colorectal cancer | Change in bowel habits, weight loss, anemia, rectal bleeding |
| Opioid-induced constipation | Recent opioid use, refractory to standard laxatives |
| Celiac disease | Bloating, diarrhea, iron deficiency, positive serology |
| Diabetes-related | Autonomic neuropathy, gastroparesis |

### Red Flags

- New-onset constipation in elderly (>50 years)
- Unintentional weight loss
- Rectal bleeding or melena
- Iron-deficiency anemia
- Family history of colorectal cancer or IBD
- Sudden change in bowel habits
- Severe abdominal pain or distension
- Fecal vomiting (complete obstruction)

### Management

**Step 1 - Lifestyle Modifications**:
- Adequate fluid intake (1.5-2 L/day unless contraindicated)
- Regular physical activity
- Fiber supplementation (psyllium 3.5 g daily) - start low and titrate up
- Establish regular toilet routine, especially after meals
- Proper positioning (footstool to elevate knees)
- Respond to urge promptly

**Step 2 - Osmotic Laxatives**:
- PEG 3350 (Miralax): 17 g daily in 8 oz water (strong recommendation) [^97^]
- Lactulose: 15-30 mL daily (conditional recommendation)
- Magnesium oxide: 1.5-2 g daily (conditional recommendation)

**Step 3 - Stimulant Laxatives** (rescue or short-term):
- Bisacodyl: 5-10 mg PRN or daily (strong recommendation for short-term/rescue) [^101^]
- Senna: 8.6-17.2 mg at bedtime (conditional recommendation)
- Sodium picosulfate: 5-10 mg daily (strong recommendation)

**Step 4 - Prescription Medications**: [^96^]

| Drug | Class | Dose | Notes |
|------|-------|------|-------|
| Linaclotide | Guanylate cyclase-C agonist | 145 mcg daily (CIC) | Strong recommendation; take 30 min before first meal |
| Plecanatide | Guanylate cyclase-C agonist | 3 mg daily | Strong recommendation |
| Prucalopride | 5-HT4 agonist | 2 mg daily | Strong recommendation |
| Lubiprostone | Chloride channel activator | 24 mcg BID | Conditional recommendation; take with food |

**Opioid-Induced Constipation (OIC)**:
- Standard laxatives often insufficient
- PEG plus stimulant laxative first-line
- Methylnaltrexone (peripheral opioid antagonist): 8-12 mg SC every other day for patients with advanced illness
- Naloxegol: 25 mg daily (opioid antagonist, peripherally acting)
- Naldemedine: 0.2 mg daily
- Lubiprostone may be effective for OIC

**Pelvic Floor Dysfunction**:
- Biofeedback therapy (first-line)
- Pelvic floor physical therapy
- Digital rectal examination findings: paradoxical anal contraction
- Anorectal manometry to confirm diagnosis

### Medications

| Drug | Dose | Class | Recommendation |
|------|------|-------|---------------|
| PEG 3350 | 17 g daily | Osmotic laxative | Strong |
| Linaclotide | 145 mcg daily | Secretagogue | Strong |
| Plecanatide | 3 mg daily | Secretagogue | Strong |
| Prucalopride | 2 mg daily | Prokinetic | Strong |
| Sodium picosulfate | 5-10 mg daily | Stimulant laxative | Strong |
| Lubiprostone | 24 mcg BID | Secretagogue | Conditional |
| Lactulose | 15-30 mL daily | Osmotic laxative | Conditional |
| Senna | 8.6-17.2 mg | Stimulant laxative | Conditional |
| Bisacodyl | 5-10 mg | Stimulant laxative | Strong (rescue) |
| Magnesium oxide | 1.5-2 g daily | Osmotic laxative | Conditional |
| Psyllium | 3.5 g daily | Fiber | Conditional |

### Special Populations

**Pregnancy**: Diet and exercise first; PEG, lactulose, and docusate are generally safe; avoid stimulant laxatives in 1st trimester if possible

**Pediatrics**: PEG 3350 first-line; lactulose alternative; rectal disimpaction if needed; maintain adequate hydration

**Elderly**: Higher prevalence; medication review essential; avoid magnesium-containing laxatives in renal impairment; monitor for electrolyte disturbances

**Renal impairment**: Avoid magnesium-containing products; PEG generally safe; consult for dosing adjustments

### Prevention

- Adequate dietary fiber (25-35 g/day)
- Adequate hydration
- Regular physical activity
- Timely response to urge to defecate
- Avoid unnecessary medications that cause constipation
- Probiotics may provide modest benefit

## 7. Liver Disease {#7-liver-disease}

### Clinical Overview

This section covers the major liver diseases: viral hepatitis (A, B, C), NAFLD/NASH, alcohol-related liver disease, cirrhosis and its complications (hepatic encephalopathy, ascites, spontaneous bacterial peritonitis).

---

### 7A. Hepatitis A

**Clinical Overview**: Acute, self-limiting viral hepatitis transmitted via fecal-oral route. No chronic infection. Incubation period 15-50 days.

**Diagnosis**: Anti-HAV IgM (acute infection), anti-HAV IgG (past infection or immunity)

**Management**: Supportive care; self-limiting in most cases

**Prevention**: 
- HAV vaccine (inactivated): 2-dose series at 0 and 6-12 months
- Post-exposure prophylaxis: HAV vaccine + immune globulin within 2 weeks of exposure
- Indicated for travelers to endemic areas, men who have sex with men, persons with chronic liver disease, food handlers

---

### 7B. Hepatitis B

**Clinical Overview**: HBV is transmitted via blood, sexual contact, and perinatal routes. Chronic infection develops in 5-10% of adults but up to 90% of infected infants. It is a leading cause of cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) worldwide. [^114^] [^115^]

**Phases of Chronic HBV**:
- **Immune tolerant**: HBeAg-positive, high HBV DNA, normal ALT, minimal inflammation
- **Immune active (HBeAg-positive)**: Elevated ALT, high HBV DNA, active inflammation
- **Inactive carrier (HBeAg-negative, anti-HBe-positive)**: Low HBV DNA, normal ALT
- **HBeAg-negative chronic hepatitis**: Elevated ALT, moderate HBV DNA, active disease
- **Resolved**: HBsAg-negative, anti-HBc-positive

**Diagnosis**:
- HBsAg: current infection
- Anti-HBs: immunity (vaccination or recovery)
- Anti-HBc: past or current infection (IgM = acute, IgG = past/chronic)
- HBeAg: active viral replication (high infectivity)
- HBV DNA: viral load for treatment monitoring

**Screening**: One-time screening for all adults (CDC/USPSTF 2023); screen all pregnant women; at-risk populations

**AASLD Guidelines for Treatment** (2018/2024 update): [^114^] [^115^]

| Parameter | Recommendation |
|-----------|---------------|
| Immune-active CHB | Treat (Peg-IFN, entecavir, or tenofovir) |
| Compensated cirrhosis + HBV DNA >2,000 IU/mL | Treat regardless of ALT |
| Decompensated cirrhosis | Treat indefinitely regardless of HBV DNA |
| Immune-tolerant | Monitor; consider treatment if age >40 or family history of HCC |
| HBeAg-positive with seroconversion | Continue treatment 12 months post-seroconversion |
| HBeAg-negative | Long-term treatment required |

**Preferred Therapies**:
- Tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF) 300 mg daily
- Tenofovir alafenamide (TAF) 25 mg daily (preferred over TDF - improved renal and bone safety)
- Entecavir 0.5 mg daily (naive) or 1 mg daily (lamivudine-resistant)
- Peg-IFN alfa-2a 180 mcg SC weekly (finite therapy; HBeAg/ HBsAg loss possible)

**Monitoring on Treatment**:
- HBV DNA every 3 months until undetectable, then every 3-6 months
- ALT every 3-6 months
- HBeAg/anti-HBe every 6 months (if HBeAg-positive)
- Renal function annually (more frequently on TDF)
- HCC surveillance: ultrasound +/- AFP every 6 months in at-risk patients

**Prevention**:
- HBV vaccine (3-dose series: 0, 1, 6 months)
- Hepatitis B immune globulin (HBIG) for post-exposure prophylaxis
- Screen all pregnant women; antiviral prophylaxis (tenofovir) in 3rd trimester if HBV DNA >200,000 IU/mL
- All infants receive birth dose vaccine + HBIG if mother is HBsAg-positive

---

### 7C. Hepatitis C

**Clinical Overview**: HCV is transmitted via blood-to-blood contact. Acute infection is usually asymptomatic. Chronic infection develops in 75-85% and can lead to cirrhosis, HCC, and liver failure. It is now a curable disease with direct-acting antivirals (DAAs). [^100^] [^121^]

**Diagnosis**:
- Anti-HCV antibody (screening test)
- HCV RNA PCR (confirms active infection)
- HCV genotype (less important with pan-genotypic regimens)

**Screening**: One-time screening for all adults 18-79 years (CDC/USPSTF 2020); risk-based screening for ongoing risk factors

**AASLD/IDSA Guidelines for DAA Therapy**: [^121^]

**Pangenotypic Regimens**:

| Regimen | Duration | Population | Notes |
|---------|----------|------------|-------|
| Glecaprevir/Pibrentasvir (GLE/PIB) | 8 weeks | Treatment-naive, no cirrhosis | 3 tablets once daily with food |
| Glecaprevir/Pibrentasvir (GLE/PIB) | 8 weeks | Treatment-naive, compensated cirrhosis | 3 tablets once daily with food |
| Sofosbuvir/Velpatasvir (SOF/VEL) | 12 weeks | Treatment-naive, no cirrhosis | 1 tablet daily |
| Sofosbuvir/Velpatasvir (SOF/VEL) | 12 weeks | Treatment-naive, compensated cirrhosis | 1 tablet daily |

**Decompensated Cirrhosis (CTP B or C)**:
- SOF/VEL + low-dose ribavirin x 12 weeks OR
- SOF/VEL x 24 weeks (if ribavirin contraindicated)
- DO NOT use protease inhibitors (GLE, VOX) in decompensated cirrhosis

**DAA Failure (Retreatment)**:
- SOF/VEL/VOX (Vosevi) x 12 weeks for most patients
- GLE/PIB + SOF + ribavirin x 16 weeks for GLE/PIB failures

**Simplified Treatment Eligibility** (2023 AASLD guidance): [^121^]
- Chronic HCV with or without compensated cirrhosis
- Treatment-naive
- Not on hemodialysis
- No decompensated cirrhosis
- No significant drug-drug interactions

**Monitoring**:
- HCV RNA at 4 weeks during treatment
- SVR12 (undetectable HCV RNA 12 weeks after treatment completion) = cure
- CBC, CMP, LFTs at baseline and week 4
- Screen for HBV before treatment (risk of HBV reactivation)

**Key Points**:
- >95% cure rates with modern DAAs
- Genotype testing not required for GLE/PIB
- Check drug-drug interactions (hep-druginteractions.org)
- No contraindication with renal impairment for GLE/PIB
- Screen for hepatocellular carcinoma if cirrhosis present

**Prevention**:
- No vaccine available
- Harm reduction for injection drug users
- Blood supply screening
- Safe injection practices
- Do not share personal items (razors, toothbrushes)

---

### 7D. NAFLD/NASH

**Clinical Overview**: Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) is the most common chronic liver disease globally, strongly associated with metabolic syndrome, obesity, and type 2 diabetes. NAFL encompasses simple steatosis; NASH (nonalcoholic steatohepatitis) includes inflammation and hepatocellular injury with or without fibrosis. "At-risk NASH" refers to NASH with stage >=2 fibrosis. [^104^]

**Diagnosis**:
- Evidence of hepatic steatosis on imaging or histology
- Exclusion of significant alcohol consumption
- Exclusion of other causes of hepatic steatosis

**Non-invasive Assessment of Fibrosis** (AASLD 2023 Practice Guidance): [^104^]

| Tool | Cutoff | Interpretation |
|------|--------|---------------|
| FIB-4 | <1.3 | Low risk; follow in primary care |
| FIB-4 | 1.3-2.67 | Indeterminate; secondary testing |
| FIB-4 | >2.67 | High risk; refer to hepatology |
| VCTE (FibroScan) | <8 kPa | Exclude advanced fibrosis |
| VCTE | 8-12 kPa | Indeterminate |
| VCTE | >12 kPa | Advanced fibrosis/cirrhosis |
| ELF test | >=11.3 | Predicts liver-related events |

**Note**: In patients >65 years, use FIB-4 cutoff >2.0. FIB-4 not validated in patients <35 years.

**Management**: [^104^]
- **Weight loss**: 5% improves steatosis; 7-10% improves NASH; >10% improves fibrosis
- **Diet**: Mediterranean diet; limit carbohydrates and saturated fat; high fiber
- **Exercise**: At least 150 minutes/week moderate-intensity; benefits independent of weight loss
- **Coffee**: >=3 cups daily associated with less advanced liver disease
- **Alcohol**: Avoid entirely
- **Pharmacotherapy**: Resmetirom (thyromimetic) approved 2024 for NASH with fibrosis; pioglitazone, vitamin E (non-diabetic NASH) have evidence
- **Bariatric surgery**: For patients meeting metabolic surgery criteria; resolves NASH in majority
- **Liver transplant**: For decompensated NASH cirrhosis

**HCC Surveillance**: Ultrasound every 6 months in patients with cirrhosis

---

### 7E. Alcohol-Related Liver Disease (ALD)

**Clinical Overview**: Spectrum ranges from hepatic steatosis (fatty liver) to alcohol-associated hepatitis (AH) to cirrhosis. AH is an acute, severe form with high mortality. [^122^] [^129^]

**Alcohol-Associated Hepatitis (AH)**:
- Clinical diagnosis: recent heavy alcohol use, jaundice, tender hepatomegaly
- Labs: elevated bilirubin, elevated AST:ALT ratio typically >2:1 (both <300 IU/mL in pure AH)
- Maddrey's Discriminant Function (MDF) >=32 = severe AH
- MELD score >20 = severe AH
- Lille score (after 7 days of steroids): >0.45 = non-responder

**ACG 2023 Guideline Recommendations**: [^122^]

| Recommendation | Strength |
|---------------|----------|
| Prednisolone 40 mg/day for severe AH (MELD >20) without contraindications | Strong |
| Against pentoxifylline for severe AH | Strong |
| IV N-acetylcysteine as adjunct to corticosteroids | Strong |
| Nutritional support (oral supplements; enteral if inadequate) | Strong |
| Against universal prophylactic antibiotics | Strong |
| Early liver transplant for highly selected non-responders | Conditional |
| Multidisciplinary care for alcohol use disorder | Strong |

**Contraindications to corticosteroids**:
- Active infection (including untreated sepsis)
- GI bleeding
- Renal failure
- Acute pancreatitis

**Lille Score** (after 7 days of steroids): Used to identify non-responders; if >0.45, consider stopping steroids

**Management**:
- Absolute alcohol abstinence (essential for all stages)
- Nutritional support (35 kcal/kg, 1.2-1.5 g/kg protein)
- Thiamine supplementation (before glucose to prevent Wernicke's)
- Prednisolone 40 mg daily for 28 days (if severe and no contraindications)
- N-acetylcysteine IV infusion for first 5 days as adjunct
- Infection surveillance
- Early liver transplant evaluation for select patients

---

### 7F. Cirrhosis, Ascites, and Hepatic Encephalopathy

**Cirrhosis**: End-stage liver disease from any cause (viral hepatitis, alcohol, NAFLD, autoimmune). Compensated cirrhosis has no complications; decompensated cirrhosis presents with ascites, variceal hemorrhage, hepatic encephalopathy, or jaundice.

**Child-Pugh Classification**:

| Parameter | 1 point | 2 points | 3 points |
|-----------|---------|----------|----------|
| Bilirubin (mg/dL) | <2 | 2-3 | >3 |
| Albumin (g/dL) | >3.5 | 2.8-3.5 | <2.8 |
| INR | <1.7 | 1.7-2.3 | >2.3 |
| Ascites | None | Mild | Moderate-severe |
| Encephalopathy | None | Grade 1-2 | Grade 3-4 |

- Class A: 5-6 points (1-year survival ~100%)
- Class B: 7-9 points (1-year survival ~80%)
- Class C: 10-15 points (1-year survival ~45%)

**MELD Score**: Used for transplant prioritization; based on bilirubin, creatinine, INR, sodium

---

**Ascites Management** (AASLD Practice Guidelines): [^107^] [^109^] [^110^]

| Aspect | Recommendation |
|--------|---------------|
| Diagnostic paracentesis | All patients with ascites admitted to hospital; cell count, culture, albumin, total protein |
| Grade 1 (mild) | Sodium restriction alone |
| Grade 2 (moderate) | Sodium restriction + diuretics (spironolactone +/- furosemide) |
| Grade 3 (large/tense) | Therapeutic paracentesis + albumin |
| Diuretic ratio | Spironolactone 100 mg + furosemide 40 mg daily; titrate to 400:160 max |
| Refractory ascites | Serial large-volume paracentesis + albumin (6-8 g per liter removed) OR TIPS |
| Hepatorenal syndrome | Terlipressin + albumin (where available), midodrine + octreotide + albumin |

**SBP Management**: [^106^] [^109^]

| Aspect | Recommendation |
|--------|---------------|
| Diagnosis | Ascitic fluid PMN >=250 cells/mm3 |
| Empiric treatment | Cefotaxime 2 g IV q8h (or ceftriaxone 1-2 g IV daily) |
| Albumin | 1.5 g/kg on day 1 + 1 g/kg on day 3 (reduces mortality from 29% to 10%) |
| Duration | Minimum 5 days; treat until PMN <250 |
| Secondary prophylaxis | Daily norfloxacin 400 mg OR ciprofloxacin 500 mg |
| Primary prophylaxis | Norfloxacin for high-risk: ascitic protein <1.5 g/dL + advanced liver failure or renal dysfunction |

---

**Hepatic Encephalopathy (HE)**: [^113^] [^118^]

**Definition**: Neuropsychiatric syndrome due to liver dysfunction; ranges from subclinical (minimal HE) to coma.

**Grading (West Haven Criteria)**:
- Grade 0: Minimal/subclinical (neuropsychiatric testing abnormal only)
- Grade 1: Trivial lack of awareness, shortened attention span
- Grade 2: Lethargy, disorientation, inappropriate behavior
- Grade 3: Somnolent but rousable, marked confusion, incoherent speech
- Grade 4: Coma

**Precipitating Factors**:
- Infection (SBP most common)
- GI bleeding
- Constipation
- Electrolyte disturbances (hypokalemia)
- Sedatives/opioids
- Dehydration
- TIPS procedure
- Acute liver insult (alcohol, viral hepatitis)
- Azotemia

**Management**: [^113^]

| Treatment | Details |
|-----------|---------|
| Lactulose | First-line; 15-30 mL 2-3 times daily; titrate to 2-3 soft stools/day |
| Rifaximin | 550 mg BID; add to lactulose for prevention of recurrent HE |
| Correct precipitant | Treat infection, stop GI bleeding, correct electrolytes |
| Protein restriction | Not recommended; ensure adequate nutrition (1.2-1.5 g/kg) |
| Zinc | Supplement if deficient |
| L-ornithine L-aspartate | Alternative ammonia-lowering agent |
| TIPS-related HE | Prophylactic lactulose +/- rifaximin |

**Secondary Prevention**: Lactulose + rifaximin 550 mg BID indefinitely after second overt HE episode

---

### Differential Diagnosis for Liver Disease

| Condition | Key Distinguishing Features |
|-----------|---------------------------|
| Autoimmune hepatitis | ANA/SMA positive, elevated IgG, interface hepatitis on biopsy |
| Primary biliary cholangitis | AMA positive, elevated ALP, granulomatous cholangitis |
| Primary sclerosing cholangitis | ANCA positive, ERCP/MRCP shows beading, UC association |
| Wilson disease | Young patient, low ceruloplasmin, Kayser-Fleischer rings |
| Hemochromatosis | Elevated ferritin/transferrin saturation, HFE gene mutation |
| Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency | Low AAT level, PAS-positive globules on biopsy |
| Drug-induced liver injury | Temporal relationship, exclusion of other causes |
| Budd-Chiari syndrome | Hepatic vein thrombosis, hypercoagulable workup |
| Veno-occlusive disease | Sinusoidal obstruction, typically post-transplant/chemotherapy |

### Red Flags in Liver Disease

- New-onset jaundice
- Ascites or peripheral edema
- Confusion or altered mental status (HE)
- GI bleeding (hematemesis, melena)
- Rapidly rising bilirubin or INR
- Hepatorenal syndrome (rising creatinine)
- Severe abdominal pain (SBP, ischemia)

### Special Populations

**Pregnancy**: 
- TDF safe for HBV in pregnancy; start 3rd trimester if HBV DNA >200,000 IU/mL
- Ribavirin contraindicated in HCV (teratogenic); ensure contraception during and 6 months after
- Acute fatty liver of pregnancy and HELLP syndrome are obstetric emergencies

**Pediatrics**: 
- All infants receive HBV birth dose vaccine
- HAV vaccine at 12-23 months
- Wilson disease, autoimmune hepatitis more common in younger patients

**Elderly**: 
- Higher risk of drug-induced liver injury
- Age >65: FIB-4 cutoff >2.0 for NAFLD screening
- Decreased physiologic reserve for liver disease complications

### Prevention

- Vaccination: HAV and HBV vaccines (routine childhood and at-risk adults)
- Harm reduction for injection drug use
- Screen blood products
- Alcohol moderation
- Weight management for NAFLD prevention
- Safe food and water practices (HAV)
- Regular HCC surveillance in at-risk patients

## 8. Gallbladder Disorders {#8-gallbladder}

### Clinical Overview

Gallbladder disorders range from asymptomatic cholelithiasis (gallstones) to acute cholecystitis (inflamed gallbladder), choledocholithiasis (stones in common bile duct), and cholangitis (bacterial infection of biliary tree). Gallstones affect 10-15% of adults in Western countries, with cholesterol stones being most common. [^103^]

---

### 8A. Cholelithiasis (Gallstones)

**Risk Factors**: Female gender, age >40, obesity, rapid weight loss, pregnancy, hemolytic disease, TPN, certain medications (ceftriaxone, octreotide)

**Types**: Cholesterol stones (80% in West), pigment stones (brown - biliary infection; black - hemolysis)

**Management**:
- Asymptomatic gallstones: No treatment (unless high-risk: porcelain gallbladder, large stone >3 cm, gallbladder polyps >1 cm, sickle cell disease, prior to bariatric surgery)
- Symptomatic biliary colic: Elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy
- Ursodeoxycholic acid (ursodiol) for cholesterol stones in select patients who decline surgery

---

### 8B. Acute Cholecystitis

**Clinical Overview**: Acute inflammation of the gallbladder, usually due to cystic duct obstruction by gallstone. Tokyo Guidelines provide internationally accepted diagnostic and severity criteria. [^103^] [^108^]

**Tokyo Guidelines 2018 Diagnostic Criteria**:
- A: Local signs of inflammation (Murphy's sign, RUQ mass/pain, tenderness)
- B: Systemic signs of inflammation (fever, elevated WBC/CRP)
- C: Imaging findings (gallstones, thickened wall, pericholecystic fluid, sonographic Murphy's)
- **Definite diagnosis**: A + C OR A + B + C

**Tokyo Classification of Severity**:

| Grade | Features | Management |
|-------|----------|------------|
| I (Mild) | No organ dysfunction; disease confined to gallbladder | Early laparoscopic cholecystectomy (ELC) |
| II (Moderate) | WBC >18,000; palpable mass; symptoms >72h; local complications (gangrene, abscess, emphysematous) | ELC after medical optimization |
| III (Severe) | Organ failure (cardiovascular, neurologic, respiratory, renal, hepatic, coagulopathy) | Medical stabilization; ELC only with ICU support or gallbladder drainage |

**Management**: [^103^]
- **Medical**: NPO, IV fluids, analgesia, broad-spectrum antibiotics
- **Timing of surgery**: ELC within 72 hours of symptom onset (optimal) or up to 7-10 days; if delayed, wait 6+ weeks
- **Antibiotics**: Empiric (ceftriaxone + metronidazole OR piperacillin-tazobactam); adjust to culture results
- **For unfit surgical candidates**: Percutaneous cholecystostomy (gallbladder drainage) or endoscopic drainage (EUS-guided)

---

### 8C. Choledocholithiasis

**Definition**: Gallstones in the common bile duct (CBD)

**Clinical Features**: Jaundice, biliary colic, pancreatitis, cholangitis; elevated bilirubin, ALP, GGT

**Diagnosis**: MRCP (non-invasive), EUS (higher sensitivity), ERCP (diagnostic and therapeutic)

**Management**:
- ERCP with sphincterotomy and stone extraction (definitive therapy)
- Laparoscopic cholecystectomy after ERCP (usually within 72 hours)
- Single-stage laparoscopic cholecystectomy with intraoperative cholangiography and CBD exploration (experienced centers)

---

### 8D. Acute Cholangitis

**Definition**: Bacterial infection of obstructed biliary tree

**Tokyo Guidelines Diagnostic Criteria (Charcot's triad + Reynolds' pentad)**:
- Charcot's triad: Fever/chills + RUQ pain + Jaundice (present in 50-70%)
- Reynolds' pentad: Charcot's triad + altered mental status + hypotension (indicates severe, suppurative cholangitis)

**Management**:
- ABCs; IV fluid resuscitation; broad-spectrum antibiotics (piperacillin-tazobactam or ceftriaxone + metronidazole)
- **Urgent biliary drainage** within 24 hours for severe cholangitis
- ERCP with sphincterotomy + stone extraction + stenting
- If ERCP fails: percutaneous transhepatic biliary drainage (PTBD) or EUS-guided drainage
- Definitive therapy: cholecystectomy after recovery

### Differential Diagnosis

| Condition | Key Distinguishing Features |
|-----------|---------------------------|
| Biliary colic | Intermittent RUQ pain, no fever, normal labs |
| Acute cholecystitis | Constant RUQ pain, fever, elevated WBC, imaging findings |
| Acute cholangitis | Fever + jaundice + RUQ pain, elevated bilirubin/ALP |
| Acute pancreatitis | Epigastric pain radiating to back, elevated lipase |
| Hepatitis | Elevated transaminases, less pain, viral serologies |
| Right lower lobe pneumonia | RUQ pain but pulmonary symptoms, chest X-ray findings |
| Myocardial ischemia | Chest pain with cardiac risk factors, ECG changes |
| Peptic ulcer disease | Epigastric pain, responds to PPI, endoscopic findings |

### Red Flags

- Fever with jaundice and RUQ pain (cholangitis)
- Hypotension or altered mental status (severe cholangitis - emergency)
- Sepsis with biliary obstruction
- Suspected gallbladder perforation or emphysematous cholecystitis
- Severe pancreatitis with biliary etiology
- Persistent vomiting with dehydration

### Special Populations

**Pregnancy**: Ultrasound first-line imaging; MRCP without gadolinium if needed; ERCP with fetal shielding for cholangitis; cholecystectomy ideally in 2nd trimester if needed

**Elderly**: Higher mortality with acute cholecystitis; lower threshold for intervention; higher risk of complications

**Diabetes**: Higher risk of complications (emphysematous cholecystitis); increased infection severity

### Prevention

- Gradual weight loss (avoid rapid weight loss)
- Regular physical activity
- Moderate fat intake (very low-fat diets may promote stones)
- Ursodiol during rapid weight loss or TPN

---

## 9. Acute Abdomen Evaluation {#9-acute-abdomen}

### Clinical Overview

The acute abdomen refers to sudden onset of severe abdominal pain requiring urgent evaluation. A systematic approach including history, focused physical examination, and targeted investigations is essential. Life-threatening conditions must be identified rapidly.

---

### 9A. Appendicitis

**Clinical Overview**: Acute inflammation of the appendix, most common surgical emergency. Lifetime risk 7-8%. Obstruction of appendiceal lumen leads to distension, bacterial overgrowth, ischemia, and potential perforation. [^116^] [^117^]

**Diagnosis**:
- Clinical: migratory RUQ pain to RLQ, anorexia, nausea, fever
- Physical: McBurney's point tenderness, Rovsing's sign, psoas sign, obturator sign
- Alvarado score (MANTRELS): Migratory pain, Anorexia, Nausea, Tenderness (RLQ), Rebound pain, Elevated temp, Leukocytosis, Left shift
- Imaging: CT abdomen with contrast (sensitivity ~94%, specificity ~95%); ultrasound first in pregnancy and children

**Management**: [^116^] [^117^]
- **Operative management (gold standard)**: Laparoscopic appendectomy (preferred); lower wound infection rates and shorter hospital stay
- **Non-operative management**: Antibiotics alone for uncomplicated appendicitis; 58-75% 1-year success rate; risk of recurrence ~30% within 1 year
- **Perforated appendicitis**: Laparoscopic appendectomy; peritoneal lavage; antibiotics for 3-7 days; drain placement if abscess present

**Note**: Appendicolith (fecalith) on imaging increases failure rate of non-operative management (48.7% appendectomy rate at 1 year vs 30.6% without appendicolith) [^117^]

---

### 9B. Diverticulitis

**Clinical Overview**: Inflammation of colonic diverticula, most commonly in sigmoid colon. Prevalence increases with age. Most cases are uncomplicated; 15-20% develop complications (abscess, perforation, fistula, obstruction).

**Classification**:
- **Uncomplicated**: localized inflammation without abscess or perforation
- **Complicated**: abscess, perforation, fistula, obstruction, stricture

**Diagnosis**:
- Clinical: LLQ pain, fever, leukocytosis, change in bowel habits
- CT abdomen with contrast: diagnostic test of choice; assess severity and complications (Hinchey classification)

**Hinchey Classification**:
- Ia: Pericolic phlegmon or abscess
- Ib: Distant abscess amenable to percutaneous drainage
- II: Pelvic or retroperitoneal abscess
- III: Generalized purulent peritonitis
- IV: Generalized fecal peritonitis

**Management**:
- **Uncomplicated outpatient**: Clear liquid diet + oral antibiotics (amoxicillin-clavulanate OR ciprofloxacin + metronidazole) for 7-10 days; 2019 IDSA/SHEA guidelines suggest antibiotics may not be necessary in immunocompetent patients with uncomplicated diverticulitis
- **Uncomplicated inpatient**: IV antibiotics, bowel rest, IV fluids
- **Complicated**: Percutaneous drainage for abscess (Hinchey Ia-II); surgery for Hinchey III-IV
- **Surgery**: Laparoscopic sigmoid colectomy with primary anastomosis; Hartmann's procedure for severe contamination
- **Recurrence prevention**: High-fiber diet; consideration of mesalamine or rifaximin; avoid nuts/seeds/popcorn advice has been debunked

---

### 9C. Bowel Obstruction

**Clinical Overview**: Mechanical or functional obstruction of intestinal lumen. Small bowel obstruction (SBO) is most commonly due to adhesions (60-70%) in developed countries. Large bowel obstruction (LBO) is most commonly due to malignancy.

**Causes**:
- **SBO**: Adhesions (post-surgical), hernias, malignancy, Crohn's disease, gallstone ileus, volvulus
- **LBO**: Colorectal cancer, diverticular stricture, volvulus (sigmoid, cecal), fecal impaction

**Clinical Features**: Classic triad - abdominal pain (colicky), vomiting, obstipation; abdominal distension; hyperactive then absent bowel sounds

**Diagnosis**:
- Plain abdominal X-ray: dilated loops, air-fluid levels
- CT abdomen with contrast: diagnostic test of choice; identifies cause, level, severity, complications (ischemia, perforation)
- **Transition point** on CT: where dilated bowel meets collapsed bowel

**Management**:
- **Initial**: NPO, NG tube decompression, IV fluids, electrolyte correction, Foley catheter
- **Complete SBO (no transit)**: Surgery
- **Partial SBO**: Conservative management may resolve; surgery if no improvement in 24-48 hours
- **Adhesive SBO**: 60-70% resolve with conservative management; early surgery if strangulation suspected
- **Malignant LBO**: Resection with or without stoma; colonic stent as bridge to surgery
- **Volvulus**: Sigmoidoscopic decompression for sigmoid volvulus; cecal volvulus requires surgery

**Strangulation Signs** (requires emergent surgery):
- Peritoneal signs (guarding, rigidity, rebound)
- Fever, tachycardia, leukocytosis
- Lactic acidosis
- Absent bowel sounds
- CT findings: pneumatosis intestinalis, portal venous gas, bowel wall thickening with absent enhancement

---

### 9D. Mesenteric Ischemia

**Clinical Overview**: Insufficient blood supply to the intestine. Acute mesenteric ischemia (AMI) is a surgical emergency with mortality >50%. Chronic mesenteric ischemia presents with postprandial pain and weight loss. [^112^]

**Types of AMI**:
- **Arterial embolism** (50%): Atrial fibrillation, valvular disease; SMA most commonly affected
- **Arterial thrombosis** (25%): Atherosclerotic plaque rupture; often history of chronic symptoms
- **Non-occlusive mesenteric ischemia** (20%): Low flow state (sepsis, heart failure, vasopressors)
- **Venous thrombosis** (5-15%): Hypercoagulable states, malignancy, IBD

**Clinical Features**:
- **Classic triad**: Acute severe abdominal pain out of proportion to physical exam findings, absent bowel sounds, and no peritoneal signs early
- Later: peritonitis, sepsis, hemodynamic instability

**Diagnosis**:
- Labs: leukocytosis, elevated lactate (late finding), elevated D-dimer, metabolic acidosis
- **CT angiography**: Gold standard; sensitivity 96-100%, specificity 89-94%
- CT findings: SMA embolus/thrombus, bowel wall thickening, pneumatosis, portal venous gas, absent bowel enhancement

**Management**: [^112^]
- **Initial**: Fluid resuscitation, broad-spectrum antibiotics, anticoagulation (heparin)
- **SMA embolus**: Embolectomy (open or catheter-directed) + bowel resection if necrotic
- **SMA thrombosis**: Bypass surgery or endovascular stenting + resection
- **NOMI**: Treat underlying cause; intra-arterial vasodilators (papaverine)
- **Mesenteric venous thrombosis**: Anticoagulation; surgery if bowel infarction

**Peritonitis or bowel necrosis = emergent laparotomy**

---

### 9E. Acute Pancreatitis

**Clinical Overview**: Acute inflammatory condition of the pancreas. Most common causes are gallstones (40-70%) and alcohol (25-35%). Revised Atlanta Classification (2012) is the international standard. [^111^]

**Diagnosis** (requires 2 of 3 criteria):
1. Typical epigastric pain (acute onset, persistent, severe, may radiate to back)
2. Serum lipase or amylase >=3x upper limit of normal
3. Characteristic imaging findings on CT/MRI

**Etiology (I GET SMASHED)**:
- **I**: Idiopathic (10-30%)
- **G**: Gallstones (most common)
- **E**: Ethanol (alcohol)
- **T**: Trauma
- **S**: Steroids
- **M**: Mumps/malignancy
- **A**: Autoimmune
- **S**: Scorpion stings/spider bites
- **H**: Hyperlipidemia (triglycerides >1000 mg/dL), Hypercalcemia
- **E**: ERCP, Emboli
- **D**: Drugs (azathioprine, 6-MP, valproic acid)

**Revised Atlanta Classification - Severity**: [^111^]

| Grade | Definition |
|-------|-----------|
| Mild (MAP) | No organ failure, no local/systemic complications |
| Moderately severe (MSAP) | Transient organ failure (<48h) and/or local/systemic complications |
| Severe (SAP) | Persistent organ failure (>48h): respiratory, cardiovascular, renal |

**Local Complications**:
- Acute peripancreatic fluid collection (<4 weeks, no wall)
- Pseudocyst (>4 weeks, encapsulated fluid)
- Acute necrotic collection (<4 weeks, heterogeneous)
- Walled-off necrosis (>4 weeks, encapsulated necrosis)

**Management**:
- **Supportive care**: Aggressive IV crystalloid (LR preferred), pain control, antiemetics
- **Nutrition**: Early oral feeding as tolerated; nasogastric or nasojejunal if unable to eat (within 24-48 hours); no need for bowel rest
- **Antibiotics**: NOT indicated for prophylaxis; use only for documented infection (culture-positive necrosis, extrapancreatic infection)
- **Gallstone pancreatitis**: ERCP within 24 hours if cholangitis or biliary obstruction; cholecystectomy during same admission
- **Severe necrosis**: Step-up approach - percutaneous/endoscopic drainage first, minimally invasive necrosectomy if needed; delay intervention until walled-off (>4 weeks) when possible
- **Infected necrosis**: Antibiotics + drainage/necrosectomy

**Prognostic Scores**:
- **BISAP** (Bedside Index for Severity in Acute Pancreatitis): BUN >25, Impaired mental status, SIRS, Age >60, Pleural effusion
- **Ranson's criteria** (within 48 hours): >=3 criteria = severe
- **APACHE II**: >=8 = severe

### Differential Diagnosis for Acute Abdomen

| Condition | Pain Location | Key Features |
|-----------|--------------|--------------|
| Appendicitis | RLQ (migratory) | Fever, anorexia, leukocytosis, McBurney's tenderness |
| Diverticulitis | LLQ | Fever, leukocytosis, older patient |
| Cholecystitis | RUQ | Murphy's sign, fever, ultrasound findings |
| Pancreatitis | Epigastric (to back) | Elevated lipase, risk factors |
| Bowel obstruction | Diffuse/cramping | Distension, vomiting, X-ray/CT findings |
| Mesenteric ischemia | Diffuse (out of proportion) | Lactic acidosis, risk factors, CT angiography |
| Perforated viscus | Diffuse, sudden | Rigid abdomen, free air |
| AAA rupture | Back/abdominal | Pulsatile mass, hypotension, older male |
| Ectopic pregnancy | Lower abdominal | Positive pregnancy test, vaginal bleeding |
| Ovarian torsion | Lower abdominal | Adnexal mass, sudden onset |

### Red Flags in Acute Abdomen

- Peritonitis (guarding, rigidity, rebound)
- Hemodynamic instability
- Severe sepsis/septic shock
- GI bleeding with acute abdomen
- Free air under diaphragm
- Pneumatosis intestinalis
- Portal venous gas
- Acute abdomen in pregnancy
- Acute abdomen with immunosuppression

### Special Populations

**Elderly**: Atypical presentations common; higher mortality; broader differential; maintain low threshold for imaging

**Immunocompromised**: Opportunistic infections, CMV colitis, neutropenic enterocolitis (typhlitis), drug-related complications

**Pregnancy**: Appendicitis most common non-obstetric surgical emergency; physiological leukocytosis confuses interpretation; ultrasound preferred initial imaging; MRI second-line (no gadolinium)

**Pediatrics**: Intussusception (target sign on ultrasound), malrotation with volvulus (bilious vomiting), Meckel's diverticulitis

---

## 10. GI Bleeding {#10-gi-bleeding}

### Clinical Overview

Gastrointestinal bleeding is classified as upper (proximal to ligament of Treitz), lower (distal to ligament of Treitz), or obscure. It is a common medical emergency requiring prompt assessment, resuscitation, and treatment.

---

### 10A. Upper GI Bleeding (UGIB)

**Clinical Overview**: Presents with hematemesis (vomiting blood), coffee-ground emesis, and/or melena (black, tarry stools). Common causes: peptic ulcer disease (most common), varices, Mallory-Weiss tears, erosive disease, malignancy. [^105^]

**Risk Stratification**: [^105^]

| Score | Components | Purpose |
|-------|-----------|---------|
| **Glasgow-Blatchford Score (GBS)** | BUN, Hgb, systolic BP, pulse, melena, syncope, hepatic disease, cardiac failure | Predicts need for intervention; **GBS <=1 allows outpatient management** |
| **Rockall Score** | Age, shock, comorbidities, endoscopic diagnosis, endoscopic stigmata | Predicts rebleeding and mortality; pre-endoscopic and post-endoscopic versions |
| **AIMS65** | Albumin, INR, mental status, systolic BP, age >65 | Predicts in-hospital mortality |

**Management**:
- **Resuscitation**: 2 large-bore IVs, IV fluids, blood products (restrictive strategy: transfuse when Hgb <7 g/dL, <8 g/dL if cardiovascular disease)
- **IV PPI**: High-dose (omeprazole 80 mg bolus + 8 mg/hr infusion) before endoscopy
- **Antibiotics** (if cirrhosis/suspected varices): Ceftriaxone 1 g IV daily (covers gut flora, reduces infection risk)
- **Vasoactive agents** (if varices): Octreotide 50 mcg bolus + 50 mcg/hr infusion or terlipressin
- **Endoscopy**: Within 24 hours; diagnostic and therapeutic
- **Variceal bleeding**: Band ligation; octreotide/terlipressin; balloon tamponade (temporary); TIPS if refractory
- **Non-variceal bleeding**: Endoscopic hemostasis (clips, thermal, injection); high-dose IV PPI x 72 hours
- **Surgery or interventional radiology**: If endoscopic therapy fails

**Forrest Classification of Ulcer Stigmata**:
- Active bleeding (spurting/oozing): High risk; endoscopic therapy required
- Non-bleeding visible vessel: High risk; endoscopic therapy recommended
- Adherent clot: Moderate risk; consider endoscopic therapy
- Flat pigmented spot: Low risk
- Clean base: Very low risk

---

### 10B. Lower GI Bleeding (LGIB)

**Clinical Overview**: Presents with hematochezia (bright red blood per rectum) or maroon-colored stools. Common causes: diverticular bleeding (most common), angioectasia, colorectal cancer, IBD, hemorrhoids, anal fissure, ischemic colitis. [^124^] [^125^] [^126^]

**Risk Stratification**: [^125^] [^126^]

| Tool | Purpose | Key Components |
|------|---------|---------------|
| **Oakland Score** | Identifies low-risk patients safe for discharge | Age, sex, rectal examination, Hgb, HR, BP, prior LGIB |
| **ACRS (AMI)** | Predicts severity | Similar clinical parameters |

**Management** (ACG 2023/ESGE 2021 Guidelines): [^124^] [^125^] [^126^]

- **Resuscitation**: IV access, fluids, blood products if needed
- **Transfusion**: Restrictive strategy (Hgb <7 g/dL; <8 g/dL with cardiovascular disease); target post-transfusion Hgb 7-9 g/dL [^124^]
- **Reverse anticoagulation** if life-threatening: Vitamin K + 4-factor PCC for warfarin; hold DOACs temporarily
- **Initial evaluation**: Rule out upper GI source (15% of hematochezia is from UGIB); consider NG lavage
- **Severe ongoing bleeding**: CT angiography first (sensitivity ~90%); if positive, proceed to transcatheter embolization or surgery [^124^]

**CT Angiography**:
- Role increasing in severe LGIB
- If positive: proceed to embolization (success rate ~98%) or surgery
- If negative: ~80% will have no rebleeding [^125^]

**Colonoscopy**:
- Standard diagnostic test in stable patients
- Perform sometime during hospital stay; urgent (<24h) colonoscopy has NOT been shown to improve outcomes [^126^] [^131^]
- Bowel preparation: 4-6 L PEG-based solution [^124^]
- Endoscopic hemostasis: clips, thermal coagulation, epinephrine injection, band ligation

**For diverticular bleeding**: Endoscopic clips or band ligation; no evidence that epinephrine alone is sufficient

**Interventional Radiology**: Transcatheter embolization for active bleeding not controlled endoscopically; ideally within 90 minutes of positive CTA

**Surgery**: Segmental resection if bleeding source localized and persistent; subtotal colectomy if unable to localize

**Post-Discharge Management**:
- Resume aspirin for secondary cardiovascular prevention as soon as bleeding stops (within 3-7 days) [^130^]
- Resume anticoagulation; timing individualized based on thrombotic vs. rebleeding risk
- Avoid NSAIDs
- Colonoscopy follow-up as indicated

---

### 10C. Obscure GI Bleeding

**Definition**: Persistent or recurrent bleeding with negative initial upper and lower endoscopy

**Approach**:
- Repeat endoscopy (missed lesions common)
- Capsule endoscopy (first-line for small bowel evaluation)
- Device-assisted enteroscopy (push, single-balloon, double-balloon)
- CT/MR enterography for small bowel pathology
- Tagged RBC scan (for intermittent bleeding)
- Angiography (for active bleeding >0.5 mL/min)

### Differential Diagnosis

**Upper GI Bleeding**:
| Condition | Key Features |
|-----------|-------------|
| Peptic ulcer disease | H. pylori, NSAIDs; most common cause |
| Esophageal/gastric varices | Portal hypertension, stigmata of liver disease |
| Mallory-Weiss tear | History of retching/vomiting; linear tear at GE junction |
| Erosive esophagitis/gastritis | GERD symptoms, NSAIDs, alcohol |
| Gastric malignancy | Weight loss, progressive symptoms |
| Dieulafoy lesion | Large tortuous submucosal vessel; massive bleeding |
| Gastric antral vascular ectasia (GAVE) | Watermelon stomach; chronic bleeding |

**Lower GI Bleeding**:
| Condition | Key Features |
|-----------|-------------|
| Diverticular bleeding | Painless, abrupt, large-volume; most common cause |
| Angioectasia | Chronic occult bleeding; older patients; cecum/ascending colon |
| Colorectal cancer | Chronic occult or overt bleeding; change in bowel habits |
| IBD | Bloody diarrhea, mucus, abdominal pain |
| Ischemic colitis | Sudden onset, abdominal pain, "watermelon" colon |
| Hemorrhoids | Bright red blood coating stool, on toilet paper |
| Anal fissure | Painful rectal bleeding, sentinel pile |
| Radiation proctitis | History of pelvic radiation |
| Rectal varices | Portal hypertension, stigmata of liver disease |
| Meckel's diverticulum | Younger patients, technetium scan positive |

### Red Flags

- Hemodynamic instability (tachycardia, hypotension, syncope)
- Ongoing active bleeding
- Melena with hemodynamic compromise (consider variceal bleed)
- Coffee-ground emesis with elevated BUN
- Hematochezia with hemodynamic instability (may be brisk UGIB)
- Known liver disease with GI bleed (high mortality)
- INR >1.5 or platelets <50,000
- Cardiovascular disease with acute anemia

### Medications

| Drug | Dose | Indication |
|------|------|------------|
| Pantoprazole (IV) | 80 mg bolus + 8 mg/hr | UGIB (before endoscopy) |
| Omeprazole (IV) | 80 mg bolus + 8 mg/hr | UGIB |
| Octreotide | 50 mcg bolus + 50 mcg/hr | Variceal bleeding |
| Terlipressin | 2 mg IV q4h | Variceal bleeding (where available) |
| Ceftriaxone | 1 g IV daily | Antibiotic prophylaxis in cirrhosis |
| Vitamin K | 10 mg IV | Warfarin reversal |
| 4-factor PCC | Weight-based | Warfarin reversal |

### Special Populations

**Cirrhosis**: All GI bleeds treated as variceal until proven otherwise; antibiotic prophylaxis; octreotide/terlipressin; early band ligation for varices; high rebleeding and mortality risk

**Anticoagulated patients**: Hold DOACs temporarily; reverse warfarin if life-threatening; resume anticoagulation when hemostasis achieved (balance thrombotic vs. rebleeding risk)

**Elderly**: Higher mortality; atypical presentations; broader differential; lower threshold for ICU admission

**Pediatrics**: Upper endoscopy for melena; Meckel's scan for obscure LGIB; intussusception can present with currant jelly stool

### Prevention

- PPI prophylaxis for high-risk patients on NSAIDs or dual antiplatelet therapy
- H. pylori eradication
- Variceal band ligation for high-risk varices
- Avoid unnecessary NSAIDs
- Colorectal cancer screening
- Smoking cessation
- Limit alcohol consumption
- Anticoagulation management optimization

## 1. Well-Child Care

### 1.1 Schedule of Visits

**Recommended Schedule:**

| 4 months | Developmental milestones, feeding cues, solid food introduction |

### 1.2 Growth Charts

**WHO vs. CDC Growth Charts:**
- **WHO charts**: Recommended for children **< 2 years** of age; based on breastfed infants in optimal conditions; better reflect normal growth patterns [^315^] [^324^]
- **CDC charts**: Used for children **> 2 years** of age [^324^]
- **Corrected age**: Must be used when plotting growth for preterm infants (<37 weeks) [^324^]
- **Specialized charts**: Available for children with specific diagnoses (e.g., Trisomy 21) [^324^]
- **Z-scores**: More precise than percentiles for tracking growth over time; represent standard deviation from the mean [^315^]

**Failure to Thrive (FTT) / Weight Faltering:**

Definitions include:
- Weight for age <5th percentile on standardized growth charts
- Decrease in weight percentile crossing >2 major percentile lines
- Weight for length/height <10th percentile [^315^] [^324^]

**Risk Factors:**
- Prematurity, developmental delay, congenital/genetic anomalies
- Intrauterine exposures, malabsorption, increased metabolic demand
- Psychosocial: social isolation, poverty, household violence/abuse, substance abuse [^324^]

**Workup:** Detailed feeding history (type, amount, frequency), caloric intake assessment, review of bowel patterns, developmental assessment, newborn screen review, physical examination including airway/swallowing, and targeted labs based on clinical suspicion. [^315^]

### 1.3 Developmental Milestones

| Domain | Key Milestones by Age |
|--------|----------------------|
| **Gross Motor** | 2 mo: lifts head; 4 mo: rolls; 6 mo: sits; 9 mo: crawls; 12 mo: walks; 18 mo: runs; 2 yr: climbs; 3 yr: pedals tricycle |
| **Fine Motor** | 4 mo: reaches; 6 mo: transfers; 9 mo: pincer grasp; 12 mo: scribbles; 18 mo: tower of 2 cubes; 2 yr: tower of 6 cubes; 3 yr: copies circle |
| **Language** | 2 mo: cooing; 6 mo: babbling; 9 mo: "mama/dada" nonspecific; 12 mo: 1-2 words; 18 mo: 10-25 words; 2 yr: 2-word phrases; 3 yr: 3-word sentences |
| **Social** | 2 mo: social smile; 6 mo: recognizes strangers; 12 mo: separation anxiety; 18 mo: parallel play; 2 yr: imitation play; 3 yr: cooperative play |

### 1.4 Developmental Screening Tools

**Ages & Stages Questionnaires (ASQ-3):**
- Parent-completed screening for children 1-66 months
- Covers communication, gross motor, fine motor, problem-solving, personal-social
- Available in multiple languages

**M-CHAT-R/F (Modified Checklist for Autism in Toddlers - Revised with Follow-up):**
- Administered at **18 and 24 months** [^323^]
- 20-item parent questionnaire; follow-up interview for positive screens
- High sensitivity for autism spectrum disorder detection
- Critical for early intervention referral

**Other Screening Tools:**
- **PEDS**: Parent's Evaluation of Developmental Status (general developmental screening)
- **SWYC**: Survey of Wellbeing of Young Children (integrated developmental, behavioral, and family risk screening)
- **PSC-17**: Pediatric Symptom Checklist (behavioral/emotional screening)

### 1.5 Vision and Hearing Screening

**Vision:**
- **Newborn**: Examine eyes for structural abnormalities, red reflex
- **6-12 months**: Cover-uncover test, corneal light reflex
- **3-5 years**: Visual acuity testing (LEA symbols or Snellen), strabismus screening
- School-age: Annual vision screening

**Hearing:**
- **Newborn**: Universal newborn hearing screening (otoacoustic emissions or ABR) before 1 month
- **Follow-up**: Diagnostic audiology by 3 months if screen positive
- **Intervention**: Early intervention by 6 months if confirmed hearing loss
- School-age: Periodic hearing screening

### 1.6 Lead, Anemia, TB Screening

**Lead Screening:**
- Risk assessment at 6, 9, 12, 18, 24 months; then annually 3-6 years
- Universal blood lead level testing recommended in high-prevalence areas
- Targeted screening based on risk factors (Medicaid eligibility, older housing, etc.)

**Anemia Screening:**
- Hemoglobin/hematocrit at 12 months
- Risk assessment at 4, 6, 9 months
- Repeat screening in high-risk children (prematurity, low iron diet, etc.)

**TB Risk Assessment:**
- Annual risk assessment beginning at 12 months
- TST or IGRA if risk factors present (travel to endemic areas, household contact, HIV, etc.)

### 1.7 Dental Referral Timing

- **First dental visit**: By first tooth eruption or **no later than 12 months** [^323^]
- Fluoride varnish application beginning at primary tooth eruption
- Risk-based caries prevention counseling
- Establish dental home by age 1

## 2. Common Pediatric Illnesses

### 2.1 Acute Otitis Media (AOM)

**Clinical Overview:** AOM is the most common childhood infection for which antibiotics are prescribed, with 23% of children diagnosed by age 1 and 60% by age 3. [^314^] [^316^]

**Definitive Diagnosis Requires:**
- Moderate or severe bulging of tympanic membrane (TM) OR
- New onset otorrhea not due to otitis externa OR
- Mild bulging of TM AND recent (<48h) otalgia or intense TM erythema [^314^]

**AAP Treatment Guidelines:**

| Patient Category | Recommendation |
|-----------------|----------------|
| **Age <6 months** | Immediate antibiotic therapy (no watchful waiting) [^317^] |
| **6-23 months, bilateral AOM** | Immediate antibiotics [^317^] |
| **6-23 months, unilateral, mild** | Watchful waiting 48-72h (with analgesia) |
| **>24 months, unilateral/bilateral, mild** | Watchful waiting 48-72h with follow-up [^319^] |
| **Severe symptoms** (fever >39C, severe otalgia, otorrhea) | Immediate antibiotics |

**Watchful Waiting:** Only appropriate if reliable follow-up within 72 hours is possible and no comorbidities. Provide analgesia (acetaminophen/ibuprofen). [^319^] [^325^]

**Antibiotic Therapy:**
- **First-line**: Amoxicillin 75-90 mg/kg/day divided BID or 45-60 mg/kg/day divided TID [^325^]
- **Duration**: 10 days (<2 years or severe); 5-7 days (>2 years, uncomplicated) [^317^] [^325^]
- **Recurrent AOM**: Amoxicillin-clavulanate 90 mg/kg/day for 7-10 days [^317^]
- **Penicillin allergy**: 2nd/3rd generation cephalosporin (cefuroxime, cefpodoxime); macrolide or clindamycin for high allergy risk [^317^]

**Red Flags:** Mastoiditis (post-auricular swelling, proptosis), meningitis signs, facial nerve palsy [^316^]

### 2.2 Streptococcal Pharyngitis

**Clinical Overview:** Group A Streptococcus (GAS) pharyngitis peaks in school-aged children (5-15 years). Untreated GAS can lead to acute rheumatic fever and post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis. [^322^]

**Centor/McIsaac Criteria:** [^318^] [^322^]

| Criterion | Points |
|-----------|--------|
| Fever (oral >38C / 100.4F) | +1 |
| Absence of cough | +1 |
| Tonsillar exudates/swelling | +1 |
| Tender anterior cervical lymphadenopathy | +1 |
| Age 3-14 years (McIsaac modification) | +1 |
| Age 15-44 years | 0 |
| Age >45 years | -1 |

**Score Interpretation:**
- **0-1**: Low probability - no testing needed
- **2-3**: Intermediate - rapid strep testing indicated
- **4-5**: High probability - empiric treatment reasonable [^318^]

**Diagnostic Approach:**
- Positive RADT: Treat empirically
- Negative RADT + McIsaac >2: Confirm with throat culture [^322^]
- PCR tests available with high sensitivity/specificity

**Treatment:**
- **First-line**: Amoxicillin 50 mg/kg once daily (max 1000mg) for 10 days OR divided BID
- Alternative: Penicillin V, cephalexin (non-anaphylactic allergy)
- Complete 10-day course to prevent rheumatic fever

**Differential Diagnosis:** Viral pharyngitis (most common), EBV/mononucleosis, Mycoplasma, peritonsillar abscess, Kawasaki disease [^322^]

### 2.3 Viral URI / Bronchiolitis

**Clinical Overview:** Bronchiolitis is the most common cause of lower respiratory tract infection in children <12 months, peaking at 3-6 months. RSV is the most common pathogen. [^325^]

**AAP 2014 Guidelines - Key Recommendations:**

**Diagnosis:**
- Clinical diagnosis based on history and physical examination
- Chest X-ray NOT routinely recommended
- Viral testing NOT routinely recommended

**Management (Supportive Care Only):** [^325^]
- Hydration (oral or IV as needed)
- Oxygen for hypoxemia (SpO2 <90%)
- Suctioning of nasal secretions
- **No bronchodilators** (albuterol, epinephrine) - not recommended
- **No corticosteroids** - not recommended
- **No chest physiotherapy** - not recommended
- Trial of bronchodilator may be considered for first episode with strong family history of atopy/asthma

**Palivizumab Prophylaxis:** [^331^]
- Monoclonal antibody against RSV
- Maximum 5 monthly doses (15 mg/kg) during RSV season
- Criteria: Infants born <29 weeks gestation OR <32 weeks with chronic lung disease OR hemodynamically significant congenital heart disease
- Restricted to first year of life

**Red Flags (Hospitalization Criteria):**
- SpO2 <90% on room air
- Severe respiratory distress (grunting, retractions, nasal flaring)
- Apnea episodes
- Dehydration (poor feeding, decreased urine output)
- Lethargy

### 2.4 Croup (Laryngotracheobronchitis)

**Clinical Overview:** Croup is a common childhood respiratory illness characterized by sudden onset of barky cough, hoarse voice, inspiratory stridor, and respiratory distress caused by upper airway inflammation. Most commonly affects children 6 months to 6 years, peaking in the second year. Parainfluenza types 1 and 3 are the most common pathogens. [^320^] [^322^]

**Westley Croup Score (Severity Classification):**
- **Mild**: Occasional barking cough, no stridor at rest, mild or no retractions
- **Moderate**: Frequent barking cough, audible stridor at rest, visible retractions but little distress
- **Severe**: Frequent cough, prominent stridor, conspicuous retractions, significant distress
- **Impending respiratory failure**: Lethargy, dusky appearance, decreasing retractions

**Treatment:** [^320^] [^322^] [^323^]

| Severity | Treatment |
|----------|-----------|
| **Mild** | Single dose oral dexamethasone 0.15 mg/kg; supportive care; home discharge |
| **Moderate** | Oral dexamethasone 0.15-0.6 mg/kg; nebulized epinephrine if distress; observe 2-4 hours |
| **Severe** | Nebulized epinephrine + dexamethasone; oxygen; hospital admission; ENT consultation |

**Key Points:**
- Oral dexamethasone preferred (0.6 mg/kg most commonly used dose) [^320^]
- Doses of 0.15, 0.3, or 0.6 mg/kg appear equally effective [^320^]
- Single dose sufficient for most children; second dose if symptoms persist 3-4 days
- Nebulized epinephrine: 0.5 mL/kg of L-epinephrine 1:1000 (max 5 mL); OR racemic epinephrine 0.5 mL [^320^]
- Observe minimum 2-4 hours after epinephrine before discharge [^322^]
- No role for humidified air/mist tents (not recommended) [^322^]
- No antibiotics or beta-agonists in typical croup [^322^]

**Red Flags:** Stridor at rest, severe retractions, cyanosis, altered mental status, fatigue (impending respiratory failure)

**Differential Diagnosis:** Epiglottitis, bacterial tracheitis, foreign body aspiration, anaphylaxis [^323^]

### 2.5 Pneumonia in Children

**Clinical Overview:** Pneumonia is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in children worldwide. Etiology varies by age and severity. [^321^] [^328^]

**Classification by Age and Pathogen:**

| Age Group | Common Pathogens | Treatment |
|-----------|-----------------|-----------|
| **Neonates (<1 month)** | GBS, E. coli, Listeria, Chlamydia | Ampicillin + gentamicin or cefotaxime |
| **1-3 months** | RSV, Chlamydia, B. pertussis, S. pneumoniae | Macrolide + beta-lactam |
| **3 months-5 years** | Viral (RSV, influenza), S. pneumoniae | Amoxicillin (if bacterial) [^328^] |
| **>5 years** | S. pneumoniae, Mycoplasma, Chlamydia | Amoxicillin; add macrolide if atypical suspected [^328^] |

**Outpatient Treatment (Community-Acquired Pneumonia):** [^328^]
- Fully/partially immunized children: **Amoxicillin** is acceptable first-line therapy
- Penicillin allergy: 3rd generation cephalosporin or clindamycin
- If atypical suspected (>=5 years): Macrolide (azithromycin) monotherapy or added to beta-lactam
- Duration: Typically 5-7 days; shorter courses (3-5 days) may be adequate for uncomplicated CAP

**Inpatient Treatment:**
- Ampicillin or ceftriaxone (fully immunized)
- Add vancomycin if MRSA or severe
- Add azithromycin if atypical suspected
- Consider viral testing; oseltamivir if influenza

**Red Flags:** Respiratory distress, SpO2 <90%, toxic appearance, dehydration, altered mental status, concern for effusion/empyema

### 2.6 Gastroenteritis

**Clinical Overview:** Acute gastroenteritis (AGE) results in 1.5 million office visits, 200,000 hospitalizations, and 300 deaths annually in US children. [^324^]

**Key Management Principles:**

**Oral Rehydration Therapy (ORT):**
- **First-line** for mild-moderate dehydration
- WHO ORS or commercial rehydration solutions (Pedialyte)
- Goal: 50-100 mL/kg over 3-4 hours for mild-moderate dehydration
- Avoid plain water, juice, or sports drinks (inadequate electrolytes)
- Continue age-appropriate feeding during rehydration

**Zinc Supplementation (WHO/International Guidelines):**
- <6 months: 10 mg elemental zinc once daily for 10-14 days
- >6 months: 20 mg elemental zinc once daily for 10-14 days
- Reduces duration and severity of diarrhea
- Evidence strongest in developing countries and malnourished children

**Ondansetron:** [^324^]
- 5-HT3 serotonin antagonist effective at improving oral rehydration success
- Dose: 0.15 mg/kg (max 8 mg) orally or IV
- Decreases vomiting, need for IV fluids, and hospitalizations
- Excellent safety profile; rare QT prolongation (avoid in congenital long QT)
- Joint ESPGHAN/ESPID guidelines encourage short course use

**Indications for IV Fluids:**
- Severe dehydration (>10%)
- Failed oral rehydration (persistent vomiting)
- Altered mental status, hemodynamic instability

**Nutrition:**
- Early refeeding with age-appropriate diet as soon as rehydrated
- Continue breastfeeding
- Avoid prolonged BRAT diet (inadequate nutrition)
- Temporary lactose intolerance uncommon in developed countries

**Red Flags:** Bloody diarrhea (dysentery), severe dehydration, bilious vomiting, abdominal distension, toxic appearance, altered mental status

### 2.7 Febrile Illness

**Febrile Infants (<3 months):**
- **Low risk (well-appearing)**: Urinalysis + urine culture (catheterized specimen), consider CBC/blood culture, observe if CSF deferred
- **High risk (ill-appearing)**: Full sepsis workup (CBC, blood culture, UA/urine culture, lumbar puncture), empiric IV antibiotics (ampicillin + gentamicin or cefotaxime), hospitalization

**Febrile Infants 3-36 months:**
- Source evaluation based on clinical appearance and height of fever
- **UTI workup**: Catheterized or suprapubic urine specimen (bag urine NOT reliable for culture); UA + urine culture
- **Occult bacteremia risk**: Now rare with widespread vaccination (PCV, Hib); consider blood culture if toxic appearance, no focus, or high-risk features
- **Immunization status**: Incomplete PCV/Hib increases risk

**Febrile Seizures:**
- Most common seizures in childhood (2-5% of children)
- Age 6 months to 5 years, with fever >38C
- Simple febrile seizure: Generalized, <15 minutes, single episode in 24 hours, no focal features, child returns to baseline
- **Complex febrile seizure**: Focal, >15 minutes, recurrent in 24 hours, or persistent post-ictal changes
- **Management**: Supportive; address fever source; antipyretics do NOT prevent recurrence
- LP if concern for meningitis (ill-appearing, persistent lethargy, meningeal signs)
- Recurrence risk: 30-50% if first seizure before age 1
- Long-term outcomes generally excellent; no increased risk of epilepsy after simple febrile seizure

**Red Flags:** Toxic appearance, petechiae/purpura, meningismus, focal neurologic deficit, persistent lethargy, seizure >15 minutes or focal features, immunocompromised, unvaccinated status

### 2.8 Constipation in Children

**Functional Constipation (Rome IV Criteria):**
- <4 years: >=2 weeks of <=2 defecations/week, stool withholding, hard/painful stools, large-diameter stools, or palpable abdominal fecal mass
- >=4 years: <=2 defecations/week AND >=1 additional symptom (incontinence, stool withholding, painful defecation, large stools, abdominal pain)

**Management:**
- **Disimpaction**: Oral polyethylene glycol (PEG 3350) 1-1.5 g/kg/day for 3-6 days; or rectal therapies (enemas) for severe cases
- **Maintenance**: PEG 3350 0.4-0.8 g/kg/day, titrated to goal of 1-2 soft stools/day
- Duration: Typically 6-12 months; gradual taper after regular bowel pattern established
- **Behavioral**: Toilet sitting 5-10 minutes after meals, positive reinforcement, stool diary
- **Diet**: Adequate fiber and fluid intake; avoid excessive milk (>24 oz/day in toddlers)

**Red Flags (Not Functional Constipation):**
- Onset in first month of life
- Failure to pass meconium within 48 hours
- Bilious vomiting, abdominal distension
- Blood in stool with normal stool caliber (Hirschsprung disease)
- Poor growth, sacral dimple/tuft, leg weakness (tethered cord)

### 2.9 Atopic Dermatitis in Infants/Children

**Clinical Features:**
- Onset typically 2-6 months of age
- Infantile pattern: Cheeks, scalp, extensor surfaces (spares diaper area)
- Childhood pattern: Flexural areas (antecubital, popliteal fossae)
- Intense pruritus, dry skin, eczematous patches

**Management:**
- **Skin hydration**: Liberal emollient application multiple times daily
- **Bathing**: Lukewarm baths, mild non-soap cleansers, pat dry, immediate emollient
- **Topical corticosteroids**: Low potency (hydrocortisone 1-2.5%) for face; medium potency (triamcinolone) for body; limit to 2-3 weeks then taper
- **Topical calcineurin inhibitors** (tacrolimus, pimecrolimus): Steroid-sparing for sensitive areas (face, intertriginous)
- **Antihistamines**: For pruritus control (sedating at bedtime)
- **Wet wrap therapy**: For severe flares
- **Trigger avoidance**: Fragrances, wool, harsh soaps, overheating

**Red Flags:** Significant infection (pus, honey-colored crusts, fever), widespread eczema herpeticum (clustered vesicles, punched-out erosions), failure to thrive, treatment resistance (consider immunodeficiency)

### 2.10 Pinworms and Head Lice

**Pinworms (Enterobius vermicularis):**
- Diagnosis: Scotch tape test (apply clear tape to perianal area in morning before bathing)
- Treatment: Albendazole 400 mg single dose (repeat in 2 weeks) OR mebendazole 100 mg single dose (repeat in 2 weeks) OR pyrantel pamoate 11 mg/kg (max 1g) single dose (repeat in 2 weeks)
- Treat all household contacts simultaneously
- Hand hygiene, short fingernails, morning bathing

**Head Lice (Pediculus capitis):**
- Diagnosis: Visualization of live lice or nits firmly attached <1 cm from scalp
- **First-line**: 1% Permethrin cream rinse (Nix) applied to damp hair for 10 minutes, repeat in 7-10 days
- Alternative: 0.5% Malathion, spinosad 0.9% suspension, oral ivermectin 200 mcg/kg (repeat in 7-10 days)
- Wet combing with fine-toothed comb
- Treat only confirmed infestations; no prophylactic treatment of contacts
- "No-nit" school policies NOT recommended by AAP

## 3. Pediatric Immunization Schedule

### 3.1 CDC ACIP 2024 Schedule Overview

The 2024 child and adolescent immunization schedule was approved by ACIP and endorsed by AAP, AAFP, ACOG, and other professional organizations. [^346^]

### 3.2 Vaccines by Age

**Birth:**
- **Hepatitis B (HepB) #1**: Monovalent vaccine within 24 hours of birth for all medically stable infants >2,000g [^347^]

**2 months:**
- HepB #2 (1-2 months, at least 4 weeks after dose 1)
- **DTaP #1** (Diphtheria, tetanus, acellular pertussis)
- **IPV #1** (Inactivated poliovirus)
- **Hib #1** (Haemophilus influenzae type b)
- **PCV #1** (Pneumococcal conjugate)
- **RV #1** (Rotavirus - RotaTeq or Rotarix)

**4 months:**
- DTaP #2, IPV #2, Hib #2, PCV #2, RV #2

**6 months:**
- DTaP #3, IPV #3 (can be given 6-18 months), Hib #3, PCV #3, RV #3 (RotaTeq only)
- HepB #3 (6-18 months, at least 8 weeks after dose 2, 16 weeks after dose 1)
- Influenza: Annual vaccination beginning at 6 months (2 doses first season if never vaccinated)

**12-15 months:**
- **MMR #1** (12-15 months)
- **Varicella #1** (12-15 months)
- **Hib #4** (12-15 months, if needed based on vaccine type)
- **PCV #4** (12-15 months)
- DTaP #4 (15-18 months)
- **HepA #1** (12-23 months)

**18 months:**
- HepA #2 (6-18 months after dose 1)

**4-6 years (Kindergarten entry):**
- DTaP #5, IPV #4, MMR #2, Varicella #2

**11-12 years:**
- **Tdap** (Tetanus, diphtheria, acellular pertussis)
- **HPV #1** (2-dose series if initiated before 15th birthday; 3-dose series if >=15 years) [^347^]
- **MenACWY #1** (Meningococcal A, C, W, Y)

**16 years:**
- MenACWY #2 (booster)

**16-18 years:**
- **MenB** (2-dose series; preferred interval 6 months; serogroup B meningococcal)

### 3.3 Catch-Up Schedule Guidance

**General Principles:**
- Use minimum interval schedule; do NOT restart series
- Minimum intervals between doses: 4 weeks for most vaccines
- Catch-up should proceed as rapidly as possible
- Immunity from previous doses is maintained even with delayed boosters

**Key Catch-Up Notes:**
- **HepB**: 3-dose series; minimum 4 weeks between doses 1-2, 8 weeks between doses 2-3, 16 weeks between doses 1-3
- **DTaP**: For children <7 years; if series started late, use accelerated schedule
- **IPV**: If started late, 4-dose series (minimum 4 weeks between doses 1-2 and 2-3, 6 months between doses 3-4)
- **MMR**: 2 doses at least 4 weeks apart
- **Varicella**: 2 doses at least 3 months apart (if <13 years) or 4 weeks (if >=13 years)
- **HPV**: If series started at >=15 years, 3-dose series (0, 1-2, 6 months)
- **Hib**: Not routinely recommended for healthy children >5 years

**Special Populations:**
- Immunocompromised, asplenia, sickle cell: Additional PCV doses, earlier MenACWY
- Travel: HepA accelerated, typhoid, Japanese encephalitis as indicated

## 4. Adolescent Health

### 4.1 HEADSSS Assessment

The HEADSSS psychosocial interview is a comprehensive screening tool for adolescent risk assessment, typically administered at 11-12, 13-15, 16-17, and 18-21 year visits.

| Domain | Assessment Content |
|--------|-------------------|
| **H** - Home | Living situation, family relationships, stability, feeling safe at home |
| **E** - Education/Employment | School performance, attendance, plans after high school, work |
| **A** - Activities | Sports, hobbies, exercise, screen time, friends |
| **D** - Drugs | Tobacco, alcohol, marijuana, vaping, prescription misuse, other substances |
| **S** - Sexuality | Sexual activity, partners, contraception, STI history, sexual orientation |
| **S** - Suicide/Depression | Mood, anxiety, self-harm thoughts, previous attempts, stress |
| **S** - Safety | Seatbelts, helmets, texting while driving, bullying, violence, guns |

### 4.2 Contraception Counseling and Confidentiality

**Confidentiality:**
- Adolescents have the right to confidential health services in many jurisdictions
- Inform both patient and parent about confidentiality limits (harm to self/others)
- Encourage parent involvement when appropriate
- Document confidential portions separately if needed

**Contraception Options:**
- **Long-acting reversible contraception (LARC)**: IUDs and implants - most effective, first-line for adolescents
- **Combined hormonal contraceptives**: Pill, patch, ring
- **Progestin-only**: Pill, injection (Depo-Provera), implant
- **Barrier methods**: Condoms (STI protection), diaphragm, cervical cap
- **Emergency contraception**: Levonorgestrel (Plan B) available OTC; ulipristal (Ella) prescription; most effective within 72 hours but effective up to 120 hours
- **Dual protection**: Condoms + hormonal method for STI and pregnancy prevention

### 4.3 Substance Use Screening (SBIRT)

**SBIRT (Screening, Brief Intervention, and Referral to Treatment):**
- **Screen**: Use validated tool (AUDIT, CRAFFT 2.1, DAST)
- **CRAFFT 2.1 questions**: Car, Relax, Alone, Forget, Friends, Trouble
- **Brief Intervention**: Motivational interviewing; express concern, set goals, provide feedback
- **Referral to Treatment**: For moderate-high risk users; substance use disorder evaluation

### 4.4 Eating Disorder Screening

**Screening Tools:**
- **SCOFF questionnaire**: 5-item screen (Sick, Control, One stone, Fat, Food)
- **Eating Disorder Examination (EDE-Q)**
- **DSM-5 criteria evaluation**

**Warning Signs:**
- Significant weight loss or gain
- Preoccupation with weight, food, or exercise
- Distorted body image
- Amenorrhea in females
- Restriction of food groups, binge eating, purging behaviors
- Excessive exercise
- Social withdrawal around meals

**Red Flags:** Electrolyte abnormalities, bradycardia, orthostatic hypotension, dehydration, syncope, hematemesis (requiring urgent evaluation)

## 5. Neonatal Care

### 5.1 Newborn Screening

**Metabolic Screening (Blood Spot/Guthrie Test):**
- Screening panel varies by state but typically includes 30+ conditions
- **Core conditions**: PKU, congenital hypothyroidism, galactosemia, sickle cell disease, medium-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency (MCAD), congenital adrenal hyperplasia, biotinidase deficiency, severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID), critical congenital heart disease
- Timing: 24-48 hours after birth (after sufficient protein intake)
- Repeat screening if done <24 hours

**Hearing Screening:**
- Universal newborn hearing screening before hospital discharge
- Otoacoustic emissions (OAE) or automated auditory brainstem response (AABR)
- Pass result: Both ears pass
- Refer: Diagnostic audiology by 3 months if refer result
- Intervention: Early intervention services by 6 months if hearing loss confirmed

**Critical Congenital Heart Disease (CCHD) Screening:**
- Pulse oximetry screening at 24-48 hours of life
- Measure preductal (right hand) and postductal (foot) SpO2
- Positive screen: SpO2 <90% in any extremity OR SpO2 90-95% in both with >3% difference OR 3 repeated measurements 90-95% with <3% difference
- Positive screen requires echocardiogram evaluation

### 5.2 Jaundice

**Clinical Overview:** Physiologic jaundice is common in newborns. Pathologic jaundice requires evaluation for underlying causes.

**Risk Assessment:**
- Gestational age
- Hours since birth
- Risk factors: Hemolysis (ABO/Rh incompatibility, G6PD deficiency), bruising/cephalohematoma, prematurity, exclusive breastfeeding, East Asian ethnicity, sibling with jaundice requiring phototherapy

**Transcutaneous Bilirubin (TCB) vs. Total Serum Bilirubin (TSB):**
- **TCB**: Non-invasive screening tool; useful for initial assessment
- **TSB**: Gold standard; obtain if TCB >=15 mg/dL or near phototherapy threshold, or if clinical concern
- TCB may underestimate TSB at higher levels

**Phototherapy Thresholds (AAP 2022 Guidelines):**
- Based on gestational age, hours since birth, and neurotoxicity risk factors
- **Phototherapy**: Initiate at lower thresholds for preterm, hemolytic disease, or other risk factors
- **Exchange transfusion**: At higher thresholds
- Use AAP hour-specific nomogram or calculator (BiliTool) for decision-making

**Management:**
- **Phototherapy**: Blue-green light (460-490 nm); expose maximum skin surface; maintain hydration; monitor temperature; eye protection
- **Exchange transfusion**: For severe/severe-rapidly rising bilirubin or signs of acute bilirubin encephalopathy
- **IVIG**: For isoimmune hemolytic disease with rising bilirubin despite intensive phototherapy

**Red Flags (Pathologic Jaundice):**
- Jaundice within first 24 hours of life
- TSB rising >0.2 mg/dL/hour or >5 mg/dL/day
- Conjugated (direct) bilirubin >1 mg/dL if TSB <5, or >20% of TSB
- Pale/acholic stools or dark urine
- Signs of acute bilirubin encephalopathy (lethargy, poor feeding, high-pitched cry, arching)

### 5.3 Breastfeeding Support

**Recommendations:**
- Exclusive breastfeeding for approximately **6 months**
- Continued breastfeeding with complementary foods through **at least 12 months** and beyond as mutually desired
- Initiate breastfeeding within first hour after birth
- Skin-to-skin contact immediately after delivery
- Rooming-in (mother and infant together 24 hours/day)
- Feed on demand (8-12 times per 24 hours in early weeks)
- Avoid pacifiers and supplements unless medically indicated
- **Contraindications to breastfeeding**: Maternal HIV (in developed countries), untreated active TB, certain medications (chemotherapy, radioactive compounds), illicit drug use, galactosemia in infant
- **Not contraindications**: Hepatitis B, Hepatitis C, most medications (check LactMed)

**Signs of Adequate Intake:**
- 6+ wet diapers/day by day 5
- 3-4+ yellow stools/day by day 5
- Audible swallowing during feeds
- Return to birth weight by 10-14 days
- Weight gain 15-30 g/day after day 5

### 5.4 Safe Sleep (Back to Sleep)

**AAP Recommendations to Reduce SIDS Risk:**
- **Back to sleep** for every sleep (naps and nighttime)
- Use a **firm sleep surface** (firm mattress with fitted sheet only)
- **No soft objects or loose bedding** in sleep area (no pillows, blankets, bumpers, toys)
- **Room-sharing without bed-sharing** for at least first 6 months (ideally first year)
- Avoid overheating; dress appropriately for room temperature
- **Pacifier** at nap and bedtime (offer after breastfeeding established)
- No exposure to smoke, alcohol, or illicit drugs during pregnancy or after birth
- Avoid commercial devices marketed to reduce SIDS
- **Do NOT use**: Wedges, positioners, home cardiorespiratory monitors (not proven to reduce SIDS)
- Tummy time when awake and supervised to prevent positional plagiocephaly

## 6. Geriatric Syndromes

### 6.1 Frailty Assessment

**Clinical Overview:** Frailty is a state of increased vulnerability to stressors resulting from cumulative decline across multiple physiologic systems. It predicts falls, disability, hospitalization, and mortality. [^342^] [^344^] [^345^]

**Fried Phenotype (5 Criteria):** [^342^] [^343^] [^345^]

| Criterion | Assessment |
|-----------|-----------|
| **Weight Loss** | Unintentional loss >10 lbs or >5% body weight in past year |
| **Exhaustion** | Self-reported fatigue (CES-D questions: "everything an effort," "could not get going") |
| **Weakness** | Decreased grip strength by dynamometer (sex and BMI adjusted) |
| **Slow gait** | Walking time over 15 feet/4.57m (sex and height adjusted) |
| **Low physical activity** | Energy expenditure below threshold (men <383 kcal/week; women <270 kcal/week) |

**Scoring:**
- **Robust**: 0 criteria
- **Pre-frail**: 1-2 criteria
- **Frail**: 3-5 criteria [^342^] [^343^]

**FRAIL Scale (Quick Screening):** [^344^]
- **F**atigue (are you fatigued?)
- **R**esistance (can you climb a single flight of stairs?)
- **A**mbulation (can you walk one block?)
- **I**llnesses (do you have more than five?)
- **L**oss of weight (>5%)
- **0**: Not frail; **1-2**: Intermediate; **>=3**: Frail

**Prevalence:** Increases with age: 31.7% in sexagenarians to 67.6% in nonagenarians [^342^]

### 6.2 Falls

**Clinical Overview:** Falls are the leading cause of injury-related death in adults >65. One in four older adults falls annually. Annual healthcare costs exceed $50 billion. [^352^] [^355^]

**Risk Assessment:**

**Risk Stratification:** [^352^]
- **Low risk**: No history of falling OR single non-severe fall without gait/balance problems
- **Intermediate risk**: Single non-severe fall WITH gait/balance problems
- **High risk**: Fall with injury, multiple falls (>=2 in 12 months), known frailty, inability to get up after fall, transient loss of consciousness

**Timed Up and Go (TUG) Test:** [^352^] [^355^]
- Patient stands from chair, walks 3 meters, turns, returns, sits
- **>12 seconds**: Increased fall risk
- Combined with other measures (grip strength, chair stand, gait speed) for comprehensive assessment

**Comprehensive Fall Risk Factors:**
- Medical: Orthostatic hypotension, vision impairment, peripheral neuropathy, arrhythmias, stroke, Parkinson's, cognitive impairment, depression
- Medications: Fall risk-increasing drugs (FRIDs) - sedatives, antidepressants, antipsychotics, opioids, antihypertensives, hypoglycemics
- Environmental: Poor lighting, loose rugs, lack of grab bars, clutter, slippery surfaces
- Functional: Lower extremity weakness, gait/balance disorders, previous falls

**Multifactorial Interventions:** [^352^] [^357^]
1. **Exercise**: Balance, strength, and gait training (Tai Chi effective)
2. **Medication review**: Deprescribing FRIDs
3. **Vision optimization**: Cataract surgery, updated glasses
4. **Environmental modification**: Home safety assessment
5. **Cardiovascular management**: Orthostatic hypotension, arrhythmia treatment
6. **Footwear**: Low-heeled, non-slip shoes
7. **Assistive devices**: Canes, walkers as needed
8. **Vitamin D supplementation** if deficient

**World Guidelines Recommendations:** [^352^]
- Gait speed recommended for falls risk prediction; TUG as alternative (Grade 1A/1B)
- Multiprofessional multifactorial assessment for high-risk patients (Grade 1B)
- Medication review and deprescribing should be part of multidomain interventions (Grade 1B)
- No physical restraints for falls prevention in care homes (Grade 1B)

### 6.3 Delirium

**Clinical Overview:** Delirium is an acute, fluctuating disturbance of attention and awareness that develops over hours to days. It indicates an underlying medical condition and requires urgent evaluation. [^348^]

**Confusion Assessment Method (CAM):** [^348^] [^349^] [^353^]

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **1. Acute onset and fluctuating course** | Change from baseline that fluctuates during the day |
| **2. Inattention** | Difficulty focusing, easily distracted, difficulty following conversation |
| **3. Disorganized thinking** | Rambling, irrelevant conversation, unclear flow of ideas |
| **4. Altered level of consciousness** | Hyperalert, lethargic, stuporous, or comatose |

**Diagnosis**: Features 1 AND 2 must be present, PLUS either 3 or 4 [^353^]

**Other Screening Tools:**
- **3D-CAM**: 3-minute diagnostic assessment; sensitivity 95%, specificity 94% [^348^]
- **CAM-ICU**: For non-verbal/ICU patients [^349^]
- **bCAM**: Brief CAM for verbal patients in ED [^349^]
- **DTS (Delirium Triage Screen)**: Ultra-brief (<20 seconds); 98% sensitive; used in ED [^349^]
- **UB-2**: Ultra-brief 2-item screen; 96% sensitive in persons with dementia [^358^]

**Delirium vs. Dementia:** [^348^]

| Feature | Delirium | Dementia |
|---------|----------|----------|
| Onset | Acute (hours-days) | Insidious (months-years) |
| Course | Fluctuating, reversible | Progressive, irreversible |
| Attention | Markedly impaired | Normal (early stages) |
| Consciousness | Altered | Normal (until late) |
| Cause | Medical illness, drugs, infection | Neurodegeneration |
| Treatment | Treat underlying cause | Supportive, disease-modifying |

**Causes (I WATCH DEATH):**
- **I**nfection, **W**ithdrawal (alcohol, benzos)
- **A**cute metabolic, **T**oxins/drugs
- **C**NS pathology, **H**ypoxia
- **D**eficiencies (B12, thiamine), **E**nvironmental
- **A**cute vascular, **T**rauma, **H**eavy metals, **H**eat stroke

**Management:**
- **Nonpharmacologic first**: Frequent reorientation, cognitive stimulation, sleep hygiene, early mobilization, vision/hearing aids, hydration, bowel/bladder care
- **Avoid restraints**
- **Pharmacologic (only if severe agitation threatening safety)**: Low-dose haloperidol or atypical antipsychotics (quetiapine); avoid benzodiazepines (except alcohol/benzo withdrawal)
- **Treat underlying cause**

**Prevention:** [^358^]
- Multicomponent nonpharmacologic interventions (Hospital Elder Life Program - HELP)
- Maintain orientation, ensure sensory input, promote sleep, early mobilization, adequate hydration, prevent constipation

**Delirium Superimposed on Dementia:** [^358^]
- Challenging to diagnose due to overlapping features
- Key: Establish baseline cognitive status from family/caregivers
- Screen for attention deficits as marker of delirium
- UB-2© has 96% sensitivity in persons with dementia [^358^]

### 6.4 Urinary Incontinence

**Clinical Overview:** Involuntary urine leakage affects up to 50% of nursing home residents and significantly impacts quality of life. Five main types exist. [^360^]

**Types and Management:** [^360^] [^364^]

| Type | Mechanism | Treatment |
|------|-----------|-----------|
| **Stress** | Urethral sphincter weakness; leakage with cough/sneeze/laugh | Pelvic floor exercises (Kegels), behavioral therapy, weight loss, pessaries, alpha-agonists, vaginal estrogen, surgical slings |
| **Urge** | Detrusor overactivity; sudden urge, frequency, nocturia | Bladder training, timed voiding, antimuscarinics (oxybutynin, tolterodine, solifenacin), beta-3 agonists (mirabegron, vibegron), onabotulinum toxin |
| **Overflow** | Urinary retention, detrusor underactivity; dribbling, weak stream | Intermittent catheterization, alpha-blockers (tamsulosin), bethanechol, relieve obstruction |
| **Functional** | Physical/cognitive impairment preventing toilet access | Address underlying cause, scheduled toileting, mobility aids, clothing modification |
| **Mixed** | Combination of types | Treat predominant symptom |

**Initial Evaluation:**
- Detailed history (timing, triggers, severity using pad count/voiding diary)
- Physical exam (pelvic exam, prostate assessment, neurologic exam)
- Urinalysis (rule out infection, hematuria, glycosuria)
- Post-void residual (PVR) measurement (>200 mL suggests retention)
- Assessment for reversible causes (DIAPPERS: Delirium, Infection, Atrophic vaginitis, Pharmaceuticals, Psychological, Excess urine output, Restricted mobility, Stool impaction)

**Pharmacologic Considerations in Elderly:** [^368^]
- Antimuscarinics: Caution with constipation, glaucoma, cognitive impairment (anticholinergic burden)
- Beta-3 agonists (mirabegron): Caution with hypertension
- Vaginal estrogen: For atrophic vaginitis-associated incontinence
- Avoid desmopressin in elderly (hyponatremia risk - Beers Criteria)

**Red Flags:** Gross hematuria, pelvic mass, neurologic deficits, recurrent UTIs, PVR >200 mL, pelvic pain, new onset incontinence with back pain (possible cauda equina)

### 6.5 Malnutrition

**Clinical Overview:** Malnutrition in older adults is associated with complications, functional decline, and increased mortality. It is often insidious and undetected. [^367^]

**Mini Nutritional Assessment (MNA):** [^362^] [^366^] [^367^]
- **MNA-SF (Short Form)**: 6 questions; preferred for clinical use; takes <5 minutes
  - Food intake decline, weight loss, mobility, psychological stress/acute disease, dementia/depression, BMI
  - Calf circumference may substitute for BMI
- **Scoring**:
  - **12-14**: Normal nutritional status
  - **8-11**: At risk of malnutrition
  - **0-7**: Malnourished [^367^]
- **Full MNA**: 18-item questionnaire for comprehensive assessment

**Validation:**
- Sensitivity 89%, specificity 82% [^367^]
- Used in >2,400 clinical studies worldwide [^363^]
- Included in 22+ expert clinical practice guidelines [^369^]
- Screening frequency: Quarterly for institutionalized; yearly for community-dwelling

**Interventions:**
- Dietary counseling and supplementation
- High-protein, nutrient-dense foods
- Oral nutritional supplements between meals
- Treatment of underlying causes (depression, dysphagia, dental problems, medication side effects)
- Monitor weight regularly

### 6.6 Pressure Injuries

**Clinical Overview:** Pressure injuries result from ischemia due to sustained pressure over bony prominences. Prevention is the highest level of care. [^374^] [^376^] [^386^]

**Staging (NPIAP):** [^376^]

| Stage | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| **Stage 1** | Non-blanchable erythema of intact skin; may be painful, firm, or warmer/cooler |
| **Stage 2** | Partial-thickness skin loss; shallow open ulcer or blister; presents as abrasion or shallow crater |
| **Stage 3** | Full-thickness skin loss; subcutaneous fat visible; deep crater; may have undermining |
| **Stage 4** | Full-thickness tissue loss; exposed muscle, tendon, or bone; undermining or sinus tracts |
| **Unstageable** | Full-thickness tissue loss; wound base covered by slough or eschar; true depth unknown |
| **Deep tissue pressure injury (DTPI)** | Persistent non-blanchable deep red/maroon/purple discoloration of intact or non-intact skin |

**Prevention:** [^374^] [^376^]
- Risk assessment: Braden Scale (sensory perception, moisture, activity, mobility, nutrition, friction/shear)
- Regular repositioning (every 2-4 hours depending on surface)
- Pressure redistribution surfaces (low-air-loss, air-fluidized beds)
- Skin care: Keep clean and dry; moisturize; protect from moisture (incontinence-associated dermatitis)
- Nutrition optimization (MNA screening, adequate protein intake)
- Heel elevation to offload pressure
- **No donut cushions** (occlude blood flow) [^376^]

**Management:** [^374^]
- Offload pressure from affected area
- Debridement of devitalized tissue (except dry intact eschar on heels without signs of infection)
- Wound care: Moisture balance; foam silicone dressings recommended [^374^]
- Infection management: Culture if suspected; antibiotics for significant cellulitis
- Nutritional support essential for healing
- Consider wound care specialist referral

### 6.7 Sarcopenia

**Clinical Overview:** Sarcopenia is a progressive, generalized skeletal muscle disorder associated with increased risk of falls, fractures, physical disability, and mortality. [^361^] [^372^]

**EWGSOP2 Definition (2018):** [^361^]
- **Probable sarcopenia**: Low muscle strength (Criterion 1)
- **Confirmed sarcopenia**: Low muscle strength + low muscle quantity/quality (Criterion 2)
- **Severe sarcopenia**: Low muscle strength + low muscle quantity/quality + low physical performance (Criteria 1, 2, and 3)

**Screening and Diagnosis (F-A-C-S Pathway):** [^361^]

| Step | Tool | Cut-off |
|------|------|---------|
| **F**ind cases | SARC-F questionnaire | Score >=4 indicates risk |
| **A**ssess | Grip strength (dynamometer) or chair stand | Men <27 kg, Women <16 kg (grip) |
| **C**onfirm | DXA (preferred), BIA, CT, MRI | Low ASM |
| **S**everity | Gait speed, SPPB, TUG | Gait speed <=0.8 m/s indicates severe |

**SARC-F Questions:** [^361^]
1. Strength difficulty lifting/carrying 10 lbs
2. Assistance walking across room
3. Difficulty transferring chair to bed
4. Difficulty climbing 10 stairs
5. Number of falls in past year

**Management:**
- **Resistance exercise**: Progressive, individualized strength training
- **Protein supplementation**: 1.0-1.2 g/kg/day (higher if acute illness: 1.2-1.5 g/kg/day)
- **Vitamin D supplementation** if deficient
- **Multimodal interventions**: Exercise + nutrition optimization
- Address underlying causes (inflammation, endocrine disorders, medication effects)

## 7. Polypharmacy

### 7.1 Beers Criteria (2023 AGS Update)

**Clinical Overview:** The AGS Beers Criteria is a widely used list of potentially inappropriate medications (PIMs) for adults >=65 years. It is intended to improve medication selection, educate clinicians and patients, and serve as a tool for evaluating quality of care. [^350^] [^354^] [^359^]

**Five Sections:** [^350^]
1. PIMs that are potentially inappropriate in **most older adults**
2. PIMs that are potentially inappropriate in older adults with **specific diseases/syndromes**
3. Medications to **use with caution** in older adults
4. Medication **combinations** to avoid (drug-drug interactions)
5. Medications with **potentially clinically important drug-disease interactions**

**Key Examples of PIMs:** [^350^]

| Category | Examples | Concerns in Elderly |
|----------|----------|---------------------|
| First-generation antihistamines | Diphenhydramine, chlorpheniramine, hydroxyzine | Anticholinergic, confusion, cognitive impairment, delirium, constipation, urinary retention |
| Antipsychotics (long-term) | Haloperidol, risperidone, quetiapine | Increased mortality, stroke, cognitive decline, falls |
| Benzodiazepines | Diazepam, lorazepam, alprazolam | Cognitive impairment, delirium, falls, fractures |
| Non-benzodiazepine hypnotics | Zolpidem, eszopiclone, zaleplon | Similar to benzos; confusion, falls |
| Tricyclic antidepressants | Amitriptyline, nortriptyline | Anticholinergic, orthostatic hypotension, cardiac arrhythmias |
| NSAIDs | Ibuprofen, naproxen (chronic) | GI bleeding, renal impairment, cardiovascular events |
| Opioids (avoid meperidine) | Meperidine | Neurotoxicity, delirium; use morphine or other alternatives |
| Anticholinergics (total burden) | Oxybutynin, tolterodine, TCAs, antihistamines | Cumulative anticholinergic burden increases delirium, constipation, urinary retention risk |
| Sulfonylureas (long-acting) | Glyburide/glipizide | Hypoglycemia risk; prefer glipizide or non-sulfonylurea agents |
| Alpha-1 blockers | Doxazosin, prazosin | Orthostatic hypotension, falls |

**2023 Key Updates:** [^354^]
- Updated for new evidence since 2019
- New criteria added for several medication classes
- Modifications to existing criteria
- Formatting changes to enhance usability
- Apply to adults >=65 in ambulatory, acute, and institutionalized settings (except hospice/end-of-life)

### 7.2 STOPP/START Criteria

**Clinical Overview:** STOPP (Screening Tool of Older Persons' Prescriptions) identifies potentially inappropriate medications; START (Screening Tool to Alert doctors to Right Treatment) identifies potential prescribing omissions. [^375^] [^378^] [^381^]

**STOPP/START Version 3 (2023):** [^375^] [^381^]
- **190 criteria total**: 133 STOPP criteria + 57 START criteria
- 66.7% increase from Version 2 (114 criteria in 2015)
- Validated by Delphi consensus process with 11 European geriatric pharmacotherapy experts
- Organized by physiological systems for ease of use

**STOPP Criteria Categories:**
- Cardiovascular system PIMs
- Coagulation system PIMs
- CNS PIMs (including anticholinergic burden, falls risk)
- Gastrointestinal PIMs
- Respiratory system PIMs
- Musculoskeletal PIMs (including long-term NSAIDs, corticosteroids)
- Urogenital system PIMs
- Endocrine PIMs
- Drugs that adversely affect patients prone to falls
- Analgesic PIMs
- Duplicate drug class prescription

**START Criteria Categories:**
- Cardiovascular system PPOs (e.g., ACE inhibitors with heart failure, statins with CV disease)
- Respiratory system PPOs (inhaled corticosteroids for moderate-severe COPD)
- CNS PPOs (antidepressants with moderate-severe depression)
- Gastrointestinal PPOs (PPI with chronic NSAIDs)
- Endocrine PPOs (metformin for type 2 diabetes)
- Musculoskeletal PPOs (calcium/vitamin D with osteoporosis)

**Comparison to Beers:** [^378^]
- STOPP/START detects both overprescribing AND prescribing omissions
- STOPP criteria more predictive of adverse drug events than Beers criteria
- Both validated, but STOPP/START designed for broader clinical application
- STOPP/START v3 approved by NICE, British National Formulary [^381^]

### 7.3 Deprescribing Principles

**Framework for Deprescribing:**
1. **Identify potentially inappropriate medications**: Use Beers Criteria and STOPP/START
2. **Assess overall medication burden**: Total number of medications, regimen complexity
3. **Determine benefit vs. harm**: Consider remaining life expectancy, time to benefit, goals of care
4. **Identify medications for deprescribing**: Prioritize by harm potential, benefit, patient preference
5. **Taper safely**: Gradual dose reduction to avoid withdrawal/rebound
6. **Monitor**: Follow for adverse effects of stopping or continuing

**Medications Commonly Targeted for Deprescribing:**
- Proton pump inhibitors (chronic use without indication)
- Antihypertensives (overtreatment, orthostatic hypotension)
- Hypoglycemic agents (overtreatment of diabetes in elderly)
- Statins (limited life expectancy, >75 years)
- Anticholinergics (cognitive impairment, falls)
- Benzodiazepines (gradual taper over weeks to months)
- Antipsychotics (especially for behavioral symptoms of dementia)
- Opioids (chronic non-cancer pain)

### 7.4 Medication Reconciliation and ADE Prevention

**Medication Reconciliation:**
- Obtain complete medication list at every transition of care
- Compare current medications to newly ordered medications
- Identify discrepancies, duplications, interactions
- Verify allergies and adverse drug reactions
- Include OTC, supplements, and herbal products

**Adverse Drug Event (ADE) Prevention:**
- Monitor renal and hepatic function
- Dose adjustments for age-related pharmacokinetic changes
- Assess anticholinergic burden (cumulative risk)
- Watch for drug-drug interactions
- Patient/caregiver education on medication purpose and side effects
- Regular medication review (every 6 months minimum for polypharmacy)

### 7.5 Drug-Disease Interactions in Elderly

**Key Interactions to Monitor:**
- Anticholinergics + dementia/cognitive impairment: Worsen cognition
- NSAIDs + CKD/heart failure: Worsen renal function, fluid retention
- Opioids + falls risk: Increase sedation, respiratory depression
- Alpha-blockers + syncope/falls: Orthostatic hypotension
- Sulfonylureas + recurrent hypoglycemia: Consider deprescribing
- Corticosteroids + diabetes/osteoporosis: Worsen glycemic control, increase fracture risk
- Benzodiazepines + COPD: Respiratory depression

## 8. Cognitive Impairment in the Elderly

### 8.1 Screening Tools

**Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA):**
- 10-minute screening tool; score 0-30
- **Cut-off**: <26 suggests cognitive impairment (adjust for education: add 1 point if <12 years education)
- Domains: Executive function, naming, memory, attention, language, abstraction, delayed recall, orientation
- **More sensitive than MMSE for MCI**; includes executive function testing
- Available in multiple languages; free online with registration
- Copyrighted but free for clinical use

**Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE):**
- 5-10 minutes; score 0-30
- **Cut-offs** (education-adjusted): [^351^]
  - No high school: <26.5 (MCI), <26.5 (dementia)
  - High school: <28.5 (MCI), <26.5 (dementia)
- Domains: Orientation, registration, attention/calculation, recall, language, construction
- **Limitations**: Ceiling effect in mild cognitive impairment; less sensitive to executive function
- Now copyrighted; licensing fees may apply

**SLUMS (Saint Louis University Mental Status Examination):**
- 10-minute screening tool; score 0-30
- **Cut-offs** (education-adjusted): [^351^] [^356^]
  - No high school: <27.5 (MCI), <27.5 (dementia)
  - High school: <29.5 (MCI), <27.5 (dementia)
- Domains: Orientation, memory, attention, executive function (clock drawing), naming, verbal fluency
- **Free to use**; more sensitive than MMSE for MCI [^356^]
- Includes story comprehension and mathematical reasoning [^351^]

**Comparison:** [^351^] [^356^]
- All three tools assess similar cognitive domains but with different sensitivity profiles
- SLUMS and MoCA may be more sensitive for detecting mild cognitive impairment than MMSE
- MMSE may be more sensitive for detecting progression to dementia [^351^]
- Low agreement between tools for any given individual [^351^]
- **Recommendation**: Use one screening tool consistently; consider combining tests if concerns persist

### 8.2 Dementia Types and Management

**Alzheimer's Disease (60-70% of cases):**
- **Presentation**: Gradual onset, progressive memory loss (episodic memory first), word-finding difficulty, visuospatial deficits, impaired judgment
- **Pathology**: Amyloid plaques, neurofibrillary tangles
- **Management**: 
  - **Cholinesterase inhibitors**: Donepezil (Aricept), rivastigmine (Exelon), galantamine (Razadyne) - mild to moderate
  - **Memantine** (Namenda): Moderate to severe; NMDA receptor antagonist
  - **Combination therapy**: Donepezil + memantine for moderate-severe
  - Nonpharmacologic: Cognitive stimulation, exercise, social engagement, caregiver support

**Vascular Dementia:**
- **Presentation**: Stepwise decline, executive dysfunction prominent, history of stroke/TIA, vascular risk factors
- **Imaging**: White matter changes, infarcts on MRI
- **Management**: Aggressive vascular risk factor modification (BP, lipids, diabetes, anticoagulation for AF)
- Cholinesterase inhibitors may provide modest benefit

**Lewy Body Dementia:**
- **Presentation**: Fluctuating cognition, visual hallucinations, parkinsonism, REM sleep behavior disorder, autonomic dysfunction, sensitivity to antipsychotics
- **Management**: Cholinesterase inhibitors (rivastigmine preferred); levodopa for parkinsonism; avoid antipsychotics (severe sensitivity); melatonin for RBD

**Frontotemporal Dementia:**
- **Presentation**: Early behavioral changes (disinhibition, apathy) or language deficits (primary progressive aphasia); relatively preserved memory early
- **Age of onset**: Typically younger (45-65 years)
- **Management**: Primarily supportive; no FDA-approved disease-modifying therapies; SSRIs may help behavioral symptoms

**Mixed Dementia:**
- Combination of Alzheimer's and vascular pathology; common in oldest old
- Management combines approaches from both

### 8.3 Caregiver Support and Respite

**Caregiver Burden:**
- High rates of depression, anxiety, physical illness among dementia caregivers
- Evaluate caregiver stress and health at each visit
- Assess safety of caregiving situation

**Support Strategies:**
- **Caregiver education**: Disease course, behavioral management techniques
- **Respite care**: Adult day programs, in-home respite, short-term residential care
- **Support groups**: Alzheimer Association support groups
- **Technology assistance**: Medication reminders, GPS tracking, home monitoring
- **Caregiver respite benefits**: Medicare covers some respite in hospice; Medicaid waiver programs vary by state
- **Counseling**: Individual or family therapy for caregiver distress
- **Long-term care planning**: Adult day care, assisted living, memory care, nursing home

**Safety Assessment:**
- Driving evaluation and eventual cessation plan
- Wandering risk assessment
- Home safety modifications
- Financial management oversight
- Medication management
- Firearm safety (removal recommended)

## 9. Functional Assessment

### 9.1 ADLs and IADLs

**Activities of Daily Living (ADLs) - Basic Self-Care:**

| Activity | Assessment Question |
|----------|-------------------|
| **Bathing** | Can the patient bathe independently? |
| **Dressing** | Can the patient select clothes and dress independently? |
| **Toileting** | Can the patient use toilet, clean, manage clothing? |
| **Transferring** | Can the patient move between bed, chair, toilet? |
| **Continence** | Can the patient manage bladder and bowel function? |
| **Feeding** | Can the patient eat independently (not necessarily prepare food)? |

- **Katz ADL Scale**: Scores 0-6; lower scores indicate greater dependence
- **Barthel Index**: Scores 0-100; widely used in rehabilitation settings [^342^]

**Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADLs) - Complex Tasks:**

| Activity | Assessment Question |
|----------|-------------------|
| **Phone use** | Can the patient make and receive calls? |
| **Shopping** | Can the patient purchase groceries and necessities? |
| **Food preparation** | Can the patient prepare meals? |
| **Housekeeping** | Can the patient maintain home? |
| **Laundry** | Can the patient wash clothes? |
| **Transportation** | Can the patient drive or use public transit? |
| **Medication management** | Can the patient take medications correctly? |
| **Financial management** | Can the patient manage money, pay bills? |

- **Lawton IADL Scale**: Scores 0-8 (0-27 in some versions) [^342^]
- Loss of IADLs typically precedes loss of ADLs in progressive conditions

**Functional Assessment Significance:** [^383^]
- Transition from independent living to requiring assistance with >=3 ADLs marks sharp increase in care burden and hospitalization risk
- Functional status guides care planning urgency [^383^]

### 9.2 Advance Care Planning

**Clinical Overview:** Advance care planning involves discussing and documenting patient preferences for future medical care, particularly important when patients lose decision-making capacity. [^383^]

**Triggers for ACP Discussion:** [^383^]
- Newly diagnosed serious illness (stage IV cancer, ESRD, advanced dementia)
- Functional decline (>=3 ADLs lost)
- Pre-surgical planning
- Early cognitive decline (surrogate identification)
- Transition between care settings

**Key Components:**
- **Advance Directives**: Living will, healthcare power of attorney
- **Physician Orders for Life-Sustaining Treatment (POLST/MOLST)**: For seriously ill patients; portable medical orders
- **Code status**: Full code, DNR, DNR-DNI
- **Goals of care**: Understanding what matters most to the patient
- **Surrogate decision-maker**: Healthcare proxy identification
- **Mental capacity assessment**: Document decision-making capacity

### 9.3 Palliative Care Integration

**Palliative Care Principles:**
- Relief of symptoms and suffering at any stage of serious illness
- Concurrent with disease-modifying treatment (NOT only end-of-life)
- Interdisciplinary approach (physician, nurse, social work, chaplaincy)
- Focus on quality of life, symptom management, psychosocial support

**Hospice Eligibility (Medicare):**
- Prognosis of <=6 months if disease runs natural course
- Decline in functional status, weight loss, increasing symptoms
- Physician certification required
- Covered services: Nursing, medications, equipment, respite, bereavement support

**Symptom Management in Palliative Care:**
- Pain: WHO analgesic ladder
- Dyspnea: Opioids, low-dose anxiolytics, positioning, oxygen
- Constipation: Aggressive bowel regimen
- Nausea: Targeted antiemetics based on mechanism
- Delirium: Nonpharmacologic first; antipsychotics for severe agitation
- Terminal secretions: Anticholinergics (glycopyrrolate, hyoscyamine)

### 9.4 Goals of Care Discussions

**Framework:**
1. **Ask**: What do you understand about your illness? What are your hopes and worries?
2. **Tell**: Provide prognostic information in understandable terms
3. **Align**: Connect medical recommendations to patient's goals and values
4. **Recommend**: Offer recommendations based on goals
5. **Document**: Record preferences; update periodically

**Communication Skills:**
- Use "wish" statements ("I wish things were different")
- Avoid medical jargon; use "serious illness" language
- Respond to emotion (NURSE: Name, Understand, Respect, Support, Explore)
- SPIKES protocol for delivering bad news
- Allow silence; don't rush decisions

## 10. Elder Abuse

### 10.1 Types of Elder Abuse

**Physical Abuse:**
- Infliction of physical pain or injury
- Signs: Unexplained bruises, fractures, burns, restraint marks
- Pattern of injuries inconsistent with explanation

**Emotional/Psychological Abuse:**
- Verbal assaults, threats, harassment, intimidation, humiliation
- Signs: Depression, withdrawal, anxiety, fearfulness around specific person

**Sexual Abuse:**
- Non-consensual sexual contact of any kind
- Signs: Bruising to genitals, unexplained STIs, torn clothing

**Financial Exploitation:**
- Illegal or improper use of funds, property, or assets
- Signs: Unexplained withdrawals, missing valuables, sudden will changes, unpaid bills despite adequate resources

**Neglect:**
- Failure to provide basic needs (food, shelter, medical care, hygiene)
- Can be caregiver neglect or self-neglect
- Signs: Poor hygiene, malnutrition, dehydration, untreated medical conditions, unsafe living conditions

### 10.2 Screening

**Elder Abuse Suspicion Index (EASI):** [^379^] [^380^]
- 6-item tool completed by healthcare professional
- Assesses risk for neglect, verbal, psychological, emotional, financial, physical, and sexual abuse over 12 months
- Administration time: ~2 minutes
- **Sensitivity: 0.77; Specificity: 0.44** [^380^]
- Validated in family practice and ambulatory care settings

**Other Screening Tools:** [^380^]
- **H-S/EAST (Hwalek-Sengstock)**: 6-item self-report or interview
- **VASS (Vulnerability to Abuse Screening Scale)**: 12-item self-report
- **BASE (Brief Elder Screen)**, **CASE (Caregiver Abuse Screen)**, **EAI (Elder Assessment Instrument)**

**Red Flags:**
- Unexplained injuries in various stages of healing
- Frequent ER visits or physician changes
- Poor hygiene or malnutrition despite adequate resources
- Social isolation, caregiver substance abuse or mental illness
- Conflicting accounts of injury from patient and caregiver
- Patient fearful or withdrawn around caregiver
- Missing medications or overdosing

### 10.3 Mandatory Reporting

**Requirements:** [^379^]
- All 50 states have elder abuse reporting laws
- Healthcare providers are mandatory reporters in most jurisdictions
- Report to Adult Protective Services (APS) or designated state agency
- Some states require reporting for patients in long-term care to facility licensing agency
- Federal reporting: Nursing facilities must report to State Survey and Certification Agency [^379^]
- **Resources**:
  - National Adult Protective Services Association: www.napsa-now.org
  - Eldercare Locator: 1-800-677-1116
  - National Center on Elder Abuse: ncea.acl.gov [^379^]

**Documentation:**
- Document concerns objectively (quotes, observations, physical findings)
- Photograph injuries with consent
- Maintain confidentiality while fulfilling reporting obligations
- Follow-up to ensure investigation occurs

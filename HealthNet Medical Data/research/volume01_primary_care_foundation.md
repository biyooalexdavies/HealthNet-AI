# 1. Emergency Recognition in Primary Care

## 1.1 Chest Pain Protocols

### Clinical Overview

Chest pain is one of the most common and potentially life-threatening presentations in primary care. The differential diagnosis includes acute coronary syndrome (ACS), pulmonary embolism (PE), aortic dissection, pericarditis, pneumothorax, and esophageal rupture. Rapid identification and risk stratification are essential for optimal outcomes.

### Acute Coronary Syndrome (ACS)

**Definition:** ACS encompasses ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), and unstable angina. It results from acute disruption of coronary blood flow leading to myocardial ischemia or necrosis. [^37^]

**Key Features Distinguishing ACS:**
- Chest pressure, tightness, or heaviness (not sharp or pleuritic)
- Radiation to arm, jaw, neck, or back
- Associated with diaphoresis, nausea, dyspnea
- Precipitated by exertion or stress
- Not reproducible by palpation
- Duration typically >20 minutes for MI

**Immediate Management (Office-Based):** [^37^]
1. **Call 911 immediately** for suspected STEMI
2. Obtain 12-lead ECG within 10 minutes of presentation
3. Check vital signs and oxygen saturation
4. Administer aspirin 325 mg (if no allergy) — chewable preferred
5. Administer sublingual nitroglycerin 0.4 mg every 5 minutes x 3 if SBP >90 mmHg
6. Establish IV access
7. Transport to PCI-capable center

**AHA/ACC Clinical Categories for ACS:** [^37^]
- **STEMI:** Complete coronary occlusion requiring emergent reperfusion (primary PCI or thrombolysis if PCI unavailable within 120 minutes)
- **NSTEMI/UA:** Incomplete occlusion; risk stratification required with serial troponins

**Oxygen Therapy:** Provide oxygen only if SpO2 <90% or signs of respiratory distress. Routine oxygen is not recommended in normoxic patients. [^37^]

**Contraindications to Nitroglycerin:**
- Systolic BP <90 mmHg
- Right ventricular infarction
- Recent PDE5 inhibitor use (sildenafil/vardenafil within 24 hours; tadalafil within 48 hours)

### Pulmonary Embolism (PE)

**Definition:** PE is obstruction of pulmonary arteries by thrombus, most commonly originating from deep vein thrombosis (DVT). Mortality is significant without treatment. [^34^]

**2026 AHA/ACC Acute PE Clinical Categories:** [^34^]

| Category | Description | Management |
|----------|-------------|------------|
| **A** | Asymptomatic PE | Discharge home from ED |
| **B** | Symptomatic, low clinical severity (sPESI = 0) | Early hospital discharge |
| **C** | Symptomatic, elevated severity score (elevated biomarkers and/or RV dysfunction) | Hospitalization |
| **D** | Incipient cardiopulmonary failure | Hospitalization, consider advanced therapies |
| **E** | Cardiopulmonary failure (persistent hypotension) | ICU, PERT activation, advanced therapies |

**Clinical Features:**
- Sudden onset dyspnea
- Pleuritic chest pain
- Tachycardia, tachypnea
- Syncope (especially with massive PE)
- Signs of DVT (unilateral leg swelling)
- Hypoxemia

**Initial Workup:**
- Wells Score or Revised Geneva Score for pre-test probability
- D-dimer (only if low/intermediate probability)
- CT pulmonary angiography (CTPA) for high probability or positive D-dimer
- ECG: S1Q3T3 pattern (uncommon but specific), sinus tachycardia, right heart strain

**Immediate Management:** [^34^]
1. Anticoagulation (LMWH preferred over UFH if not thrombolyzing)
2. DOACs recommended over VKAs unless contraindicated (apixaban, rivaroxaban)
3. Consider systemic thrombolysis for Category E with hemodynamic instability
4. PERT (PE Response Team) activation for severe cases

**Key 2026 Guideline Changes:** [^34^]
- LMWH is recommended over UFH for initial parenteral anticoagulation
- DOACs recommended over VKAs unless contraindicated
- Extended anticoagulation recommended for first PE without major reversible risk factor
- PERTs recommended to improve timeliness of care

### Aortic Dissection

**Definition:** A tear in the intimal layer of the aorta creating a true and false lumen. Mortality increases 1-2% per hour for ascending aortic dissection left untreated. [^53^]

**Classification:** [^53^]
- **Stanford Type A:** Involves ascending aorta (surgical emergency)
- **Stanford Type B:** Does not involve ascending aorta (usually medical management unless complicated)
- **DeBakey Type I:** Ascending + arch + descending
- **DeBakey Type II:** Ascending only
- **DeBakey Type III:** Descending only

**New 2024 TEM Classification:** [^59^] Type-Entry-Malperfusion classification provides prognostic and therapeutic stratification.

**Key Features ("Red Flag" Pain Characteristics):** [^53^] [^59^]
- Sudden, severe "tearing" or "ripping" chest pain
- Pain maximal at onset (unlike MI which builds)
- Interscapular back pain
- Migration of pain as dissection extends
- Blood pressure differential >20 mmHg between arms
- New neurologic deficit
- Pulse deficit
- New aortic regurgitation murmur
- Hypotension/shock

**Initial Management:**
1. Immediate IV beta-blockade (esmolol or labetalol) to reduce shear stress
2. IV opioid analgesia
3. Blood pressure control (goal SBP <120 mmHg if tolerated)
4. Emergent surgery for Type A (mortality 1-2% per hour)
5. Type B: medical management unless complicated (malperfusion, rupture, refractory pain)

**2024 ESC Guidelines - High-Risk Features Requiring Surgery:** [^59^]
- Surgery indicated for ascending aorta >=55 mm (both bicuspid and tricuspid valves) — Class I/B
- Bicuspid valve with root phenotype: surgery >50 mm — Class I/B
- Low surgical risk patients: surgery considered at >52 mm — Class IIa/B

### Acute Pericarditis

**Definition:** Inflammation of the pericardium, most commonly idiopathic or viral. [^67^] [^68^]

**Diagnostic Criteria (2 of 4 required):** [^68^]
1. Chest pain (>85-90%) — sharp, pleuritic, improved by sitting up/leaning forward
2. Pericardial friction rub (<33%)
3. ECG changes (up to 60%): diffuse concave ST elevation with reciprocal ST depression in aVR
4. Pericardial effusion (up to 60%, usually mild)

**ECG Evolution (4 Stages):** [^67^] [^68^]
- Stage I: Diffuse concave ST elevation, PR depression, reciprocal ST depression in aVR
- Stage II: Normalization of ST and PR changes
- Stage III: Widespread T-wave inversions
- Stage IV: Normalization of T waves

**Key Distinguishing Features from MI:**
- ST elevation is diffuse and concave (not convex/localized as in MI)
- Reciprocal ST depression only in aVR (not in other leads as in MI)
- No Q waves
- PR segment depression present

**High-Risk Features (require admission):** [^67^] [^68^]
- Fever >38 degrees C
- Subacute course (>several days)
- Large effusion (>20 mm)
- Cardiac tamponade physiology
- Myopericarditis (elevated troponin)
- Failure to respond to NSAIDs within 7 days
- Immunosuppression, trauma, oral anticoagulation

**Treatment:** [^68^]
- NSAIDs (aspirin 750-1000 mg q8h OR ibuprofen 600-800 mg q8h) + PPI gastroprotection
- Colchicine 0.5 mg BID (reduces recurrence by ~40%)
- Duration: until symptoms resolve + CRP normalizes (typically 1-2 weeks), then taper
- Activity restriction: minimum 1 month, HR <100 bpm until remission
- 2025 ESC defines remission as: full symptom regression, normalization of CRP, ECG, and imaging

### Chest Pain Differential Diagnosis Summary

| Condition | Pain Quality | Location | Key Distinguishing Features |
|-----------|-------------|----------|----------------------------|
| **STEMI/NSTEMI** | Pressure, squeezing | Retrosternal, radiation to arm/jaw | ECG changes, troponin elevation |
| **PE** | Pleuritic, sharp | Substernal or lateral | Dyspnea, tachycardia, DVT signs |
| **Aortic Dissection** | Tearing, ripping | Chest + interscapular back | BP differential, pulse deficit |
| **Pericarditis** | Sharp, pleuritic | Retrosternal | Relieved by sitting forward, friction rub, diffuse ST elevation |
| **Pneumothorax** | Sudden, sharp | Unilateral | Decreased breath sounds, hyperresonance |
| **Esophageal Spasm** | Squeezing | Retrosternal | Related to swallowing, nitroglycerin-responsive |
| **Costochondritis** | Aching, sharp | Localized, reproducible | Reproducible by palpation |
| **GERD** | Burning | Retrosternal | Worse after meals, supine |

### Red Flags in Chest Pain (Immediate Emergency Activation)

1. **STEMI on ECG:** ST elevation, new LBBB
2. **Hemodynamic instability:** SBP <90 mmHg, altered mental status
3. **Aortic dissection features:** Tearing pain, BP differential, pulse deficit
4. **Massive PE:** Syncope, hypotension, severe hypoxemia
5. **Cardiac tamponade:** Hypotension, JVD, muffled heart sounds (Beck's triad)
6. **Tension pneumothorax:** Severe dyspnea, tracheal deviation, hypotension

## 1.2 Stroke Recognition

### Clinical Overview

Stroke is a medical emergency requiring rapid recognition and treatment. Time-sensitive interventions (IV thrombolysis within 4.5 hours; mechanical thrombectomy within 6-24 hours for selected patients) dramatically improve outcomes. Primary care providers play a critical role in initial recognition and emergency activation. [^3^] [^6^]

### FAST and BE-FAST Screening Tools

**FAST Scale (Original):**
- **F**ace drooping
- **A**rm weakness
- **S**peech difficulty
- **T**ime to call emergency services

**BE-FAST Scale (Enhanced):** [^6^]
- **B**alance problems (new onset ataxia, vertigo, dizziness)
- **E**yes (sudden vision changes, diplopia)
- **F**ace drooping
- **A**rm weakness
- **S**peech difficulty
- **T**ime to call emergency services

**Evidence for BE-FAST:** [^6^]
- Studies show BE-FAST identifies posterior circulation strokes missed by FAST
- Implementation in pre-hospital and hospital triage settings improved stroke team activation
- Propensity-score matched studies demonstrated improved door-to-imaging and door-to-needle times
- Training of emergency nurses, primary care physicians, and EMS paramedics is essential

**BE-FAST Implementation Protocol:** [^6^]
1. Deploy in pre-hospital and hospital triage settings
2. Train personnel through structured workshops and simulation
3. Any positive screening result prompts immediate stroke team activation
4. Mandatory documentation of BE-FAST screening time and first positive sign
5. Monthly audits and refresher sessions for compliance

### Ischemic vs. Hemorrhagic Stroke

| Feature | Ischemic Stroke | Hemorrhagic Stroke |
|---------|----------------|--------------------|
| **Percentage** | ~85% of all strokes | ~15% of all strokes |
| **Onset** | Sudden, may awaken from sleep | Sudden, often with headache |
| **Associated headache** | Less common | Common ("thunderclap") |
| **Level of consciousness** | Usually preserved early | Often decreased |
| **NIHSS** | Variable | Often higher |
| **Imaging** | Non-contrast CT: early subtle changes; MRI: DWI positive | Non-contrast CT: hyperdense lesion |
| **Key treatment** | IV tPA within 4.5h; thrombectomy 6-24h | Blood pressure control; neurosurgery if indicated |

**NIH Stroke Scale (NIHSS):** [^6^]
- 0: No stroke symptoms
- 1-4: Minor stroke
- 5-15: Moderate stroke
- 16-20: Moderate-severe stroke
- 21-42: Severe stroke

### Initial Management Protocol

1. **Activate EMS immediately** — "last known well" time is critical
2. **ABCs** — airway protection if GCS <8
3. **Check glucose** — treat hypoglycemia immediately
4. **NPO status** — aspiration risk
5. **Blood pressure management:**
   - If candidate for thrombolysis: goal <185/110 mmHg
   - If not thrombolyzing: permissive hypertension unless >220/120 mmHg
6. **Immediate CT head** — must distinguish ischemic from hemorrhagic
7. **Labs:** CBC, coagulation studies, electrolytes, troponin

### Thrombolysis Eligibility (Key Criteria)

- Age >=18 years
- Clinical diagnosis of ischemic stroke
- Time of symptom onset <4.5 hours (known "last known well")
- Measurable neurologic deficit (NIHSS >=1)
- No hemorrhage on CT
- BP <185/110 mmHg
- Not on anticoagulation or INR <1.7
- Blood glucose >50 mg/dL
- Platelets >100,000

## 1.3 Severe Allergic Reactions / Anaphylaxis

### Definition

Anaphylaxis is a severe, potentially life-threatening systemic allergic reaction characterized by rapid onset and involvement of multiple organ systems (skin/mucosa, respiratory, cardiovascular, gastrointestinal). [^13^]

### 2023 AAAAI Practice Parameter Updates: [^13^]

**Diagnostic Criteria (meeting any ONE criterion):**
1. Acute onset with skin/mucosal involvement AND either respiratory compromise or hypotension
2. Two or more organ systems after exposure to likely allergen
3. Hypotension after known allergen exposure

**Note:** 2024 studies found EMS protocols vary significantly — only 50% include GI symptoms, 40% include neurologic manifestations. [^11^]

### Immediate Management (First-Line)

**Epinephrine is the FIRST-LINE treatment — no contraindications in anaphylaxis.** [^13^]

**Epinephrine Dosing:**
- **Adults:** 0.3-0.5 mg IM into lateral thigh (vastus lateralis)
- **Children >=30 kg (66 lbs):** 0.3 mg IM
- **Children 15-30 kg:** 0.15 mg IM
- **Children 7.5-15 kg:** 0.1 mg IM (if available)
- **Repeat every 5-15 minutes** as needed for persistent symptoms

**Route is Critical:**
- **IM preferred** over SC — faster absorption, better plasma concentrations
- **IV epinephrine** only for refractory cases with appropriate dosing

**Adjunctive Therapies (NOT substitutes for epinephrine):** [^11^]
- H1 antihistamines (diphenhydramine): for cutaneous symptoms only
- H2 blockers (ranitidine/cimetidine): adjunctive
- Inhaled beta-agonists: for bronchospasm
- IV fluids: for hypotension
- Corticosteroids: may prevent biphasic reactions (weak evidence)

**Positioning:** Supine with legs elevated. Do NOT sit upright — this can precipitate cardiovascular collapse.

**Observation:** Minimum 4-6 hours after symptom resolution; longer for severe reactions.

### Discrepancies in Current EMS Protocols (2024): [^11^]
- 30 states analyzed; only 47% used two-organ system definition
- 100% recommended diphenhydramine + epinephrine
- 97% recommended epinephrine as first-line
- Only 57% provided epinephrine autoinjectors
- Outdated recommendations (steroids, first-generation antihistamines) persist

### Epinephrine Autoinjector (EAI) Prescription: [^13^]
- Risk factors for severe anaphylaxis guide prescribing
- Higher likelihood of EAI use: prior systemic reaction, venom allergy, elevated tryptase, older age, CVD, honey bee venom, exercise-induced anaphylaxis, cold-induced urticaria
- Multiple EAIs commercially available: consider dose, needle length, affordability

### Biphasic Reactions
- Occur in up to 20% of patients
- Typically within 8 hours but may occur up to 72 hours
- Observation and patient education critical

## 1.4 Severe Bleeding and Hemorrhage

### Clinical Overview

Severe bleeding requires rapid assessment, hemorrhage control, and potential blood product administration. The primary survey approach (ABCDE) guides initial management.

### Initial Assessment

**Primary Survey:**
- **A:** Airway with cervical spine protection
- **B:** Breathing and oxygenation
- **C:** Circulation — control external bleeding, IV access x2, crystalloid resuscitation
- **D:** Disability (GCS)
- **E:** Exposure/environment (identify all sources of bleeding)

### Hemorrhage Control

**External Bleeding:**
1. Direct pressure
2. Pressure dressing
3. Tourniquet (for extremity hemorrhage not controlled by pressure)
4. Hemostatic dressings

**Internal Bleeding:**
- Two large-bore IVs (14-16 gauge)
- Crystalloid resuscitation (avoid over-resuscitation)
- Type and crossmatch
- Tranexamic acid if indicated
- Urgent surgical consultation

### Tranexamic Acid (TXA) [^48^]

**Mechanism:** Synthetic lysine analog that competitively inhibits plasminogen activation, stabilizing fibrin clots.

**FDA-Approved Indications:**
- Heavy menstrual bleeding
- Short-term prevention in hemophilia patients

**Off-Label Emergency Uses (Evidence-Based):**

| Indication | Dosing |
|------------|--------|
| **Trauma with significant hemorrhage** | 1 g IV over 10 min within 3 hours of injury; may repeat 1 g over 8 hours |
| **Postpartum hemorrhage** | 1 g IV over 10 min |
| **Surgical bleeding** | 10-15 mg/kg IV at incision |
| **Epistaxis** | Topical application |

**CRASH-2 Trial Findings:** [^48^]
- TXA improves survival when administered within 3 hours of injury
- Administering within 1 hour: 32% relative risk reduction in death from bleeding
- Administering within 1-3 hours: 21% relative risk reduction
- **No benefit after 3 hours** — may increase mortality

**Administration Notes:**
- Administer as slow IV push over 10 minutes (rapid infusion causes hypotension)
- Maximum dose: 2 g total
- Contraindicated in patients with active intravascular clotting
- Use caution in patients with renal impairment

### Massive Transfusion Protocol (MTP)
- Activated for blood loss >1500 mL or hemodynamic instability
- Ratio: 1:1:1 (RBC:Plasma:Platelets)
- Calcium replacement (citrate toxicity)
- Monitor for acidosis, hypothermia, coagulopathy ("lethal triad")

## 1.5 Altered Mental Status (AMS)

### Definition

Altered mental status describes a change in cognitive functioning that is not attributable to a pre-existing condition. It is a medical emergency requiring systematic evaluation. It is a descriptor, not a diagnosis. [^2^] [^7^]

### Categories

1. **Delirium:** Acute, fluctuating change in attention and awareness
2. **Dementia:** Chronic, progressive cognitive decline
3. **Coma:** Unarousable unresponsiveness
4. **Psychosis:** Disordered thought processes with preserved alertness

### Initial Evaluation Algorithm (ABCDEF) [^2^] [^10^]

**A — Airway:** Ensure patency; suction if needed; intubate if GCS <8
**B — Breathing:** Assess rate, effort, oxygenation; provide supplemental O2
**C — Circulation:** Check HR, BP, perfusion; IV access; cardiac monitoring
**D — Disability:** Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) or AVPU; glucose check; focal neurologic exam
**E — Exposure:** Full body exam for trauma, rashes, track marks, transdermal patches
**F — Fingerstick Glucose:** Treat immediately if <70 mg/dL

### Rapidly Reversible Causes ("4 S's") [^8^]

1. **Sugar** — Hypoglycemia: give D50 (after thiamine if malnourished/alcoholic)
2. **Stroke** — Perform FAST/BE-FAST; emergent CT if positive
3. **Sepsis** — Broad-spectrum antibiotics within 1 hour; IV fluids
4. **Seizure** — Benzodiazepines if active; check sodium

### Comprehensive Differential (AEIOU TIPS Mnemonic) [^8^]

- **A:** Alcohol, Acidosis, Arrhythmia
- **E:** Electrolytes, Endocrine (thyroid, adrenal)
- **I:** Infection (sepsis, meningitis, encephalitis, UTI, pneumonia)
- **O:** Oxygen deficit, Opiates (give naloxone 0.4-2 mg IV if suspected)
- **U:** Uremia, Hepatic encephalopathy
- **T:** Trauma, Temperature (hypothermia/hyperthermia), Tumor
- **I:** Insulin (hypo- or hyperglycemia), Intracranial hemorrhage
- **P:** Poisoning/Pharmacology (review all medications)
- **S:** Seizure, Shock, Subarachnoid hemorrhage, Syncope

### Key Investigations [^2^] [^9^]

**Immediate:**
- Complete vital signs (including temperature and O2 sat)
- Point-of-care glucose
- ECG
- Consider naloxone (0.4-2 mg IV)
- Consider thiamine 100 mg IV before glucose if at risk for Wernicke encephalopathy

**Urgent Labs:**
- CBC with differential
- Complete metabolic panel (electrolytes, renal function, liver function)
- Calcium, magnesium, phosphorus
- Troponin
- Urinalysis with culture
- Blood cultures (if infection suspected)
- Urine toxicology screen
- Ammonia (if liver disease suspected)

**Imaging:**
- CT head without contrast (first-line for suspected hemorrhage, mass, stroke)
- MRI if CT unrevealing and high suspicion
- Chest X-ray
- Lumbar puncture (after CT) if meningitis/encephalitis suspected

**Additional:**
- Thyroid function tests
- Vitamin B12, folate
- Arterial blood gas
- EEG (if seizure/non-convulsive status epilepticus suspected)

### Coma Cocktail (Empiric Treatment)

If etiology unclear, administer:
1. **Dextrose** (D50) — if glucose <70 mg/dL
2. **Naloxone** — 0.4-2 mg IV if opioid overdose suspected
3. **Thiamine** — 100 mg IV before glucose in malnourished patients

### Delirium-Specific Management [^2^]
- **Prevention is key:** Multicomponent interventions prevent 30-40% of delirium cases
- **Non-pharmacologic first:** Reorientation, sleep hygiene, vision/hearing aids, early mobilization, hydration
- **Avoid restraints** when possible
- **Antipsychotics only if:** Danger to self/others; severe agitation preventing essential treatment
- **Preferred agents:** Haloperidol (0.5-1 mg PO/IM) or quetiapine (25-50 mg PO) in elderly

## 1.6 Severe Dyspnea / Respiratory Failure

### Clinical Overview

Dyspnea is a subjective experience of breathing discomfort. Acute severe dyspnea represents a medical emergency requiring rapid assessment and intervention. The differential includes cardiac, pulmonary, and systemic causes. [^38^] [^40^]

### Causes of Acute Dyspnea (Can't Miss Diagnoses) [^38^] [^40^]

| Category | Conditions |
|----------|-----------|
| **Airway** | Upper airway obstruction (angioedema, foreign body, epiglottitis), anaphylaxis |
| **Pulmonary** | Asthma/COPD exacerbation, pneumonia, PE, pneumothorax, ARDS |
| **Cardiac** | ACS, acute heart failure/pulmonary edema, cardiac tamponade |
| **Systemic** | Sepsis, severe anemia, metabolic acidosis |
| **Central** | Anxiety/hyperventilation (diagnosis of exclusion) |

### Initial Assessment and Management [^38^] [^40^]

1. **ABCs** — assess work of breathing
2. **Full vital signs** including SpO2
3. **Oxygen** — target SpO2 94% for most; 88-92% for COPD patients
4. **Cardiac monitoring** and pulse oximetry
5. **IV access**
6. **EKG** (for cardiac causes)
7. **CXR** (for pulmonary causes)
8. **ABG/VBG** for acid-base status

### Non-Invasive Ventilation (NIV) [^38^] [^42^]

**Indications for NIV:**
- Moderate to severe dyspnea with accessory muscle use
- RR >25 bpm
- pH <7.35 with pCO2 >45 (hypercapnic respiratory failure)
- Fatigue

**Contraindications to NIV:**
- Respiratory arrest or absent respiratory drive
- Hemodynamic instability
- Airway obstruction
- Altered mental status
- Aspiration risk
- Inability to tolerate mask

**Types:**
- **CPAP:** Continuous positive airway pressure — preferred for cardiogenic pulmonary edema
- **BiPAP:** Bilevel positive airway pressure — preferred for COPD/asthma (hypercapnic failure)

### Management of Specific Causes

**Acute Cardiogenic Pulmonary Edema:** [^38^] [^42^]
1. CPAP or BiPAP (reduces intubation rate, improves oxygenation)
2. IV nitrates (afterload reduction)
3. IV diuretics (furosemide)
4. Supplemental O2
5. Treat underlying cause (ischemia, arrhythmia)

**Asthma/COPD Exacerbation:** [^38^]
1. Inhaled beta-agonists (albuterol) — continuous nebulization if severe
2. Inhaled anticholinergics (ipratropium)
3. Systemic corticosteroids (prednisone 40-60 mg or methylprednisolone 125 mg IV)
4. Supplemental O2 (target 88-92% for COPD)
5. BiPAP if hypercapnic
6. Magnesium sulfate 2 g IV if severe
7. Intubation if deteriorating despite above

**Pneumonia:** [^38^]
1. CURB-65 scoring for severity and disposition
2. Empiric antibiotics based on severity and risk factors
3. Supplemental O2
4. Fluids (careful — don't overload)

**Pneumothorax:**
1. Tension pneumothorax: immediate needle decompression (2nd intercostal space, midclavicular line)
2. Simple pneumothorax: chest tube if >2 cm or symptomatic

### Point-of-Care Ultrasound (POCUS) for Dyspnea [^38^] [^42^]

**BLUE Protocol findings:**
- **A-profile:** A-lines with lung sliding = COPD/asthma (excludes PE if no DVT)
- **B-profile:** Bilateral B-lines = pulmonary edema
- **A'-profile:** Absent lung sliding with A-lines = pneumothorax
- **C-profile:** Consolidation = pneumonia
- **PLAPS:** Posterolateral alveolar/pleural syndrome = effusion or consolidation

## 1.7 Suicidal Ideation Protocols

### Clinical Overview

Suicide is a leading cause of death worldwide. Primary care providers are often the first contact for patients with suicidal ideation. Systematic assessment and appropriate disposition are critical for patient safety. [^49^]

### Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS) [^49^]

The C-SSRS is the gold-standard assessment tool with validated psychometric properties:
- Good convergent and divergent validity
- High sensitivity and specificity for suicidal behavior
- Predictive validity: worst-point lifetime ideation predicts future attempts
- Sensitive to change over time

**Severity of Ideation Subscale (5-point):**
1. Wish to be dead
2. Nonspecific active suicidal thoughts
3. Suicidal thoughts with methods
4. Suicidal intent
5. Suicidal intent with plan

**Intensity of Ideation Subscale (5 dimensions, each 5-point):**
- Frequency
- Duration
- Controllability
- Deterrents
- Reasons for ideation

**Behavior Subscale:**
- Actual attempts
- Aborted attempts
- Interrupted attempts
- Preparatory behavior
- Nonsuicidal self-injurious behavior

**Lethality Subscale:**
- Actual lethality (6-point)
- Potential lethality (3-point, if actual = 0)

### Primary Care Assessment Protocol

**Step 1: Screen (during routine visits)**
- Ask directly: "Over the past 2 weeks, have you had thoughts that you would be better off dead, or of hurting yourself in some way?"
- Ask about plans, intent, and means
- C-SSRS screening version can be self-administered

**Step 2: Risk Stratification**

| Risk Level | Features | Disposition |
|------------|----------|-------------|
| **Low** | Passive ideation, no plan, no intent, protective factors | Outpatient with close follow-up, safety plan |
| **Moderate** | Active ideation with plan but low intent, some protective factors | Intensive outpatient or partial hospitalization |
| **High** | Active ideation with plan AND intent, access to means | Emergency department or psychiatric hospitalization |

**Step 3: Safety Planning**
1. Identify warning signs
2. Identify internal coping strategies
3. Identify social contacts for distraction
4. Identify family/friends for help
5. Identify professionals/resources to contact
6. Remove or secure lethal means

**Step 4: Risk Factors Assessment**

**Major Risk Factors:**
- Prior suicide attempt (strongest predictor)
- Mental health disorders (depression, bipolar, psychosis, substance use)
- Access to lethal means (firearms, medications)
- Recent loss (relationship, job, financial)
- Chronic pain or medical illness
- Family history of suicide
- Social isolation
- Recent discharge from psychiatric hospitalization

**Protective Factors:**
- Strong social support
- Religious/spiritual beliefs
- Children in the home
- Therapeutic relationship
- Problem-solving skills

### Red Flags (Immediate Emergency Activation)
1. Active suicidal intent with plan and means
2. Recent suicide attempt
3. Command hallucinations directing self-harm
4. Severe hopelessness
5. Psychosis with depressive features
6. Acute intoxication with suicidal statements

## 1.8 Sepsis Recognition

### Clinical Overview

Sepsis is life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection. Early recognition and treatment within the "golden hour" dramatically improve mortality. [^5^] [^12^]

### Definition Updates (Sepsis-3, 2016)

**Sepsis:** Life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection. Clinically identified by **SOFA score >=2** points from baseline.

**Septic Shock:** Sepsis with circulatory, cellular, and metabolic abnormalities sufficient to substantially increase mortality. Identified by:
- Vasopressor requirement to maintain MAP >=65 mmHg
- Serum lactate >2 mmol/L despite adequate fluid resuscitation

### SIRS Criteria (Original, 1992) [^5^] [^15^]

Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome — 2+ of:
1. Temperature >38 C (100.4 F) or <36 C (96.8 F)
2. Heart rate >90 bpm
3. Respiratory rate >20 breaths/min or PaCO2 <32 mmHg
4. WBC >12,000 or <4,000, or >10% bands

**Limitations:** Low specificity; many non-infectious conditions cause SIRS

### qSOFA (Quick SOFA) [^5^] [^12^] [^15^]

**Components (1 point each):**
1. Glasgow Coma Score <15
2. Respiratory rate >=22 breaths/min
3. Systolic blood pressure <=100 mmHg

**Interpretation:**
- qSOFA >=2: Suggests high risk of poor outcome
- More specific but less sensitive than SIRS for sepsis
- **2021 Surviving Sepsis Campaign: Recommends AGAINST using qSOFA as a single screening tool** due to poor sensitivity [^5^] [^15^]

### 2021 Surviving Sepsis Campaign Guidelines: [^5^]

**Key Recommendations:**
- Do NOT use qSOFA as a single screening tool (weak recommendation)
- Screen patients with known/suspected infection for organ dysfunction
- Measure blood lactate (weak recommendation, low-quality evidence)
- Treat patients with suspected infection + organ dysfunction or elevated lactate as sepsis regardless of screening score results
- Each hospital should formalize a screening process

### Screening Tool Comparison [^15^]

| Tool | Sensitivity | Specificity | Best Use |
|------|-------------|-------------|----------|
| **SIRS** | High | Low | Sensitive screening |
| **qSOFA** | Low | High | Risk stratification (not screening) |
| **NEWS** | Moderate | Moderate | General early warning |
| **SOFA** | N/A (diagnostic) | N/A | Organ dysfunction quantification |

### The "Hour-1 Bundle" (Emergency Management)

1. **Measure lactate** — remeasure if initial >2 mmol/L
2. **Obtain blood cultures** before antibiotics (do NOT delay antibiotics >45 minutes)
3. **Administer broad-spectrum antibiotics** within 1 hour of recognition
4. **Rapid fluid resuscitation** — 30 mL/kg crystalloid for hypotension or lactate >=4 mmol/L
5. **Apply vasopressors** if hypotensive after fluid resuscitation (goal MAP >=65 mmHg)

### Source Control
- Remove infected lines/catheters
- Drain abscesses
- Debride infected tissue
- Surgery if indicated (perforated viscus, necrotizing fasciitis)

### Antibiotic Considerations

**Empiric Broad-Spectrum Coverage:**
- Vancomycin + piperacillin-tazobactam (common combination)
- Add coverage for resistant organisms based on local patterns
- De-escalate based on culture results

## 2.5 Medication Reconciliation [^65^] [^66^]

### Definition
The process of comparing a patient's medication orders to all medications the patient has been taking to avoid errors during transitions of care.

### Four-Step Process: [^65^]
1. **Collect:** Obtain accurate medication list from most reliable sources
2. **Check:** Compare current medications against new orders
3. **Communicate:** Share reconciled list with care team and patient
4. **Document:** Record changes and rationale

### Information to Obtain
- All prescription medications (name, dose, route, frequency)
- OTC medications and supplements
- Medications from multiple prescribers
- Recently discontinued medications
- Patient-reported adherence

### Sources of Information (Minimum 2 Sources): [^65^]
- Patient/caregiver interview
- Community pharmacy records
- GP records
- Hospital discharge summaries
- Previous medication administration records
- Patient-held records (insulin passport, anticoagulation card)

### Common Discrepancies [^66^]
- Medication omitted (prescribed but not taken)
- Medication added (taken but not prescribed)
- Dose discrepancy
- Frequency discrepancy
- Documentation of reason for changes often poor

## 2.6 Allergy Documentation [^65^] [^66^]

### Standards for Allergy Documentation
1. **Drug name** (generic and brand)
2. **Type of reaction** (allergy vs. intolerance vs. side effect)
3. **Clinical description** of reaction (rash, anaphylaxis, GI upset)
4. **Date of reaction** if known
5. **Severity** assessment
6. **Update at every encounter**

### Key Points
- Distinguish true allergy from adverse effect or intolerance
- Document both the allergy AND the reaction
- Include allergies to foods, environmental triggers, latex, contrast
- Cross-sensitivities should be noted
- Review and update at each transition of care

# 4. Diagnostic Reasoning Framework

## 4.1 Bayesian Reasoning and Pre-Test Probability [^50^]

### Core Principle

Diagnostic testing should always be interpreted within the context of the patient's pre-test probability of disease. Bayes' theorem mathematically describes how test results update our probability estimate. [^50^]

**Bayes' Theorem (Odds Form):**

Post-test odds = Pre-test odds x Likelihood ratio

### Pre-Test Probability

**Definition:** The probability of disease before performing a diagnostic test, based on:
- Prevalence of disease in the population
- Patient's clinical history and presentation
- Findings from physical examination
- Results of previously performed tests

**Estimation Sources:**
- Clinical prediction rules (Wells score, HEART score, CURB-65)
- Population prevalence data
- Physician clinical judgment
- Previous studies of similar patients

### Why Pre-Test Probability Matters [^50^]

1. **A positive test in a low-prevalence setting** has lower positive predictive value (more false positives)
2. **A negative test in a high-prevalence setting** has lower negative predictive value (more false negatives)
3. **Tests have greatest utility at intermediate pre-test probabilities** where they change clinical management
4. **Sequential testing** uses the post-test probability from one test as the pre-test probability for the next

### Clinical Example: Exercise Stress Testing [^50^]

Exercise ECG for CAD:
- Sensitivity = 70% (TPR = 0.70)
- Specificity = 85% (FPR = 0.15)
- LR(+) = 0.70/0.15 = 4.67
- LR(-) = 0.30/0.85 = 0.35

If pre-test probability = 30%:
- Pre-test odds = 0.30/0.70 = 0.43
- If positive: Post-test odds = 0.43 x 4.67 = 2.0 -> Probability = 67%
- If negative: Post-test odds = 0.43 x 0.35 = 0.15 -> Probability = 13%

## 4.3 Heuristics and Cognitive Biases in Diagnosis [^41^] [^43^] [^47^]

### Dual-Process Theory [^43^]

**System 1 (Fast/Intuitive):**
- Pattern recognition, heuristics, gut feeling
- Automatic, requires less cognitive effort
- Prone to cognitive biases
- Appropriate for common, straightforward presentations

**System 2 (Slow/Analytical):**
- Deliberate, methodical reasoning
- Rule-based, considers all possibilities
- More accurate for complex or atypical presentations
- Requires more cognitive resources and time

### Common Cognitive Biases [^41^] [^43^] [^47^]

| Bias | Definition | Mitigation Strategy |
|------|-----------|-------------------|
| **Anchoring** | Fixating on initial information/diagnosis | Actively consider alternatives; revisit data |
| **Availability** | Judging likelihood by ease of recall | Consider base rates; systematic differential |
| **Confirmation** | Seeking data to confirm rather than refute | Actively seek disconfirming evidence |
| **Premature Closure** | Accepting a diagnosis before fully exploring | Generate complete differential before testing |
| **Representativeness** | Judging by how similar to prototype | Consider atypical presentations |
| **Attribution** | Blaming patient characteristics (psychosomatic bias) | Maintain diagnostic humility |
| **Overconfidence** | Overestimating accuracy of own judgment | Seek second opinions; use checklists |
| **Sunk Cost** | Continuing on a path due to prior investment | Reassess when not responding to treatment |
| **Framing Effect** | Decision influenced by how information presented | Consider data from multiple perspectives |
| **Search Satisficing** | Stopping search once finding first abnormality | Complete full systematic evaluation |

### Impact of Cognitive Biases [^43^]
- Studies show cognitive biases associated with diagnostic errors in up to 77% of case studies
- Anchoring bias is the most prominent cognitive bias in non-concordant diagnoses

### Debiasing Strategies
1. **Metacognition:** Think about your thinking
2. **Consider the opposite:** Actively seek disconfirming evidence
3. **Checklists:** Systematic review of all possibilities
4. **Second opinions:** Fresh perspective on complex cases
5. **Deliberate reflection:** Take time before finalizing diagnosis
6. **Reference to base rates:** Consider disease prevalence
7. **Probabilistic reasoning:** Quantify pre-test probability

## 4.4 Diagnostic Testing Principles [^50^] [^51^]

### Sensitivity and Specificity

**Sensitivity (True Positive Rate):**
- Probability of a positive test given disease is present
- Sensitivity = TP / (TP + FN)
- High sensitivity: Good for ruling OUT disease (SnNOut)

**Specificity (True Negative Rate):**
- Probability of a negative test given disease is absent
- Specificity = TN / (TN + FP)
- High specificity: Good for ruling IN disease (SpPIn)

**Relationship:**
- Sensitivity and specificity are inversely related
- Increasing cutoff threshold: Higher specificity, lower sensitivity
- Decreasing cutoff threshold: Higher sensitivity, lower specificity

### Predictive Values

**Positive Predictive Value (PPV):**
- Probability of disease given a positive test
- PPV = TP / (TP + FP)
- Depends on pre-test probability (prevalence)

**Negative Predictive Value (NPV):**
- Probability of no disease given a negative test
- NPV = TN / (TN + FN)
- Depends on pre-test probability (prevalence)

### Key Principle [^51^]
> Disease prevalence in a population affects PPV and NPV. When a disease is highly prevalent, the test is better at 'ruling in' the disease and worse at 'ruling it out.' Therefore, disease prevalence should be considered when providers examine their diagnostic test metrics or interpret values from other providers or researchers.

### Likelihood Ratios [^50^] [^51^]

**Positive Likelihood Ratio LR(+):**
- LR(+) = Sensitivity / (1 - Specificity)
- How much a positive test increases the odds of disease
- LR(+) >10: Excellent test; 5-10: Good; 2-5: Fair; 1-2: Poor

**Negative Likelihood Ratio LR(-):**
- LR(-) = (1 - Sensitivity) / Specificity
- How much a negative test decreases the odds of disease
- LR(-) <0.1: Excellent test; 0.1-0.2: Good; 0.2-0.5: Fair; 0.5-1: Poor

**Interpreting LRs:**
| LR Range | Interpretation |
|----------|---------------|
| >10 | Large increase in probability |
| 5-10 | Moderate increase |
| 2-5 | Small increase |
| 1-2 | Minimal change |
| 0.5-1 | Minimal decrease |
| 0.2-0.5 | Small decrease |
| 0.1-0.2 | Moderate decrease |
| <0.1 | Large decrease |

### Fagan Nomogram
A graphical tool to convert pre-test probability to post-test probability using likelihood ratios:
1. Identify pre-test probability on left axis
2. Draw line through LR value on middle axis
3. Read post-test probability on right axis

# 5. Evidence-Based Medicine Principles

## 5.1 Levels of Evidence Hierarchy [^56^] [^62^] [^63^]

### Oxford/Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine Levels [^56^]

| Level | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| **1A** | Systematic review (with homogeneity) of RCTs |
| **1B** | Individual RCT (narrow confidence intervals) |
| **1C** | All-or-none study (no treatment before, all died; treatment available, some survive) |
| **2A** | Systematic review (homogeneity) of cohort studies |
| **2B** | Individual cohort study (including low-quality RCT) |
| **2C** | "Outcomes" research; ecological studies |
| **3A** | Systematic review (homogeneity) of case-control studies |
| **3B** | Individual case-control study |
| **4** | Case series (and poor quality cohort/case-control studies) |
| **5** | Expert opinion without explicit critical appraisal |

### GRADE System [^62^]

The GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation) system:

**Starting Point:**
- RCTs: High quality
- Observational studies: Low quality

**Factors to Rate DOWN Quality:** [^62^]

| Factor | Potential Downgrade |
|--------|-------------------|
| Limitations in study design (risk of bias) | 1-2 levels |
| Inconsistency of results | 1-2 levels |
| Indirectness of evidence | 1-2 levels |
| Imprecision | 1-2 levels |
| Publication bias | 1-2 levels |

**Factors to Rate UP Quality:** [^62^]

| Factor | Potential Upgrade |
|--------|------------------|
| Large magnitude of effect | 1-2 levels |
| All plausible confounding would reduce demonstrated effect | 1 level |
| Dose-response gradient | 1 level |

**Final GRADE Quality Categories:**
- **High:** Further research is very unlikely to change confidence in the effect estimate
- **Moderate:** Further research is likely to have an important impact on confidence
- **Low:** Further research is very likely to have an important impact
- **Very Low:** Any estimate of effect is very uncertain

### 2024 ESC Revised Evidence Grading [^57^]

| Level | Definition |
|-------|-----------|
| **A** | Conclusive evidence from >=2 adequately powered RCTs with substantial evidence against play of chance |
| **B1** | Suggestive evidence from >=1 adequately powered RCT with some evidence against play of chance |
| **B2** | Limited evidence from >=2 adequately powered non-randomized studies with careful bias control |
| **C** | Preliminary evidence from non-randomized studies without careful bias control, small RCTs, or expert consensus |

**Class of Recommendation (COR):**
- **Class I:** Evidence and/or general agreement that treatment is beneficial
- **Class IIa:** Weight of evidence favors usefulness
- **Class IIb:** Usefulness less well established
- **Class III:** Evidence of harm or no benefit

## 5.2 Number Needed to Treat (NNT) and Number Needed to Harm (NNH) [^64^] [^69^] [^71^]

### Definition

**NNT:** The average number of patients who need to be treated to prevent one additional adverse outcome. [^69^] [^71^]

**NNH:** The average number of patients who need to be exposed to a risk factor to cause one additional adverse outcome.

### Calculation [^64^] [^71^]

**Step 1:** Calculate Absolute Risk Reduction (ARR)
- ARR = Control Event Rate (CER) - Experimental Event Rate (EER)

**Step 2:** Calculate NNT
- NNT = 1 / ARR

**Example:** [^71^]
- Drug reduces MI risk from 4% to 2%
- CER = 0.04; EER = 0.02
- ARR = 0.04 - 0.02 = 0.02
- NNT = 1 / 0.02 = 50
- Interpretation: 50 patients need treatment to prevent 1 MI

**For NNH:**
- NNH = 1 / Absolute Risk Increase (ARI)
- ARI = EER - CER (when experimental rate is worse)

### NNT for Time-to-Event Outcomes [^64^]

At a specific time point:
- NNT(t) = 1 / |S_control(t) - S_treatment(t)|
- Where S = survival probability

### Practical Considerations [^64^]

1. **Always specify time frame:** "NNT = 4 to prevent one case of PONV within 24 hours"
2. **Confidence intervals:** If CI includes infinity, result not statistically significant
3. **Baseline risk matters:** Same relative effect yields different NNT in high vs. low-risk populations
4. **Do not pool NNTs directly:** Pool absolute risk reductions, then compute NNT
5. **NNT varies with patient characteristics:** Use NNT from studies matching your patient's profile

### Limitations [^72^] [^73^]
- NNT is specific to a comparison between two treatments, not a single intervention
- Influenced by baseline risk, time frame, and outcomes
- Confidence interval interpretation can be complex when statistically non-significant
- Some argue for presenting absolute risk differences instead of NNTs [^73^]
- Does not capture patient preferences or values

### Common NNT Examples in Primary Care [^69^]

| Intervention | Outcome | NNT (Timeframe) |
|-------------|---------|-----------------|
| Statins (high-risk CVD) | Prevent major CV event | ~20-50 (5 years) |
| Aspirin primary prevention | Prevent MI | ~250 (5 years) |
| Metformin (diabetes) | Prevent progression | ~4 (3 years) |
| Antihypertensives | Prevent stroke | ~50-100 (5 years) |
| Smoking cessation | Prevent death | ~20-50 (lifetime) |

## 5.3 Shared Decision-Making Framework

### Definition

Shared decision-making (SDM) is a collaborative process in which clinicians and patients make healthcare decisions together, combining clinical evidence with patient values and preferences.

### Components of Shared Decision-Making

1. **Inform:** Provide clear, evidence-based information about options
2. **Present probabilities:** Use natural frequencies (e.g., "8 out of 100" rather than "8%")
3. **Elicit patient values:** What matters most to this patient?
4. **Reach agreement:** Joint decision aligned with patient preferences
5. **Document:** Record the decision-making process

### Practical Tools

**Option Grid:** One-page summary comparing treatment options on outcomes that matter to patients.

**Decision Aid:** Evidence-based tools to help patients understand options and consider personal values.

**Teach-Back Method:** Ask patient to explain back their understanding to confirm comprehension.

### The BRAN Framework for Patients
- **B**enefits: What are the benefits?
- **R**isks: What are the risks?
- **A**lternatives: What are the alternatives?
- **N**othing: What happens if I do nothing?

### Communicating Risk

**Best Practices:**
- Use absolute risks, not just relative risks
- Use natural frequencies ("1 in 50" vs. "2%")
- Use consistent denominators
- Visual aids improve understanding
- Avoid framing bias (present both positive and negative frames)
- Consider using icon arrays or pictographs

### Evidence-Based SDM Example: Statin Therapy

1. **Explain risk:** "Your 10-year risk of heart attack or stroke is 15%"
2. **Present benefit:** "Taking a statin would reduce this to about 10% — a 5% absolute reduction"
3. **Translate to NNT:** "About 20 people like you need to take a statin for 5 years to prevent one heart attack or stroke"
4. **Present harms:** "About 1 in 100 will develop muscle pain; 1 in 200 will develop diabetes"
5. **Elicit values:** "What's most important to you? Preventing a heart attack? Avoiding medication?"

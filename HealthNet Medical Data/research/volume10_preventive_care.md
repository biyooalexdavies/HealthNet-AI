## 1. Vaccination Guidelines

### 1.1 CDC ACIP Adult Immunization Schedule (2024-2025)

**Clinical Overview:** The Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP) publishes annual immunization schedules for adults aged 19 years and older, incorporating new evidence and vaccine recommendations throughout the year via addenda. [^274^]

**Key 2025 Schedule Changes:**
- Transition from quadrivalent to **trivalent influenza vaccines** (IIV3, aIIV3, HD-IIV3, RIV3) for the 2024-25 season [^456^]
- Updated **COVID-19 vaccine** recommendations with 2024-2025 formulation [^276^]
- Addition of **21-valent pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV21; CAPVAXIVE)** as an option for adults [^467^]
- Addition of **inactivated polio vaccine (IPV)** to adult tables [^456^]
- Updated **respiratory syncytial virus (RSV)** recommendations [^452^]
- New **mRNA RSV vaccine (mResvia)** added to abbreviations [^456^]
- Updated **meningococcal B** vaccination schedule (Bexsero: 2 doses at 0 and 6 months for healthy persons 16-23 years) [^456^]

**How to Use the Schedule:** [^276^]
1. Determine recommended vaccines by age (Table 1)
2. Assess need for additional vaccines by medical condition/indication (Table 2)
3. Review vaccine types, dosing, intervals, and special situations (Notes)
4. Review contraindications and precautions (Appendix)
5. Review new/updated ACIP guidance (Addendum)

### 1.2 Influenza Vaccine

**Guidelines & Evidence:**
- **Annual vaccination** recommended for all adults 19 years and older [^276^]
- **Age 65+:** HD-IIV3, aIIV3, or RIV3 **preferred** over standard-dose unadjuvanted IIV3. If none available, any age-appropriate influenza vaccine should be used [^276^]
- **Egg allergy:** Persons with egg allergy can receive **any** influenza vaccine (egg-based or non-egg-based) appropriate for age and health status [^274^]
- **Solid organ transplant recipients (19-64 years):** HD-IIV3 and aIIV3 are acceptable options [^274^]
- **LAIV3 contraindication:** Close contacts of severely immunosuppressed persons requiring protected environment should NOT receive LAIV3 [^274^]

**Key Clinical Points:**
- Trivalent formulation replaces quadrivalent for 2024-25 season [^456^]
- Vaccination ideally completed by end of October
- Pregnant women should receive inactivated influenza vaccine (not live attenuated)

### 1.3 COVID-19 Vaccine

**Guidelines & Evidence (2024-2025 Updated Recommendations):** [^274^] [^276^]

**Adults 19-64 years:**
- Unvaccinated: 1 dose of updated 2024-25 Moderna or Pfizer-BioNTech; OR 2 doses of Novavax at 0, 3-8 weeks
- Previously vaccinated: 1 dose of updated 2024-25 vaccine at least 8 weeks after most recent dose

**Adults 65+ years:**
- 2 doses of updated 2024-25 vaccine, minimum 2 months apart

**Immunocompromised adults:**
- May receive additional doses beyond routine recommendations
- Use vaccine from same manufacturer for all doses in initial series
- 3-dose initial series for mRNA vaccines, followed by booster doses

**Pregnancy:** COVID-19 vaccination recommended during pregnancy; safe at any gestational age [^542^]

### 1.4 Tdap/Td

**Guidelines & Evidence:** [^534^] [^535^] [^538^] [^539^]

**Routine Recommendations:**
- **Primary series:** 3 doses of Td-containing vaccine (at least 1 dose Tdap)
- **Booster:** Td or Tdap every 10 years throughout life
- **Adults 19+ who have never received Tdap:** Single dose of Tdap regardless of interval since last Td

**Pregnancy:** [^538^] [^543^]
- **Tdap during each pregnancy**, preferably at **27-36 weeks gestation**
- Earlier vaccination in 27-36 week window maximizes passive antibody transfer
- Reduces risk of infant pertussis by **78%** in babies <2 months old
- If not given during pregnancy, administer immediately postpartum
- If tetanus booster indicated during pregnancy (>10 years since prior Td), give Tdap at 27-36 weeks
- **Wound management:** If tetanus vaccine indicated and >5 years since last booster, give Tdap regardless of gestational age [^546^]

**Catch-up Schedule:**
- Unvaccinated adults: Tdap, then Td/Tdap at 4 weeks, then Td/Tdap at 6-12 months

### 1.5 HPV Vaccine (Gardasil 9)

**Guidelines & Evidence:** [^464^]

**Routine Recommendations:**
- Routine vaccination at **age 11-12 years**; may start at age 9
- Catch-up vaccination through age **26 years** for all persons not adequately vaccinated

**Adults 27-45 years:** [^464^]
- Shared clinical decision-making
- Vaccination not routinely recommended but may be considered for those most likely to benefit
- Less benefit due to higher likelihood of prior HPV exposure
- Not recommended for everyone >26 years

**Dosing Schedule:**
- **2-dose series:** For those starting before 15th birthday (doses at 0 and 6-12 months)
- **3-dose series:** For those starting at ages 15-26 years, and for immunocompromised persons aged 9-26 years (schedule: 0, 1-2 months, 6 months)

**Key Clinical Points:**
- HPV vaccination prevents new infections but does NOT treat existing infections
- No pregnancy test needed before vaccination
- HPV vaccination not recommended during pregnancy; no intervention needed if inadvertently given
- Immunocompromising conditions including HIV: complete 3-dose series even if initiated at age 9-14 [^274^]

### 1.6 Pneumococcal Vaccines

**Guidelines & Evidence (2024-2025 with PCV21):** [^461^] [^462^] [^467^]

**Available Vaccines:**
- PCV15 (VAXNEUVANCE), PCV20 (Prevnar20), PCV21 (CAPVAXIVE), PPSV23 (Pneumovax23)

**Adults 50+ years (never received PCV):**
- Single dose of PCV15, PCV20, or PCV21
- If PCV15 used: follow with PPSV23 at least 1 year later (minimum 8 weeks for immunocompromised, cochlear implant, or CSF leak)
- If PCV20 or PCV21 used: no additional pneumococcal vaccine needed

**Adults 19-49 years with risk conditions:**
- Single dose of PCV15, PCV20, or PCV21
- Risk conditions: chronic heart/lung/liver disease, diabetes, cigarette smoking, alcoholism; immunocompromising conditions, CSF leak, cochlear implant [^467^]

**Adults 65+ with prior PCV13 and PPSV23:**
- Shared clinical decision-making regarding PCV20 or PCV21 (single dose, at least 5 years after last pneumococcal vaccine) [^461^]

**Clinical Tool:** CDC offers PneumoRecs VaxAdvisor app for patient-specific guidance [^462^]

### 1.7 Shingles (Herpes Zoster)

**Guidelines & Evidence:** [^465^]

**Recombinant Zoster Vaccine (RZV; Shingrix):**
- Recommended for **all adults 50 years and older**
- Recommended for **adults 19+ with immunocompromising conditions**
- **2-dose series:** second dose 2-6 months after first
- **No maximum age** for vaccination
- Efficacy: 97% in adults 50-69; 91% in adults 70+ [^465^]
- Efficacy against postherpetic neuralgia: 91% (ages 50+); 89% (ages 70+)

**Key Clinical Points:**
- Should get vaccinated even if previously had shingles, received Zostavax, or received varicella vaccine
- No specific waiting period after shingles episode; wait until rash resolves
- Contraindicated during current episode of herpes zoster and during pregnancy
- Common side effects: sore arm, fatigue, muscle pain, headache, fever (usually 2-3 days)
- Very rare risk of Guillain-Barre syndrome reported

### 1.8 Hepatitis A and B

**Hepatitis A:** [^518^]
- 2-dose series for adults at risk (travelers, men who have sex with men, persons who use drugs, persons with chronic liver disease)
- Combined HepA-HepB vaccine (Twinrix) available: 3-dose series (0, 1, 6 months)

**Hepatitis B (Updated 2022-2023 Recommendations):** [^519^] [^523^] [^517^]

**Universal Vaccination:**
- **All adults aged 19-59 years:** Routine HepB vaccination recommended (catch-up)
- **Adults 60+ with risk factors:** Recommended
- **Adults 60+ without risk factors:** May receive vaccine if desired

**This replaces previous risk-based approach to improve coverage** [^523^]

**Risk Factors for Adults 60+:** [^523^]
- Sexual exposure risks (MSM, multiple partners, STI evaluation)
- Percutaneous/mucosal blood exposure (injection drug use, healthcare workers, dialysis)
- HIV, hepatitis C, chronic liver disease
- Travel to endemic countries, diabetes
- Incarcerated persons

**Vaccine Options:**
- Heplisav-B: 2 doses, 1 month apart (adults 18+)
- Engerix-B/Recombivax HB: 3 doses (0, 1, 6 months)
- Twinrix (HepA+HepB): 3 doses (0, 1, 6 months)

**Universal Screening (2023):** [^517^]
- All adults 18+ should be screened at least once with **triple panel**: HBsAg, anti-HBs, total anti-HBc
- Pregnant people: test during each pregnancy

### 1.9 MMR and Varicella

**MMR (Measles, Mumps, Rubella):** [^274^] [^541^]

**Evidence of Immunity:**
- Documentation of adequate vaccination (2 doses measles/mumps, 1 dose rubella for adults; doses separated by at least 28 days)
- Laboratory evidence of immunity
- Laboratory confirmation of disease
- Birth before 1957 (except healthcare personnel)

**Healthcare Personnel:** [^536^]
- Born 1957 or later: need documentation of 2 doses MMR or laboratory evidence of immunity
- Born before 1957: considered immune, but 2 doses MMR recommended if no laboratory evidence during outbreaks

**Adults without evidence of immunity:** 1 dose MMR; 2 doses for healthcare personnel, students, travelers [^274^]

**Pregnancy:** MMR contraindicated during pregnancy; give 1 dose postpartum before discharge if no rubella immunity [^274^]

**Varicella (Chickenpox):** [^533^] [^540^]

**Evidence of Immunity:**
- Documentation of 2 doses of varicella vaccine
- Laboratory evidence of immunity
- Birth in US before 1980 (not for HCP, pregnant women, immunocompromised)
- Diagnosis/verification of varicella or shingles history by healthcare provider

**Healthcare Personnel:** [^536^] [^540^]
- All HCP should have evidence of immunity
- 2 doses of varicella vaccine, 4-8 weeks apart
- 70-90% of adults who don't remember having varicella actually have antibodies

### 1.10 RSV (Respiratory Syncytial Virus)

**Guidelines & Evidence (2024 Update):** [^452^] [^515^] [^528^]

**Adults 75+ years:** RSV vaccination recommended (routine)

**Adults 60-74 years:** RSV vaccination recommended for those at **increased risk** for severe RSV disease:
- Chronic cardiopulmonary disease
- Diabetes mellitus
- Moderate or severe immune compromise
- Chronic kidney/liver/hematologic disorders
- Neurologic/neuromuscular conditions
- Nursing home/long-term care residents
- Frailty [^530^]

**Pregnancy:** RSV vaccination (Abrysvo) recommended at **32-36 weeks gestation** during RSV season [^542^]

**Available Vaccines:** Abrysvo (Pfizer), Arexvy (GSK), mResvia (Moderna) [^452^]
- Single dose only; no repeat dosing recommended
- Coadministration with other vaccines acceptable

### 1.11 Travel-Specific Vaccines

**Key Travel Vaccines:** [^516^] [^518^] [^522^]

| Vaccine | Indication | Schedule |
|---------|-----------|----------|
| **Hepatitis A** | Most travelers to developing countries | 2 doses, 0 and 6-12 months |
| **Hepatitis B** | Travelers to endemic areas; healthcare workers | 2-3 dose series |
| **Typhoid** | Travel to endemic areas (S. Asia, Africa, Latin America) | Injectable (ViCPS): 1 dose; Oral (Ty21a): 4 capsules every other day |
| **Yellow fever** | Travel to endemic areas (parts of Africa, South America) | Single dose; booster may be needed after 10+ years |
| **Japanese encephalitis** | Travel >1 month in endemic Asia/Western Pacific during transmission season | 2 doses, 28 days apart; booster at 1+ years |
| **Meningococcal** | Travel to meningitis belt of sub-Saharan Africa; Hajj/Umrah pilgrims | 1-2 doses depending on vaccine |
| **Rabies** | Adventure travelers, veterinarians, long-term travelers to endemic areas | 3 doses at 0, 7, 21-28 days |
| **Polio** | Adults known or suspected unvaccinated traveling to endemic areas | Complete 3-dose series if incomplete |

**Pre-travel Consultation:** CDC Yellow Book and destination-specific pages at wwwnc.cdc.gov/travel [^522^]

### 1.12 Immunocompromised Host Considerations

**General Principles:** [^545^]
- **Inactivated vaccines:** Generally safe; may have reduced immunogenicity
- **Live vaccines:** Contraindicated in severely immunocompromised persons
- **Household contacts:** Should receive all age-appropriate vaccines; prefer inactivated influenza over LAIV

**Timing After Chemotherapy:** [^545^]
- Inactivated vaccines: 3-6 months after completion of chemotherapy
- Live vaccines: after evidence of immune reconstitution
- Anti-CD20 therapies (e.g., rituximab): wait 6-9 months after last dose before vaccinating

**Key Considerations:**
- Higher-dose or additional vaccine doses may be needed
- Antibody titers may be checked when indicated
- Annual influenza vaccination essential
- COVID-19: additional doses recommended [^274^]
- Pneumococcal: may need both PCV and PPSV23 with shorter intervals [^467^]

### 1.13 Vaccine Hesitancy Counseling

**CASE Method (Corroboration, About Me, Science, Explain/Advise):**
- Builds trust through relationship establishment
- Uses open-ended questions to understand specific concerns
- Provides tailored information based on identified concerns
- Respects autonomy while maintaining therapeutic relationship

**Motivational Interviewing Approach:** [^510^]

**4-Step Model:**

**Step 1: Engage - Establish Trust**
- Use MI spirit: Compassion, Acceptance, Partnership
- Skills: Open-ended questions, reflective listening, affirmation
- **Don't:** Try to correct misinformation or convince with arguments
- **Do:** Listen to learn; let people express fears without interruption

**Step 2: Understand Views**
- Clarify specific concerns with open questions
- Affirm desire to act responsibly
- Use complex reflections and summaries

**Step 3: Provide Information**
- Ask-offer-ask technique
- Co-build knowledge with patient
- Offer information relevant to specific concerns

**Step 4: Respect Autonomy**
- Don't force or pressure for immediate decision
- Strengthen trust relationship
- Move toward planning when patient is ready

**Evidence:** Motivational interviewing reduces vaccine hesitancy and enhances intention to vaccinate [^510^]

## 2. Cancer Screening Guidelines

### 2.1 Breast Cancer Screening

**Guidelines & Evidence:**

**USPSTF 2024 Update:** [^455^] [^453^] [^458^]
- **Screening mammography every 2 years for women ages 40-74 years (Grade B)**
- Expanded from prior recommendation that started at age 50
- Applies to all persons assigned female at birth at average risk
- **Age 75+:** Insufficient evidence to assess balance of benefits/harms (Grade I)
- **Dense breasts:** Insufficient evidence for supplemental screening with ultrasound or MRI (Grade I)

**Rationale for Lowering Age to 40:** [^458^]
- Rising breast cancer incidence in women under 50
- Black women have 40% higher death rate than White women despite similar screening rates
- Disparities widest for women under 50

**ACS Guidelines (2023):** [^416^]
- Age 45-54: Annual screening
- Age 40-44: Option to start annual screening
- Age 55+: Transition to every 2 years or continue annually
- Continue as long as in good health with life expectancy >10 years

**Modalities:**
- Digital mammography and digital breast tomosynthesis (DBT/3D mammography) both effective [^455^]
- DBT has elevated cancer detection vs. conventional digital mammography [^454^]
- Supplemental screening (MRI, ultrasound) for dense breasts: insufficient evidence per USPSTF [^455^]

**Breast Density Notification:**
- FDA requires reporting of breast density to patients (2024)
- Dense breasts increase cancer risk and reduce mammography sensitivity
- Shared decision-making about supplemental imaging

**Key Clinical Points:**
- Ensure equitable follow-up for abnormal mammograms
- Black women face disparities including later stage at diagnosis
- Annual screening may be preferred for women 40-49 and those with dense breasts

### 2.2 Cervical Cancer Screening

**Guidelines & Evidence (USPSTF 2018):** [^466^]

**Age 21-29 years:**
- Cervical cytology (Pap smear) every **3 years** (Grade A)

**Age 30-65 years:**
- One of three options (all Grade A):
  1. Cervical cytology every **3 years**
  2. **Primary high-risk HPV testing** every **5 years**
  3. **Co-testing** (cytology + hrHPV) every **5 years**

**Do NOT Screen:**
- Under 21 years (Grade D)
- Over 65 years with adequate prior screening (3 consecutive negative screens or 2 negative within 10 years, most recent within 5 years) (Grade D)
- After hysterectomy with cervix removal without history of high-grade lesion or cancer (Grade D)

**Key Clinical Points:**
- Primary HPV testing every 5 years is now a preferred option for ages 30-65
- Screening prevents cervical cancer incidence and mortality
- Risk factors: low socioeconomic status, smoking, multiple sexual partners, early coitarche
- Ensure equitable access to follow-up of abnormal results

### 2.3 Colorectal Cancer Screening

**Guidelines & Evidence (USPSTF 2021):** [^494^]

**Screening Age:** 45-75 years (Grade A) [^494^]
- Lowered from age 50 in 2021 update due to rising CRC in younger adults

**Screening Modalities:**

| Test | Frequency | Notes |
|------|-----------|-------|
| **FIT (Fecal Immunochemical Test)** | Annual | Stool-based; detects hemoglobin |
| **mt-sDNA (Cologuard)** | Every 1-3 years | Stool-based; detects DNA alterations and hemoglobin |
| **Colonoscopy** | Every 10 years | Gold standard; visual examination of entire colon |
| **CT Colonography** | Every 5 years | Requires bowel prep; radiation exposure |
| **Flexible Sigmoidoscopy** | Every 5 years | Limited to distal colon |
| **FIT + Sigmoidoscopy** | FIT annually + sigmoidoscopy every 10 years | Combined approach |

**Age 76-85:** Individual decision based on prior screening, health status, life expectancy (Grade C)

**Implementation:** [^494^]
- Colonoscopy is most commonly used modality (52.7% post-2021 recommendation)
- FIT and mt-sDNA are noninvasive options that improve adherence
- Any recommended test is better than no screening

**Risk Factors:** Family history of CRC or advanced adenoma, inflammatory bowel disease, hereditary CRC syndromes, abdominal radiation

### 2.4 Lung Cancer Screening

**Guidelines & Evidence (USPSTF 2021):** [^468^] [^473^] [^476^]

**Eligibility Criteria:**
- **Age:** 50-80 years
- **Smoking history:** 20 pack-years or more
- **Current smoker OR quit within past 15 years**
- **Grade B recommendation**

**Screening Modality:** Annual low-dose computed tomography (LDCT)

**Discontinue When:**
- Person has not smoked for 15 years
- Health problem that limits life expectancy or ability/willingness to have curative lung surgery

**2023 ACS Update:** [^474^]
- ACS removed "years since quitting" requirement
- Recommend annual screening for adults 50-80 who have ever smoked with 20+ pack-year history
- Note: USPSTF and Medicare have not adopted this change

**Key Clinical Points:** [^468^]
- Expanded criteria (from age 55/30 pack-years) nearly doubles eligible population
- Includes more Black patients and women who tend to smoke fewer cigarettes
- Estimated 30,000-60,000 lives saved annually with full implementation
- Requires counseling about benefits (early detection) and harms (false positives, radiation, overtesting)
- Smoking cessation support should be offered to all current smokers
- Shared decision-making visit required by Medicare before first LDCT

**False Positive Management:**
- Most lung nodules detected on LDCT are not cancer
- Follow Lung-RADS classification for management
- Avoid excessive follow-up procedures

### 2.5 Prostate Cancer Screening

**Guidelines & Evidence (USPSTF 2018):** [^482^] [^469^]

**Age 55-69 years:**
- **Shared decision-making** about PSA-based screening (Grade C)
- Discuss possible benefits and harms
- Decision should be individualized

**Age 70+ years:**
- **Do not routinely screen** (Grade D)

**Benefits:**
- PSA screening may slightly lower chance of death from prostate cancer in some men

**Harms:**
- False-positive results leading to additional testing
- Overdiagnosis of problems that wouldn't cause symptoms or death
- Treatment complications (urinary incontinence, erectile dysfunction)

**Shared Decision-Making Model:** [^469^]
1. **Choice talk:** Introduce the choice; acknowledge decision needs to be made
2. **Option talk:** Describe PSA screening options; can integrate decision aid
3. **Decision talk:** Help patients explore preferences and make decision

**High-Risk Populations:** [^479^]
- Black men: 1.8x more likely to be diagnosed; 2.2x more likely to die
- Consider earlier screening (age 45) for Black men and those with family history
- Over 60% of prostate cancer deaths occur in men who never had PSA test

**Key Clinical Points:**
- Less than 40% of US men who received PSA test report shared decision-making conversation [^480^]
- Decision aids improve knowledge but modest effect on screening likelihood
- After 2012 USPSTF recommendation against screening, PSA rates fell 20-40%, late-stage diagnoses increased [^479^]

## 3. Cardiovascular Risk Reduction

### 3.1 ASCVD Risk Calculator

**PREVENT Equations (2024):** [^470^] [^472^] [^493^]

The American Heart Association's **PREVENT (Predicting Risk of cardiovascular disease EVENTs)** equations are now the preferred risk calculator, replacing the Pooled Cohort Equations (PCE).

**Key Features:**
- Estimates risk for adults aged **30-79 years**
- **Race-free** (removes race as predictor)
- Incorporates **kidney function (eGFR)** and **BMI**
- Provides both **10-year and 30-year** risk estimates
- Three outcomes: Total CVD, ASCVD, Heart Failure
- Recalibrated to contemporary event rates (PCE overestimated by 40-50%) [^493^]

**Risk Categories (2026 ACC/AHA Guidelines):** [^138^] [^470^]

| Category | 10-Year ASCVD Risk | Action |
|----------|-------------------|--------|
| **Low** | <3% | Lifestyle counseling |
| **Borderline** | 3% to <5% | Moderate-intensity statin may be reasonable after discussion |
| **Intermediate** | 5% to <10% | At least moderate-intensity statin; high-intensity at upper end |
| **High** | >=10% | High-intensity statin for >=50% LDL-C reduction |

**Risk-Enhancing Factors:** [^493^]
- Family history of premature ASCVD
- Chronic inflammatory conditions (lupus, rheumatoid arthritis)
- Cardiometabolic conditions (overweight/obesity, diabetes, CKD)
- Higher-risk ancestry (South Asian, Filipino)
- Reproductive risk markers (early menopause, preeclampsia, gestational diabetes)
- Biomarkers: Lp(a), apoB, hsCRP, elevated triglycerides

**Coronary Artery Calcium (CAC) Scoring:**
- Used to reclassify risk when uncertainty remains after PREVENT + risk enhancers
- CAC = 0: may avoid statin therapy (unless smoker, diabetes, strong family history)
- CAC 1-99: moderate-intensity statin
- CAC 100-999: high-intensity statin
- CAC >1000: very high risk, intensify therapy [^138^]

### 3.2 Statin Therapy

**Guidelines & Evidence (2026 ACC/AHA Dyslipidemia Guidelines):** [^138^] [^493^]

**Intensity-Based Approach:**

| Statin Intensity | LDL-C Reduction | Examples |
|-----------------|-----------------|----------|
| **High-intensity** | >50% | Atorvastatin 40-80mg, Rosuvastatin 20-40mg |
| **Moderate-intensity** | 30-49% | Atorvastatin 10-20mg, Rosuvastatin 5-10mg, Simvastatin 20-40mg, Pravastatin 40-80mg |
| **Low-intensity** | <30% | Simvastatin 10mg, Pravastatin 10-20mg, Lovastatin 20mg |

**Indications:**
- **LDL-C >=190 mg/dL:** High-intensity statin regardless of risk
- **Age 40-75 with diabetes:** At least moderate-intensity statin
- **10-year ASCVD risk >=10%:** High-intensity statin
- **10-year risk 5-9.9%:** Moderate-intensity statin; high-intensity at upper end
- **10-year risk 3-4.9%:** Moderate-intensity statin after clinician-patient discussion

**Add-on Therapy:** [^138^]
- Ezetimibe: if LDL-C goal not achieved on maximally tolerated statin
- PCSK9 inhibitor or bempedoic acid: if still above goal
- Icosapent ethyl: for TG 150-499 mg/dL on statin with additional risk factors

**Special Populations:**
- **Age >75:** Individualize based on life expectancy, multimorbidity, frailty
- **Life expectancy <1 year:** May discontinue lipid-lowering therapy
- **CKD Stage 3+:** Moderate-intensity statin or statin + ezetimibe
- **HIV:** Statins generally safe; consider drug interactions

**Monitoring:**
- Baseline lipid panel
- Recheck 4-12 weeks after initiation or dose change
- Then every 3-12 months

### 3.3 Blood Pressure Management

**ACC/AHA Categories:**

| Category | Systolic (mmHg) | Diastolic (mmHg) |
|----------|----------------|------------------|
| **Normal** | <120 | and <80 |
| **Elevated** | 120-129 | and <80 |
| **Stage 1 HTN** | 130-139 | or 80-89 |
| **Stage 2 HTN** | >=140 | or >=90 |

**Treatment Thresholds (General Population):**
- Target <130/80 mmHg for most adults
- Start pharmacotherapy at >=130/80 for most patients with CVD risk >=10%
- Start at >=140/90 for patients with lower risk

**First-Line Agents:**
- Thiazide diuretics, ACE inhibitors, ARBs, calcium channel blockers

**Key Points:**
- Proper measurement technique essential (seated, rested, appropriate cuff)
- Out-of-office BP monitoring recommended (home BP, ambulatory monitoring)
- White coat hypertension and masked hypertension common

### 3.4 Aspirin Primary Prevention

**Current Evidence:** [^508^] [^513^]

**Key Trials:**
- **ARRIVE (2018):** Aspirin did not lower CVD events in moderate-risk patients; increased GI bleeding [^508^]
- **ASPREE (2018):** No benefit on disability-free survival in adults >70; increased all-cause mortality [^508^]
- **ASCEND (2018):** In diabetics, aspirin reduced serious vascular events but increased major bleeding [^513^]

**Meta-Analysis (Zheng 2019):** [^508^]
- Aspirin associated with reduced composite CV events (HR 0.89) but increased major bleeding (HR 1.43)
- NNT = 265 for CVD benefit; NNH = 210 for bleeding harm

**Current Guidelines (ACC/AHA):** [^508^]
- **Do NOT routinely recommend aspirin for primary prevention**
- May consider for select adults 40-70 years at higher ASCVD risk WITHOUT increased bleeding risk
- Do NOT use for primary prevention in adults >70 or adults with increased bleeding risk
- Always involve shared decision-making

**Aspirin remains indicated for secondary prevention** (established ASCVD, recent MI, stroke, PCI, CABG)

### 3.5 Lifestyle Counseling

**Diet:**
- Emphasize vegetables, fruits, whole grains, legumes, nuts, fish, poultry
- Limit saturated fats, trans fats, sodium, red meat, sweets, sugar-sweetened beverages
- Mediterranean and DASH diets have strongest evidence for CVD prevention

**Physical Activity:**
- At least 150 minutes/week moderate-intensity or 75 minutes/week vigorous [^489^]
- Muscle-strengthening activities 2+ days/week [^492^]

**Weight Management:**
- BMI 18.5-24.9 kg/m2 goal
- Weight loss of 3-5% produces clinically meaningful health benefits
- 10% weight loss produces greater benefits

**Smoking Cessation:**
- Most important single preventive measure
- Combination of counseling + pharmacotherapy most effective

**Alcohol:**
- If consumed: limit to <=1 drink/day for women, <=2 drinks/day for men
- No safe level for cardiovascular protection

## 4. Diabetes Prevention

### 4.1 Prediabetes Definitions

**Diagnostic Criteria (ADA):**

| Test | Prediabetes Range | Diabetes Range |
|------|------------------|----------------|
| **Fasting Plasma Glucose (FPG)** | 100-125 mg/dL (IFG) | >=126 mg/dL |
| **2-h OGTT** | 140-199 mg/dL (IGT) | >=200 mg/dL |
| **HbA1c** | 5.7-6.4% | >=6.5% |

**Risk Factors for Prediabetes:**
- Overweight/obesity (BMI >=25, or >=23 in Asian Americans)
- Age >=35 years
- First-degree relative with diabetes
- History of gestational diabetes
- Physical inactivity
- Race/ethnicity (African American, Hispanic/Latino, Native American, Asian American, Pacific Islander)
- Hypertension, dyslipidemia, PCOS, sleep apnea, CVD

### 4.2 Diabetes Prevention Program (DPP)

**Original DPP Trial (2002):** [^485^] [^486^]
- Major multicenter RCT of intensive lifestyle intervention
- Goals: 7% weight loss, 150+ minutes/week physical activity
- **Results: 58% reduction in diabetes incidence** (71% in adults >=60 years)
- Metformin: 31% reduction

**10-Year Follow-up (DPPOS):** [^485^]
- Participants remained one-third less likely to develop diabetes a decade later
- Average delay in diabetes onset: ~4 years

**CDC National DPP:** [^485^] [^486^]
- Year-long structured lifestyle change program
- **16 weekly sessions** (months 1-6), then **6 monthly sessions** (months 7-12)
- Goals: 5-7% weight loss, 150 minutes/week physical activity
- Delivered in person, online, or combination
- Covered by Medicare, many Medicaid programs, and most private insurers

**Real-World Effectiveness:** [^495^]
- 35.5% of participants achieve 5% weight loss
- Average weight loss: 4.2%
- 41.8% meeting physical activity goal
- Greater attendance = greater weight loss (0.3% per additional session)

### 4.3 Metformin for Prevention

**ADA Recommendations:** [^471^]
- Metformin should be considered for diabetes prevention, especially for:
  - BMI >=35 kg/m2
  - Age <60 years
  - Women with prior gestational diabetes
  - Both IFG + IGT (very high risk)

**Dosing:**
- Start 500 mg daily with evening meal
- Titrate to 500 mg twice daily, then 850 mg twice daily as tolerated
- Maximum: 1000 mg twice daily

**Monitoring:**
- eGFR should be >=30 mL/min/1.73m2
- Vitamin B12 levels periodically with long-term use
- Discontinue if eGFR falls below 30

**Note:** No medication is FDA-approved specifically for diabetes prevention

### 4.4 Screening Intervals

**ADA Screening Recommendations:**
- **Age >=35:** Screen regardless of risk factors
- **Age <35 with risk factors:** Screen
- **Normal result:** Rescreen every 3 years
- **Prediabetes:** Test annually
- **Overweight/obese children/adolescents:** Start at age 10 or onset of puberty if risk factors present

**Preferred Test:** HbA1c, FPG, or 2-hour OGTT (any is acceptable; abnormal result should be confirmed)

## 5. Smoking Cessation

### 5.1 The 5As and 5Rs Frameworks

**The 5As (Clinical Intervention):**
1. **Ask** about tobacco use at every visit
2. **Advise** to quit in clear, strong, personalized manner
3. **Assess** willingness to make a quit attempt
4. **Assist** with quit plan (medication + counseling)
5. **Arrange** follow-up within first week after quit date

**The 5Rs (For Those Not Ready to Quit):**
1. **Relevance** - Encourage patient to identify personal reasons to quit
2. **Risks** - Ask patient to identify potential negative consequences
3. **Rewards** - Ask patient to identify benefits of quitting
4. **Roadblocks** - Ask patient to identify barriers to quitting
5. **Repetition** - Repeat motivation intervention at every visit

### 5.2 Pharmacotherapy

**WHO 2024 Recommendations:** [^477^]

**First-Line Options (Strong recommendation, high certainty):**

| Medication | Dosing | Key Considerations |
|------------|--------|-------------------|
| **Varenicline** | 0.5mg daily x3 days, then 0.5mg BID x4 days, then 1mg BID for 12 weeks | Most effective single agent; nausea, vivid dreams; contraindicated in severe renal impairment |
| **Combination NRT** | Patch (long-acting) + gum/lozenge/inhaler (short-acting) | Patch provides baseline; short-acting for breakthrough cravings |
| **Bupropion SR** | 150mg daily x3 days, then 150mg BID; start 1-2 weeks before quit date | Contraindicated in seizure disorder, eating disorders, MAOIs; monitor for mood changes |

**Also Recommended:**
- **Cytisine:** Partial nicotine agonist; strong recommendation, moderate certainty [^477^]

**Combination Therapy:**
- Bupropion + varenicline: conditional recommendation when inadequate response to first-line [^477^]
- Bupropion + NRT: conditional recommendation

**Evidence Summary:** [^475^] [^477^]
- Varenicline: RR 2.27 vs. placebo (Cochrane)
- Combination NRT more effective than single NRT
- 12-week treatment extension improves cessation rates in those who quit initially
- Combining medication with counseling significantly improves outcomes

### 5.3 Behavioral Counseling

**Effective Components:** [^477^]
- Multisession counseling (up to 5 sessions)
- Duration of at least 10 minutes per session
- Individual, group, and telephone counseling all effective
- Quitlines: 1-800-QUIT-NOW (free, evidence-based)

**Digital Interventions:** [^477^]
- Text messaging: moderate certainty evidence
- Smartphone apps: low certainty
- Internet-based: very low certainty
- May be adjuncts to behavioral and pharmacological support

### 5.4 E-Cigarette Considerations

**Current Evidence:** [^520^]
- USPSTF: Evidence currently **insufficient** to recommend e-cigarettes for smoking cessation
- Cochrane Review (2024): Nicotine e-cigarettes help people stop smoking for at least 6 months; may outperform NRT
- UK NHS recommends e-cigarettes as a quit aid
- No e-cigarette has FDA approval as a smoking cessation therapeutic [^520^]

**Key Points:**
- E-cigarettes may help some smokers quit but are not risk-free
- Dual use (cigarettes + e-cigarettes) is common and less beneficial
- Youth initiation with e-cigarettes is a significant public health concern
- If used for cessation, should be with goal of complete switching, not dual use

## 6. Nutrition and Exercise

### 6.1 US Dietary Guidelines (2020-2025)

**Core Principles:**
- Follow a healthy dietary pattern at every life stage
- Customize and enjoy nutrient-dense food and beverage choices
- Focus on meeting food group needs with nutrient-dense foods and beverages, stay within calorie limits
- Limit foods and beverages higher in added sugars, saturated fat, and sodium; limit alcoholic beverages

**Recommended Dietary Pattern:**
- Vegetables (variety of types and colors)
- Fruits (whole fruits preferred)
- Grains (at least half whole grains)
- Dairy (fat-free or low-fat)
- Protein foods (lean meats, poultry, eggs, seafood, nuts, seeds, soy)
- Oils (vegetable oils, nuts)

**Limit:**
- Added sugars: <10% of calories/day
- Saturated fat: <10% of calories/day
- Sodium: <2,300 mg/day
- Alcoholic beverages: <=2 drinks/day men, <=1 drink/day women

### 6.2 Physical Activity Guidelines

**Adults (18-64 years):** [^487^] [^489^] [^492^]
- **150-300 minutes/week** of moderate-intensity aerobic activity, OR
- **75-150 minutes/week** of vigorous-intensity aerobic activity, OR
- Equivalent combination
- **Muscle-strengthening activities** involving all major muscle groups on **2+ days/week** [^492^]
- Additional benefits with >300 minutes/week moderate activity

**Older Adults (65+ years):**
- Same as adults plus balance training to prevent falls
- Should perform balance activities 3+ days/week

**Children/Adolescents (6-17 years):**
- >=60 minutes/day moderate-to-vigorous physical activity
- Vigorous activity >=3 days/week
- Muscle- and bone-strengthening >=3 days/week

**Intensity Assessment:**
- **Moderate:** Can talk but not sing (3-5.9 METs)
- **Vigorous:** Cannot maintain conversation (>=6 METs)
- Examples moderate: brisk walking, water aerobics, cycling, dancing, doubles tennis
- Examples vigorous: running, swimming laps, singles tennis, basketball

**Key Messages:** [^489^]
- Some activity is better than none
- Reduce sedentary time; even light activity offsets some risk
- Activity can be accumulated in short bouts throughout the day

### 6.3 Obesity Screening and Counseling

**Screening:**
- **BMI** calculated at least annually: Weight (kg) / Height (m2)
  - Underweight: <18.5
  - Normal: 18.5-24.9
  - Overweight: 25-29.9
  - Obesity Class I: 30-34.9
  - Obesity Class II: 35-39.9
  - Obesity Class III: >=40
- **Waist circumference:** Men >40 inches, Women >35 inches = increased risk

**Behavioral Counseling (USPSTF):**
- Adults with overweight/obesity: Offer or refer to intensive behavioral interventions (Grade B)
- **6-12 month programs** with >=12 sessions
- Components: behavioral management, physical activity, dietary changes
- Goal: 5-10% weight loss

**Pharmacotherapy:**
- Consider for BMI >=30, or >=27 with weight-related comorbidity
- Options: GLP-1 receptor agonists (semaglutide, liraglutide), orlistat, phentermine/topiramate, naltrexone/bupropion
- Must be combined with lifestyle modifications

**Bariatric Surgery:**
- BMI >=40, or >=35 with obesity-related comorbidities
- Most effective treatment for severe obesity

### 6.4 Mediterranean and DASH Diets

**Mediterranean Diet:**
- Emphasis: Olive oil, fruits, vegetables, whole grains, legumes, nuts, fish
- Limited: Red meat, processed foods, sweets
- Strong evidence for CVD prevention, stroke reduction, cognitive benefits
- PREDIMED trial: 30% reduction in CV events

**DASH Diet:**
- Emphasis: Fruits, vegetables, whole grains, lean protein, low-fat dairy
- Limited: Sodium, sweets, red meat
- Proven to lower blood pressure
- Also reduces LDL cholesterol

### 6.5 Alcohol Screening and Counseling

**USPSTF Recommendation:** [^511^] [^514^]
- **Screen adults >=18 years for unhealthy alcohol use** (Grade B)
- Provide brief behavioral counseling for those engaged in risky/hazardous drinking

**Screening Tools:**
- **AUDIT-C** (3 questions): Best accuracy; 1-2 minutes
- **SASQ** (Single question): "How many times in past year have you had >=5 (men) or >=4 (women/65+) drinks in a day?"
- **AUDIT** (10 questions): More comprehensive; 2-5 minutes

**Risky Drinking Thresholds:** [^511^]
- Women/Adults 65+: >3 drinks/day, >7 drinks/week
- Men 21-64: >4 drinks/day, >14 drinks/week
- Pregnant women: No alcohol

**Intervention:**
- Brief counseling (6-15 minutes) effective for reducing drinking
- Behavioral counseling interventions include: feedback, advice, goal-setting, follow-up

## 7. Mental Health Screening

### 7.1 Depression Screening

**USPSTF Recommendation:**
- Screen all adults for depression, including pregnant and postpartum persons (Grade B)
- Screening positive requires confirmatory diagnostic assessment

**PHQ-2 (Initial Screen):**
- Over the last 2 weeks, how often have you been bothered by:
  1. Little interest or pleasure in doing things?
  2. Feeling down, depressed, or hopeless?
- Scoring: 0-3 for each (Not at all=0, Several days=1, More than half=2, Nearly every day=3)
- **Positive screen:** >=3 total -> proceed to PHQ-9

**PHQ-9 (Full Assessment):**
- 9 items corresponding to DSM-5 criteria for major depression
- Same scoring as PHQ-2 (0-27 total)
- **Interpretation:**
  - 1-4: Minimal depression
  - 5-9: Mild depression
  - 10-14: Moderate depression
  - 15-19: Moderately severe depression
  - 20-27: Severe depression
- **Question 9** (thoughts of being better off dead or hurting oneself) requires suicide risk assessment

**Perinatal Depression:** [^503^]
- Screen: First prenatal visit, at least once each trimester, 6-week postpartum visit, 6 and 12 months
- Tools: PHQ-9 or EPDS (Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale)
- EPDS cutoff: >=10 positive
- Screen fathers/partners when possible (lower cutoff recommended)

### 7.2 Anxiety Screening

**USPSTF 2023 Recommendation:** [^505^]
- Screen all adults <65 years for anxiety disorders, including pregnant/postpartum (Grade B)
- Applies to asymptomatic adults without diagnosed mental health disorder

**GAD-2 (Initial Screen):**
- Over the last 2 weeks, how often bothered by:
  1. Feeling nervous, anxious, or on edge?
  2. Not being able to stop or control worrying?
- Scoring: 0-6 total; >=3 positive screen -> proceed to GAD-7

**GAD-7 (Full Assessment):**
- 7 items; scored 0-21
- **Interpretation:**
  - 0-4: Minimal anxiety
  - 5-9: Mild anxiety
  - 10-14: Moderate anxiety
  - 15-21: Severe anxiety
- Validated in pregnant persons; GAD-2 sensitivity 1.0 at cutoff >=1 [^505^]

**Other Tools:**
- EPDS anxiety subscale (perinatal)
- Geriatric Anxiety Scale (GAS) and Geriatric Anxiety Inventory (GAI) for older adults [^505^]

### 7.3 Suicide Risk Screening

**C-SSRS (Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale):** [^509^]

**Screening Version for Primary Care:**
- Ask about: Wish to be dead, non-specific active suicidal thoughts, active ideation with methods, active ideation with intent, active ideation with plan
- Also assess: Preparatory acts/behavior, aborted attempts, interrupted attempts, actual attempts

**Interpretation:**
- **Low risk:** No ideation or plan; outpatient management
- **Moderate risk:** Ideation but no plan; close follow-up, safety planning
- **High risk:** Plan, intent, or recent attempt; immediate psychiatric evaluation

**Key Clinical Points:** [^509^]
- C-SSRS has 67% sensitivity and 76% specificity for suicidal behaviors
- Validated in children as young as 5, veterans with PTSD, psychiatry outpatients
- Recommended by FDA for clinical trials; adopted by CDC
- Should not replace complete clinical evaluation
- Positive screen on any tool requires comprehensive suicide risk assessment

**Safety Planning:**
1. Recognize warning signs
2. Employ internal coping strategies
3. Socialize with others who may offer support
4. Contact family members or friends who may help
5. Contact mental health professionals or agencies
6. Reduce access to lethal means

### 7.4 PTSD Screening

**PC-PTSD-5:**
- 5-item screen for primary care
- In your life, have you ever had any experience that was so frightening, horrible, or upsetting that, in the past month you:
  1. Have had nightmares or thought about it when you didn't want to?
  2. Tried hard not to think about it or went out of your way to avoid situations that remind you of it?
  3. Were constantly on guard, watchful, or easily startled?
  4. Felt numb or detached from others, activities, or your surroundings?
  5. Felt guilty or unable to stop blaming yourself or others for the event?
- **3+ positive responses:** High probability of PTSD; needs further evaluation

### 7.5 Substance Use Screening

**Alcohol:** See Section 6.5 (AUDIT-C, SASQ)

**Drug Use:**
- **DAST-10 (Drug Abuse Screening Test):** 10-item tool for drug use screening
- **Single question:** "How many times in the past year have you used an illegal drug or used a prescription medication for nonmedical reasons?"

**Tobacco:** See Section 5 (Smoking Cessation)

**USPSTF Recommendations:**
- Screen adults >=18 for unhealthy drug use when services for accurate diagnosis and treatment are available (Grade B)
- Evidence insufficient for adolescents

## 8. Screening in Special Populations

### 8.1 LGBTQ+ Health Disparities

**Key Disparities:**
- Higher rates of depression, anxiety, suicidal ideation
- Higher rates of tobacco, alcohol, and drug use
- Barriers to healthcare access including discrimination, lack of culturally competent providers
- Unique cancer risks (e.g., cervical cancer in transgender men who retain cervix)

**Preventive Care Considerations:**
- Screen based on current anatomy, not gender identity alone
- Mental health and trauma-informed care essential
- HPV vaccination for all eligible persons regardless of sexual orientation or gender identity
- Shared decision-making about cancer screening based on organs present

### 8.2 PrEP Counseling

**Indications for PrEP:** [^498^] [^501^]

CDC recommends PrEP for HIV-negative persons who:
- Have anal or vaginal sex in past 6 months AND:
  - Sexual partner with HIV (especially unknown/detectable viral load)
  - Inconsistent condom use
  - STI diagnosis in past 6 months
- Inject drugs AND:
  - Injection partner with HIV
  - Share needles/syringes

**PrEP Options (2024):** [^497^] [^498^]

| Option | Regimen | Efficacy |
|--------|---------|----------|
| **TDF/FTC (Truvada)** | Daily oral | ~99% for sexual transmission |
| **TAF/FTC (Descovy)** | Daily oral | ~99% for sexual transmission (not for receptive vaginal sex) |
| **Cabotegravir (Apretude)** | IM injection every 2 months | ~99% for sexual transmission |
| **Lenacapavir** | SC injection every 6 months | >95% (PURPOSE trials) |

**Baseline and Monitoring Labs:**
- HIV testing (antigen/antibody + HIV RNA)
- Renal function (CrCl for tenofovir-based)
- Hepatitis B serology
- Pregnancy test
- STI screening (gonorrhea, chlamydia, syphilis)
- Hepatitis C antibody

**Follow-up:**
- HIV testing: every 3 months for oral PrEP; prior to each injection for CAB
- Renal function: every 6 months (more frequent if age >50 or eGFR <90)
- STI screening: every 3-6 months

### 8.3 Racial/Ethnic Disparities in Screening

**Breast Cancer:**
- Black women: 40% higher death rate despite similar screening rates [^455^]
- Later stage at diagnosis contributes to disparity
- Ensure equitable follow-up for abnormal mammograms

**Colorectal Cancer:**
- Higher incidence and mortality in Black Americans
- Earlier screening (age 45) addresses some disparity

**Prostate Cancer:**
- Black men: 1.8x higher diagnosis rate, 2.2x higher death rate [^479^]
- Consider earlier screening (age 45) and shared decision-making

**Cervical Cancer:**
- Hispanic/Latina women have highest incidence rates
- Immigrant women may face barriers to screening access

**Systemic Approaches:**
- Culturally tailored outreach and education
- Navigation programs to improve follow-up
- Community health worker integration
- Address structural barriers (transportation, insurance, language)

### 8.4 Rural Health Considerations

**Challenges:**
- Fewer primary care providers per capita
- Longer travel distances to screening facilities
- Limited specialty care for follow-up of abnormal results
- Higher rates of uninsured/underinsured
- Later stage at diagnosis for many cancers
- Higher rates of tobacco use and obesity

**Strategies:**
- Mobile screening units (mammography, lung cancer screening)
- Telehealth for counseling and follow-up
- Community health worker programs
- State and federal programs for uninsured (NBCCEDP, CRCSDP)
- FOBT/FIT as alternatives to colonoscopy for CRC screening

### 8.5 Health Literacy Considerations

**Principles:**
- Use teach-back method to confirm understanding
- Provide materials at 5th-6th grade reading level
- Use visual aids and multimedia resources
- Offer interpreter services for non-English speakers
- Simplify complex medical concepts
- Confirm understanding of screening risks/benefits before procedures

## 9. Occupational Medicine

### 9.1 Pre-Employment Physicals

**Components:**
- Medical history review
- Physical examination relevant to job requirements
- Vision and hearing screening as indicated
- Spirometry for respiratory exposure jobs
- Tuberculosis screening for healthcare workers
- Drug screening per employer policy
- Immunization review and update

**ADA Considerations:**
- Medical exams must be job-related and consistent with business necessity
- Cannot be performed before conditional offer of employment
- Must be same exam for all employees in same job category

### 9.2 OSHA Requirements

**Key OSHA Standards:**
- **Bloodborne Pathogens (1910.1030):** Hepatitis B vaccination offered to at-risk employees; post-exposure follow-up
- **Respiratory Protection (1910.134):** Medical clearance, fit testing for respirator use
- **Hazard Communication (1910.1200):** Right to know about chemical exposures
- **Personal Protective Equipment (1910.132):** Employer-provided PPE
- **Recordkeeping (1904):** Log of work-related injuries/illnesses

**Medical Surveillance Programs:**
- Required for specific exposures (asbestos, lead, noise, respiratory hazards)
- Baseline and periodic monitoring
- Removal from exposure when medical criteria exceeded

### 9.3 Workers' Compensation

**Principles:**
- No-fault insurance system for work-related injuries/illnesses
- Covers medical treatment and wage replacement
- Physician role: Determine causation, treatment plan, work restrictions
- Maximum medical improvement (MMI) determination
- Return-to-work planning with appropriate restrictions

### 9.4 Common Workplace Injuries

**Musculoskeletal Disorders:**
- Back injuries from lifting
- Repetitive strain injuries (carpal tunnel, tendinitis)
- Ergonomic assessment and modification

**Respiratory Conditions:**
- Occupational asthma
- Pneumoconiosis (silicosis, asbestosis, coal workers' pneumoconiosis)
- Work-related COPD

**Dermatologic:**
- Contact dermatitis (irritant and allergic)
- Chemical burns

**Hearing Loss:**
- Noise-induced hearing loss (prevention with hearing conservation programs)

**Return-to-Work Considerations:**
- Light duty assignments during recovery
- Graduated return-to-work programs
- Functional capacity evaluations for permanent restrictions

## 10. Environmental Health

### 10.1 Lead Screening

**Children:** [^496^] [^500^] [^502^]

**USPSTF Recommendation:**
- Evidence insufficient for routine screening of asymptomatic children at average risk (Grade I)
- Screen children at increased risk

**CDC/AAP Recommendations:**
- **Universal blood lead testing** at ages **12 and 24 months** for:
  - Medicaid-enrolled children
  - Children in high-prevalence areas
- Risk-based screening in areas with targeted screening programs
- Test all children 36-72 months who have not been previously screened

**Risk Factors:**
- Housing built before 1960 with chipped paint or renovation
- Sibling/playmate with elevated lead level
- Parent exposed through work/hobbies
- Living near lead-based industry
- Recent immigrant, refugee, internationally adopted child
- Pica behavior (eating non-food items)

**Blood Lead Level Interpretation:**
- **<3.5 ug/dL:** Current CDC reference level
- **3.5-9 ug/dL:** Identify and reduce exposure sources; follow-up testing
- **10-19 ug/dL:** Environmental investigation; nutritional counseling
- **20-44 ug/dL:** Environmental case management; chelation if symptomatic
- **>=45 ug/dL:** Chelation therapy indicated
- **>=70 ug/dL:** Medical emergency

**Pregnant Women:** [^500^] [^506^]
- Risk assessment at earliest prenatal contact
- Blood lead test if any risk factors present
- Confirmed BLL >=5 ug/dL: environmental health assessment
- BLL >=40 ug/dL: discontinue breastfeeding
- BLL >=10 ug/dL: remove from occupational exposure

### 10.2 Radon Exposure

**Overview:**
- Colorless, odorless radioactive gas
- Second leading cause of lung cancer after smoking
- Enters homes through cracks in foundation
- Concentrated in basements and lower levels

**Screening:**
- Test all homes below third floor
- Do-it-yourself test kits or professional measurement
- EPA action level: **4 pCi/L**
- Fix home if radon level >=4 pCi/L

**Risk:**
- Smokers with radon exposure have exponentially higher lung cancer risk
- Non-smokers still at significant increased risk

**Mitigation:**
- Sub-slab depressurization (most common method)
- Sealing cracks and openings
- Increased ventilation

### 10.3 Air Quality

**Health Effects:**
- Particulate matter (PM2.5, PM10): cardiovascular and respiratory disease
- Ground-level ozone: asthma exacerbation, decreased lung function
- Nitrogen dioxide, sulfur dioxide: respiratory irritation
- Air quality alerts: sensitive groups (children, elderly, those with heart/lung disease) should limit outdoor activity

**Clinical Recommendations:**
- Monitor Air Quality Index (AQI) for at-risk patients
- Advise patients with asthma/COPD/heart disease to limit outdoor activity when AQI >100
- Encourage use of HEPA air filters indoors
- Advocate for pollution reduction policies

### 10.4 Climate Health Impacts

**Health Impacts:** [^537^]
- **Heat-related illness:** More frequent/severe heat waves; heat exhaustion, heat stroke
- **Respiratory disease:** Worsening air quality; increased pollen/allergens; wildfire smoke
- **Vector-borne disease:** Expanded range of Lyme, West Nile, dengue, malaria
- **Waterborne disease:** Flooding, compromised water systems
- **Mental health:** Climate anxiety, displacement trauma, disaster-related PTSD
- **Food security:** Crop failures, food distribution disruption
- **Extreme weather:** Injuries, deaths, displacement from hurricanes, floods, wildfires

**CDC Building Resilience Against Climate Effects (BRACE) Framework:** [^537^]
1. Anticipate climate impacts and assess vulnerabilities
2. Project disease burden
3. Assess public health interventions
4. Develop and implement climate and health adaptation plan
5. Evaluate impact and improve quality

**Clinical Recommendations:**
- Screen vulnerable patients for heat and air quality risks
- Provide anticipatory guidance for extreme weather
- Incorporate climate considerations into chronic disease management
- Support community resilience planning
- Advocate for climate mitigation and adaptation policies

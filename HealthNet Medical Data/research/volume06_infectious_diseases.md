## 1. COVID-19

### 1.1 Clinical Overview

COVID-19 is caused by SARS-CoV-2, an RNA coronavirus. As of 2024-2025, the disease has transitioned from pandemic to endemic status in most regions, with ongoing circulation of evolving variants. Clinical presentation ranges from asymptomatic infection to severe pneumonia, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), multisystem inflammatory syndrome, and death.

### 1.2 Current Variants 2024-2025

Since late 2023-2024, SARS-CoV-2 has continued to evolve with Omicron sublineages predominating:

- **JN.1** and its descendants emerged as the dominant global lineage in late 2023
- **KP.2, KP.3, and related FLiRT variants** (named for mutations at F456L and R346T in the spike protein) became increasingly prevalent through 2024
- **XBB-derived variants** continued to circulate with incremental antigenic drift
- These variants demonstrate enhanced immune evasion but generally cause similar clinical severity to prior Omicron subvariants
- Updated monovalent XBB.1.5 vaccines were released for the 2024-2025 season

**Key Point:** Variants continue to evolve with mutations in the spike protein that enhance transmissibility and immune evasion. Severity in vaccinated populations remains generally mild-to-moderate for most healthy individuals.

### 1.3 Diagnosis

#### PCR Testing
- Gold standard for diagnosis; detects viral RNA via NAAT
- High sensitivity and specificity; can detect infection earlier and for longer than antigen tests
- Turnaround time varies (hours to days depending on laboratory)
- Recommended for: symptomatic high-risk individuals, confirmation of negative antigen tests in symptomatic patients, and pre-procedure screening

#### Rapid Antigen Testing
- Detects viral nucleocapsid protein
- Highest sensitivity when viral load is highest (typically first 5-7 days of symptoms)
- Advantage: rapid results (15-30 minutes), widely available
- Limitation: lower sensitivity than PCR, especially in asymptomatic or early infection
- **Strategy:** If symptoms present but antigen test negative, confirm with PCR
- Serial antigen testing (e.g., 48 hours apart) improves sensitivity

### 1.4 Treatment (Outpatient)

Per CDC and IDSA guidelines [^22^]:

| Treatment | Population | Route | Duration | Time from Onset | Key Considerations |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Nirmatrelvir-Ritonavir (Paxlovid)** | Adults; children >=12 years and >=40 kg | Oral | 5 days | <=5 days | Drug-drug interactions; severe renal/hepatic contraindications; no specific testing required |
| **Remdesivir (Veklury)** | Adults; children >=28 days and >=3 kg | IV | 3 days | <=7 days | Infusion over 30-120 min; check liver function and PT before initiation |
| **Molnupiravir (Lagevrio)** | Adults only | Oral | 5 days | <=5 days | Lower efficacy; avoid in pregnancy; contraception required for women of childbearing potential |

#### Nirmatrelvir-Ritonavir (Paxlovid) - Preferred First-Line
- **Efficacy:** Reduced risk of hospitalization and death by ~87% in unvaccinated high-risk outpatients; real-world studies confirm effectiveness in Omicron era [^22^]
- **Dosing:** 300mg nirmatrelvir + 100mg ritonavir orally twice daily for 5 days
- **Renal adjustment:** 150mg nirmatrelvir + 100mg ritonavir twice daily if eGFR 30-59; not recommended if eGFR <30
- **Drug interactions:** Significant CYP3A interactions; requires careful medication review (common interactions: statins, immunosuppressants, anticoagulants, some antiarrhythmics)

#### Remdesivir - Second Preferred
- **Efficacy:** 87% reduction in hospitalization/death in high-risk outpatients
- Requires IV infusion over 3 consecutive days
- Preferred option when Paxlovid contraindicated due to drug interactions

#### Molnupiravir - Alternative Only
- Lower efficacy in clinical trials, limited evidence in vaccinated individuals
- Use only when Paxlovid and remdesivir are unavailable or inappropriate

#### High-Risk Criteria for Treatment
- Age >=65 years
- Chronic lung, heart, kidney, or liver disease
- Diabetes mellitus
- Immunocompromised state
- Obesity (BMI >=30, or >=25 in some guidelines)
- Pregnancy
- Sickle cell disease
- Neurodevelopmental disorders

### 1.5 Post-COVID / Long COVID

#### Definition
Post-COVID-19 condition (long COVID) is broadly defined as symptoms persisting >=3 months after acute SARS-CoV-2 infection, lasting at least 2 months, without alternative explanation (WHO definition) [^263^].

#### Epidemiology
- Approximately 15% of people still have symptoms at 12 months per global estimates
- More common after severe acute illness but can occur after mild infection
- Risk factors: female sex, older age, pre-existing conditions, severe acute infection

#### Common Symptoms
- Fatigue (most common)
- Dyspnea/exercise intolerance
- Cognitive impairment ("brain fog")
- Post-exertional symptom exacerbation
- Headache, sleep disturbances
- Anosmia/dysgeusia (olfactory/gustatory dysfunction)
- POTS (postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome)
- Musculoskeletal pain

#### Treatment Approaches (Per Updated Guidelines) [^79^]
- **Fatigue:** Rehabilitation therapy, graded exercise, energy conservation; rule out other causes
- **Cognitive impairment:** Screen objectively; treat sleep disorders, mood disorders, pain; referral for neuropsychological evaluation
- **Headache:** Symptomatic treatment; consider migraine prophylaxis if migraine-like; risk of medication overuse headache
- **Anosmia:** Olfactory training (strongly recommended, Grade A); topical corticosteroid nasal sprays may be considered [^79^]
- **POTS:** Non-pharmacologic first (fluid intake 2-3L/day, NaCl 10-12g/day, compression stockings); pharmacologic options include beta-blockers, ivabradine, midodrine, fludrocortisone
- **Respiratory symptoms:** Respiratory rehabilitation, inspiratory muscle training
- **Psychiatric symptoms:** SSRIs for depression/anxiety; psychiatric referral if severe

#### Prevention
- Vaccination reduces (but does not eliminate) risk of long COVID
- Each reinfection carries risk of developing or worsening long COVID

### 1.6 Vaccination Schedules 2024-2025

Per CDC ACIP recommendations [^274^][^295^][^298^]:

#### Adults 19-64 Years (NOT immunocompromised)
- **Unvaccinated:** 1 dose 2024-25 Moderna or Pfizer-BioNTech; OR 2 doses 2024-25 Novavax at 0, 3-8 weeks
- **Previously vaccinated:** 1 dose 2024-25 Moderna, Novavax, or Pfizer-BioNTech at least 8 weeks after most recent dose

#### Adults >=65 Years
- 2 doses of 2024-25 vaccine recommended, 6 months apart (minimum 2 months)

#### Immunocompromised Persons
- Additional doses recommended; 3-dose initial series followed by booster doses
- Minimum intervals may differ; consult CDC immunization schedule

#### Key Points
- 2024-25 vaccines are updated monovalent formulations targeting XBB.1.5 or JN.1 lineages
- Vaccination is now based on shared clinical decision-making for most adults
- Highest benefit for those at increased risk of severe COVID-19
- No current booster requirement for the general population beyond standard annual dosing

### 1.7 Red Flags
- Oxygen saturation <94% on room air
- Severe dyspnea, respiratory rate >=30/min
- Chest pain, hemoptysis
- Altered mental status
- Signs of septic shock (hypotension, poor perfusion)
- Cyanosis

## 2. Influenza

### 2.1 Clinical Overview

Influenza is an acute respiratory illness caused by influenza A and B viruses. Seasonal epidemics occur annually, typically October through May in the Northern Hemisphere. Complications include pneumonia, myocarditis, encephalitis, secondary bacterial infections, and exacerbation of chronic conditions.

### 2.2 Diagnosis

- **Rapid influenza diagnostic tests (RIDTs):** Point-of-care antigen detection; sensitivity 50-70%, specificity 90-95%; results in 10-15 minutes
- **RT-PCR:** Gold standard; high sensitivity and specificity; preferred for hospitalized patients
- **Viral culture:** Not used for clinical decision-making due to prolonged turnaround
- **Clinical diagnosis:** Can be made based on symptoms during community outbreaks when testing unavailable

**Symptom Clues:** Abrupt onset fever, myalgia, headache, dry cough, sore throat; gastrointestinal symptoms more common in children

### 2.3 Antiviral Treatment

Per CDC recommendations [^259^][^260^]:

| Antiviral | Age | Route | Duration | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Oseltamivir (Tamiflu)** | >=14 days | Oral | 5 days | Twice daily; generic available; renal dose adjustment |
| **Zanamivir (Relenza)** | >=7 years | Inhaled | 5 days | Twice daily; avoid in asthma/COPD; allergy to lactose/milk protein |
| **Peramivir (Rapivab)** | >=6 months | IV (single) | Single dose | Once; for patients unable to take oral meds; renal adjustment |
| **Baloxavir (Xofluza)** | >=5 years | Oral | Single dose | Single dose; not recommended in pregnancy/breastfeeding or complicated illness |

#### Key Treatment Principles
- **Start as early as possible** - ideally within 48 hours of symptom onset
- Treatment initiated >48 hours may still benefit severely ill or immunocompromised patients
- Empiric treatment should not wait for laboratory confirmation in high-risk patients

#### Post-Exposure Prophylaxis
- **Oseltamivir:** 75 mg once daily for 7 days after last exposure
- **Zanamivir:** 10 mg once daily for 7 days
- **Baloxavir:** Single dose for post-exposure prophylaxis (approved for >=5 years)
- Duration for outbreak control in institutions: minimum 2 weeks, continue until 1 week after last known case

### 2.4 High-Risk Groups (Priority for Treatment and Vaccination)
- Adults >=65 years
- Children <2 years (highest risk <6 months)
- Chronic pulmonary, cardiovascular (excluding isolated hypertension), renal, hepatic, neurologic, metabolic, or hematologic conditions
- Immunosuppressed persons
- Pregnant women and women up to 2 weeks postpartum
- Persons <19 years receiving long-term aspirin therapy
- Residents of nursing homes and long-term care facilities
- American Indians and Alaska Natives
- Morbid obesity (BMI >=40)

### 2.5 Complications
- Primary influenza pneumonia
- Secondary bacterial pneumonia (S. aureus, S. pneumoniae, H. influenzae)
- Exacerbation of COPD/asthma
- Myocarditis, pericarditis
- Encephalopathy, Guillain-Barre syndrome
- Rhabdomyolysis
- Sepsis, multi-organ failure

### 2.6 Vaccination
- Annual vaccination recommended for all persons >=6 months without contraindications
- **High-dose or adjuvanted vaccines** preferred for adults >=65 years
- **Contraindications:** Severe allergic reaction to prior influenza vaccine or component (excluding egg allergy - may receive any age-appropriate vaccine); history of Guillain-Barre syndrome within 6 weeks of prior influenza vaccine (relative contraindication)
- **Live attenuated influenza vaccine (LAIV):** Not for immunocompromised, pregnant, children 2-4 years with asthma/wheezing, or close contacts of severely immunocompromised

### 2.7 Red Flags
- Dyspnea, hypoxia
- Altered mental status
- Severe dehydration
- Persistent high fever >3 days
- Chest pain
- Severe weakness in children
- Recurrent fever after initial improvement (possible secondary bacterial infection)

## 3. Tuberculosis

### 3.1 Clinical Overview

Tuberculosis (TB) is caused by *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*, an acid-fast bacillus. It remains one of the world's deadliest infectious diseases. Approximately 25% of the world's population has latent TB infection (LTBI), with 5-10% lifetime risk of progression to active disease.

### 3.2 Latent TB vs Active TB

| Feature | Latent TB Infection (LTBI) | Active TB Disease |
|---|---|---|
| Symptoms | Asymptomatic | Cough >=2-3 weeks, hemoptysis, fever, night sweats, weight loss, fatigue |
| CXR | Usually normal or calcified granuloma | Infiltrates, cavities, nodules |
| Sputum smear | Negative | May be positive |
| Sputum culture | Negative | Positive |
| NAAT | Negative | Positive |
| Infectious | No | Yes (if pulmonary) |
| Treatment | LTBI regimen (see below) | Full TB treatment (4-6 months) |

### 3.3 Diagnosis

#### TST (Tuberculin Skin Test/Mantoux)
- Intradermal injection of PPD; read induration at 48-72 hours
- **Interpretation cutoffs:**
  - >=5 mm: Positive in HIV, recent TB contacts, fibrotic changes on CXR, organ transplant recipients, immunosuppressed
  - >=10 mm: Positive in recent immigrants from high-prevalence countries, injection drug users, residents/employees of high-risk congregate settings, Mycobacteriology lab personnel, persons with clinical conditions that increase risk, children <4 years
  - >=15 mm: Positive in persons with no known risk factors
- **Limitations:** False positives from BCG vaccination or non-tuberculous mycobacteria; requires return visit; operator-dependent

#### IGRA (Interferon-Gamma Release Assay)
- Blood tests (T-SPOT.TB, QuantiFERON-TB Gold) measuring IFN-gamma response to M. tuberculosis-specific antigens
- Not affected by BCG vaccination; only one visit required
- Preferred in BCG-vaccinated individuals and those unlikely to return for TST reading
- **Limitations:** More expensive; processing requirements; less data in very young children; indeterminate results in immunosuppression

#### TST vs IGRA: Comparative Performance [^302^]
- TST generally has higher specificity but lower sensitivity than IGRA
- IGRA (especially T-SPOT.TB) offers more reliable detection in immunocompromised populations (HIV, dialysis, transplant)
- In pediatric populations, TST shows higher sensitivity and lower specificity
- IGRA preferred for immunocompromised patients at high risk of reactivation

### 3.4 LTBI Treatment Regimens

Per CDC 2024-2025 guidelines [^262^]:

CDC preferentially recommends **short-course rifamycin-based 3- or 4-month regimens** over 6- or 9-month isoniazid monotherapy due to higher completion rates:

| Regimen | Duration | Drugs | Frequency | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **3HP** | 3 months | Isoniazid + Rifapentine | Once weekly | Age >=2 years; DOT preferred; not for pregnancy or HIV on interacting ART |
| **4R** | 4 months | Rifampin | Daily | All ages HIV-negative; good for INH-resistant contacts |
| **3HR** | 3 months | Isoniazid + Rifampin | Daily | All ages HIV-negative; avoid with some ART |
| **6H** | 6 months | Isoniazid | Daily | Alternative when rifamycins contraindicated |
| **9H** | 9 months | Isoniazid | Daily | Least preferred due to duration |

- All patients on isoniazid should receive pyridoxine (vitamin B6) 25-50 mg daily
- Rifamycin drug-drug interactions are common; review all medications
- Women using hormonal contraception should add barrier method during rifamycin therapy

### 3.5 Active TB Treatment (Drug-Susceptible)

#### Standard RIPE Therapy
- **Intensive phase (2 months):** Isoniazid + Rifampin + Pyrazinamide + Ethambutol (HRZE)
- **Continuation phase (4 months):** Isoniazid + Rifampin (HR)
- **Total duration:** 6 months for most pulmonary TB
- DOT (directly observed therapy) strongly recommended
- Pyridoxine 25-50 mg daily with isoniazid to prevent neuropathy

#### Updated 4-Month Regimen [^261^]
- A 4-month regimen of isoniazid + rifapentine + moxifloxacin + pyrazinamide is now conditionally recommended for adults >=12 years with drug-susceptible pulmonary TB (WHO/ATS/CDC/IDSA 2024 update)
- Nonsevere TB in children: 4 months vs 6 months of standard therapy both acceptable

### 3.6 Drug-Resistant TB [^261^]

#### Rifampin-Resistant, Fluoroquinolone-Susceptible
- **BPaLM regimen:** Bedaquiline + Pretomanid + Linezolid + Moxifloxacin for 6 months
- Strong recommendation over 15-month or longer regimens

#### Rifampin-Resistant, Fluoroquinolone-Resistant
- **BPaL regimen:** Bedaquiline + Pretomanid + Linezolid for 6 months
- Linezolid preferred at 600 mg daily; therapeutic drug monitoring recommended (trough <2 mcg/mL)

#### Monitoring for BPaLM Regimens
- Baseline and monthly: ECG (QTc), CBC, liver enzymes, bilirubin
- Monitor for linezolid toxicity (myelosuppression, neuropathy)
- Sputum culture monthly

### 3.7 Screening in High-Risk Populations
- Persons with HIV infection
- Recent immigrants from high-prevalence countries
- Residents/employees of correctional facilities, homeless shelters, long-term care
- Healthcare workers
- Contacts of active TB cases
- Persons initiating immunosuppressive therapy (especially anti-TNF agents)
- Persons with silicosis, diabetes, chronic kidney disease on dialysis

### 3.8 Red Flags
- Hemoptysis
- Severe respiratory distress
- Altered mental status
- Military TB pattern on imaging
- Suspected drug resistance or treatment failure
- Pregnant women with suspected active TB

## 4. HIV/AIDS

### 4.1 Clinical Overview

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) targets CD4 T-lymphocytes, leading to progressive immunodeficiency if untreated. With modern antiretroviral therapy (ART), HIV is a manageable chronic condition with near-normal life expectancy.

### 4.2 Screening and Diagnosis

#### 4th Generation HIV Test [^296^]
- **Standard of care:** Detects HIV-1/2 antibodies AND p24 antigen
- **Window period:** p24 antigen detectable as early as 14 days post-exposure
- **Advantage over 3rd gen:** Identifies 28% more infections; can differentiate HIV-1 from HIV-2
- **Positive pattern:** Antigen positive/antibody negative suggests acute infection; requires HIV RNA confirmation
- **Conclusive negative:** Test >3 months after last potential exposure is conclusive

#### HIV RNA (Viral Load)
- Detectable as early as 5-10 days post-transmission
- Initial test of choice if acute HIV infection suspected (<14 days from exposure)
- Also used for indeterminate 4th generation results

### 4.3 PrEP (Pre-Exposure Prophylaxis)

#### Available Regimens [^264^][^265^][^268^][^270^]

| Regimen | Agent | Dosing | Route | Efficacy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **TDF/FTC** | Tenofovir disoproxil fumarate/emtricitabine | Daily oral | Oral | ~99% risk reduction for sexual transmission |
| **TAF/FTC** | Tenofovir alafenamide/emtricitabine | Daily oral | Oral | ~99% (sexual transmission only; NOT studied for receptive vaginal sex) |
| **CAB-LA** | Long-acting cabotegravir | Every 2 months IM | Injectable | ~99% (sexual transmission) |
| **LEN** | Long-acting lenacapavir | Every 6 months SC | Injectable | 96-100% (FDA approved June 2025) [^265^] |

#### PrEP Selection
- All sexually active persons without HIV at substantial risk should be offered PrEP
- Injectable options preferred for those with adherence concerns
- TDF/FTC is the most widely studied and approved for all populations including pregnancy/breastfeeding
- TAF/FTC NOT recommended for persons likely to get HIV through receptive vaginal sex
- LEN and CAB-LA approved for pregnancy/breastfeeding (LEN: AI recommendation; CAB: BII recommendation) [^270^]

#### PrEP Monitoring
- HIV testing every 2-3 months (depending on regimen)
- Renal function (for TDF-containing regimens)
- STI screening at follow-up
- Hepatitis B status at baseline (TDF and TAF treat HBV; do not discontinue if co-infected)

### 4.4 PEP (Post-Exposure Prophylaxis)

#### Updated 2025 CDC Guidelines [^264^]
- **Preferred regimen:** Bictegravir/TAF/FTC (BIC/TAF/FTC) - single pill, once daily
- **Alternative preferred:** TDF/FTC + Dolutegravir (DTG)
- **Duration:** 28 days
- **Timing:** Start as soon as possible, ideally within 2 hours, no later than 72 hours
- BIC/TAF/FTC completion rates >90% (higher than historical rates with older regimens)

### 4.5 ART Treatment

#### Preferred Initial Regimens
- **Integrase strand transfer inhibitors (INSTIs)** are preferred first-line:
  - Bictegravir/TAF/FTC (Biktarvy) - single tablet, once daily
  - Dolutegravir + TAF/FTC or TDF/FTC
  - Dolutegravir/Lamivudine (Dovato) - 2-drug regimen for some patients
- INSTIs favored for: rapid viral suppression, high barrier to resistance, favorable side effect profile
- Goal: viral suppression <50 copies/mL within 3-6 months

### 4.6 U=U (Undetectable = Untransmittable)
- Persons with HIV who maintain undetectable viral load (<200 copies/mL) for at least 6 months do not sexually transmit HIV
- Evidence from HPTN 052, PARTNER, Opposites Attract studies
- Viral load testing every 3-6 months
- Does not apply to perinatal or bloodborne transmission

### 4.7 Opportunistic Infections Prophylaxis

#### Primary Prophylaxis (by CD4 count)

| CD4 Count | OI Risk | Prophylaxis |
|---|---|---|
| <200 | PCP (Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia) | TMP-SMX DS 1 tab daily or 3x/week |
| <100 | Toxoplasmosis | TMP-SMX DS daily (if Toxoplasma IgG+) |
| <50 | Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC) | Azithromycin 1200 mg weekly |
| <150 | Histoplasmosis, Coccidioidomycosis | Consider in endemic areas |
| Any | TB | Treat LTBI if indicated |

- Discontinue prophylaxis after CD4 rises above threshold for >3-6 months on ART with viral suppression

### 4.8 Special Populations

#### Pregnancy
- All pregnant women with HIV should initiate ART regardless of CD4 count
- Goal: undetectable viral load by delivery
- Preferred: INSTI-based regimens with good placental transfer (e.g., DTG)
- Intravenous zidovudine during labor if viral load >1000 copies/mL near delivery
- Breastfeeding NOT recommended in US/Europe settings (formula feeding preferred)

#### Pediatrics
- Children <15 kg need liquid formulations
- DTG preferred for children (palatable, well-tolerated)

### 4.9 Red Flags
- Acute retroviral syndrome (fever, pharyngitis, rash, lymphadenopathy)
- OI symptoms in known HIV: dyspnea (PCP), visual changes (CMV), headache/focal deficits (toxoplasmosis, PML)
- New-onset seizure, altered mental status
- Suspected IRIS (immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome) after ART initiation

## 5. Sexually Transmitted Infections

### 5.1 Chlamydia (*Chlamydia trachomatis*)

#### Diagnosis
- NAAT (nucleic acid amplification test) - gold standard
- Can test urine (first-catch), vaginal/cervical swab, rectal swab, pharyngeal swab
- Screen: sexually active women <25 years; older women with risk factors; all pregnant women; MSM annually

#### Treatment (CDC STI Guidelines 2021) [^280^]
- **First-line:** Doxycycline 100 mg orally twice daily for 7 days
- **Alternative:** Azithromycin 1 g orally in a single dose (preferred in pregnancy, though doxycycline may be used if needed)
- **Note:** Doxycycline is preferred due to superior microbiologic cure, especially for rectal infections
- **Test of cure:** Recommended at 3 months (reinfection is common)

### 5.2 Gonorrhea (*Neisseria gonorrhoeae*)

#### Diagnosis
- NAAT from urine, cervical/vaginal, rectal, or pharyngeal specimens
- Culture with antimicrobial susceptibility testing if treatment failure suspected

#### Treatment [^283^]
- **Uncomplicated urogenital, anorectal, or pharyngeal:** Ceftriaxone 500 mg IM single dose (<150 kg); 1 g IM if >=150 kg
- **Always treat for chlamydia concurrently** if not excluded: Doxycycline 100 mg BID x 7 days
- **No reliable alternatives** for pharyngeal infection; consult infectious disease if cephalosporin allergy
- For IgE-mediated penicillin/cephalosporin allergy: Gentamicin 240 mg IM + Azithromycin 2 g orally (single dose each)

#### Antimicrobial Resistance (AMR) Concerns
- Fluoroquinolone resistance is widespread in the US; fluoroquinolones not recommended since 2007
- Cephalosporins remain the only reliable class; monitor MIC trends
- Suspected treatment failure: retreat with ceftriaxone, obtain culture with susceptibility testing, report to CDC
- Gentamicin + azithromycin alternative regimen has lower efficacy for pharyngeal infection

#### Test of Cure
- All pharyngeal infections: test of cure at 7-14 days (culture preferred)
- Not routinely needed for urogenital/anorectal if symptoms resolve
- Retest at 3 months due to high reinfection rates

### 5.3 Syphilis (*Treponema pallidum*)

#### Stages and Treatment [^280^]

| Stage | Clinical Features | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary** | Painless chancre at inoculation site | Benzathine penicillin G 2.4 million units IM single dose |
| **Secondary** | Rash (palms/soles), mucous patches, condyloma lata, generalized lymphadenopathy | Benzathine penicillin G 2.4 million units IM single dose |
| **Early latent** | Asymptomatic, <1 year duration | Benzathine penicillin G 2.4 million units IM single dose |
| **Late latent** | Asymptomatic, >1 year or unknown duration | Benzathine penicillin G 2.4 million units IM weekly x 3 doses |
| **Tertiary** | Gummas, cardiovascular, neurologic involvement | Benzathine penicillin G 2.4 million units IM weekly x 3 doses |
| **Neurosyphilis** | Cranial nerve deficits, meningitis, stroke, dementia | Aqueous crystalline penicillin G 3-4 million units IV q4h x 10-14 days OR Procaine penicillin G 2.4 million units IM daily + Probenecid 500 mg QID x 10-14 days |
| **Ocular/otosyphilis** | Vision changes, hearing loss, tinnitus | Same as neurosyphilis |

#### Key Points
- Penicillin is the only therapy with documented efficacy for neurosyphilis and in pregnancy
- If penicillin-allergic (non-pregnant): Doxycycline 100 mg BID x 14 days (early) or x 28 days (late)
- Pregnant patients MUST receive penicillin; desensitization if allergic
- **Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction:** Acute febrile reaction within 24 hours of treatment; NOT an allergy; manage with antipyretics
- Follow RPR/VDPR titers at 6, 12, and 24 months (4-fold decline expected)

### 5.4 Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (PID)

#### Diagnosis
- Minimum criteria: pelvic/abdominal pain PLUS cervical motion tenderness, uterine tenderness, or adnexal tenderness
- Additional criteria: fever >38.3 C, abnormal mucopurulent discharge, elevated ESR/CRP, documented cervical infection

#### Treatment (2024 Guidelines) [^287^][^285^][^291^]

**Outpatient:**
- Ceftriaxone 500 mg IM single dose (1 g if >=150 kg)
- PLUS Doxycycline 100 mg orally twice daily x 14 days
- PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg orally twice daily x 14 days
- 72-hour follow-up mandatory

**Inpatient:**
- Ceftriaxone 1 g IV daily
- PLUS Doxycycline 100 mg oral/IV twice daily
- PLUS Metronidazole 500 mg oral/IV twice daily
- Continue IV until 24 hours after clinical improvement, then oral to complete 14 days

**Alternative:** Clindamycin 900 mg IV TID + Gentamicin IV, then oral clindamycin or doxycycline + metronidazole

#### Admission Criteria
- Surgical emergency cannot be excluded
- Tubo-ovarian abscess
- Pregnancy
- Severe illness, vomiting, temperature >38.5 C
- Unable to tolerate oral therapy
- No clinical response to outpatient therapy

#### RCT Evidence [^287^]
- Metronidazole should be routinely added to ceftriaxone + doxycycline: reduces endometrial anaerobes, decreases M. genitalium, and reduces pelvic tenderness compared to ceftriaxone + doxycycline alone

### 5.5 Trichomoniasis (*Trichomonas vaginalis*)
- **Diagnosis:** NAAT (preferred), wet mount (51-65% sensitivity)
- **Treatment:** Metronidazole 2 g orally single dose OR 500 mg BID x 7 days; OR Tinidazole 2 g orally single dose
- **Note:** Treat sexual partners; test of cure not routine except in pregnancy (retest at 3 months)

### 5.6 HSV (Herpes Simplex Virus)

#### Treatment [^297^]

| Indication | Regimen |
|---|---|
| **First episode (genital)** | Acyclovir 400 mg TID x 7-10 days OR Valacyclovir 1 g BID x 7-10 days |
| **Recurrent episode** | Acyclovir 800 mg BID x 5 days OR Valacyclovir 500 mg BID x 3 days (start within 48 hours) |
| **Suppressive therapy** | Acyclovir 400 mg BID OR Valacyclovir 500 mg-1 g daily OR Famciclovir 250 mg BID |

#### Suppressive Therapy Indications
- Frequent recurrences (>=6/year)
- Severe symptoms
- To reduce transmission to discordant partner (reduces but does not eliminate risk)
- Pregnancy: suppressive therapy from 36 weeks to reduce cesarean need

### 5.7 HPV (Human Papillomavirus) Vaccination [^292^]

- **9-valent vaccine (Gardasil 9):** Covers HPV types 6, 11, 16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52, 58
- **Routine age:** 11-12 years (can start at age 9)
- **Catch-up:** Through age 26 years for all; ages 27-45 through shared clinical decision-making
- **Dosing:** 2 doses if series started before 15th birthday (0, 6-12 months); 3 doses if started at >=15 years or immunocompromised (0, 1-2 months, 6 months)
- Also recommended for MSM and immunocompromised persons through age 26

### 5.8 Doxycycline Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (Doxy-PEP)
- Doxycycline 200 mg within 72 hours after sex reduces syphilis, chlamydia, and gonorrhea in MSM and transgender women
- CDC approved Doxy-PEP in 2023 for persons with a history of bacterial STI in the past 12 months
- Does not reduce gonorrhea as effectively; does not replace condoms
- Concerns about antimicrobial resistance with widespread use

## 6. Urinary Tract Infections

### 6.1 Clinical Overview

UTIs are among the most common bacterial infections. Classification by anatomic location (lower = cystitis; upper = pyelonephritis) and complexity (uncomplicated vs. complicated) guides management.

### 6.2 Uncomplicated Cystitis (Acute Simple Cystitis)

#### Definition
- Non-pregnant, immunocompetent women with acute cystitis and no structural/functional urinary tract abnormalities

#### First-Line Treatment (2024 WikiGuidelines) [^266^]

| Antibiotic | Duration | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Nitrofurantoin** | 5 days | First-line; excellent E. coli activity; may use with CrCl >=30 mL/min |
| **Fosfomycin tromethamine** | Single dose | Alternative first-line; good for suspected MDR organisms |
| **Pivmecillinam** | 3 days | Where available; good safety profile |
| **TMP-SMX** | 3 days | Only if local E. coli resistance <20% and no sulfa allergy |

#### Second-Line/Reserve
- **Fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin):** 3 days - avoid due to collateral damage; reserve for alternatives not suitable
- **Beta-lactams:** 5-7 days - if other options unsuitable; amoxicillin-clavulanate or cephalexin preferred

#### Key Points [^266^]
- Nitrofurantoin is the drug of choice for uncomplicated cystitis based on robust efficacy data and ability to spare systemically active agents
- Fluoroquinolones should not be used if other treatment options exist
- Beta-lactam duration is less certain due to heterogeneity in studies

### 6.3 Complicated Cystitis / Complicated UTI

#### Definition
- UTIs in men, pregnant women, children, elderly, immunocompromised, catheter-associated, urinary tract abnormalities, recurrent UTIs, or recent instrumentation

#### Treatment Approach
- Urine culture before antibiotics when feasible
- Empiric therapy based on local resistance and patient risk factors
- Longer duration typically needed (7-14 days depending on severity)
- **Ceftriaxone 1-2 g IV** recommended empiric choice for patients requiring IV therapy

### 6.4 Pyelonephritis

#### Treatment (2024 Guidelines) [^266^]

| Antibiotic | Duration | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Fluoroquinolones** | 5-7 days | Levofloxacin 750 mg daily or ciprofloxacin; highly bioavailable |
| **Dose-optimized beta-lactams** | 7 days | Based on RCTs showing comparable outcomes to longer regimens |
| **TMP-SMX** | 7-14 days | If susceptible; based on culture |

#### IV-to-Oral Transition
- Ceftriaxone 1-2 g IV daily for empiric therapy if hospitalization needed
- Transition to oral when clinically improving (typically 24-48 hours)
- Total duration 7-14 days depending on agent and clinical response

### 6.5 Recurrent UTIs

#### Definition
- >=2 episodes in 6 months or >=3 episodes in 1 year

#### Prevention Strategies [^266^]

| Strategy | Evidence Level | Details |
|---|---|---|
| **Cranberry products** | Clear recommendation | 36 mg proanthocyanidins daily; reduces recurrences in women |
| **Vaginal estrogen** | Clear recommendation | For postmenopausal women; reduces UTIs |
| **Methenamine hippurate** | Clear recommendation | 1 g BID; antimicrobial-sparing option |
| **Continuous/postcoital antibiotics** | Clinical review | TMP-SMX, nitrofurantoin; effective but resistance concerns |
| **Increased water intake** | Clinical review | Additional 1.5 L daily may help |
| **Probiotics** | Insufficient evidence | Studies heterogeneous |

### 6.6 Prostatitis

#### Acute Bacterial Prostatitis
- **Pathogens:** E. coli most common; Enterococcus, Pseudomonas, Klebsiella
- **Treatment:** Fluoroquinolone (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin) or TMP-SMX for 2-4 weeks
- If severe/systemically ill: IV ceftriaxone or fluoroquinolone initially
- **Avoid:** Nitrofurantoin (poor prostatic penetration), fosfomycin (limited data)

#### Chronic Bacterial Prostatitis
- Fluoroquinolones or TMP-SMX for 4-6 weeks
- May require prolonged or suppressive therapy

### 6.7 Asymptomatic Bacteriuria

- **General rule:** Do NOT treat (including elderly, diabetics, spinal cord injury patients)
- **Exceptions where treatment IS indicated:**
  - Pregnancy (screen at first prenatal visit; treat if positive; nitrofurantoin or amoxicillin-clavulanate preferred)
  - Before urologic procedures with mucosal bleeding
  - Renal transplant recipients (first 1-2 months)
  - Neutropenia (some guidelines)
- Screening and treatment not beneficial in elderly institutionalized patients and may cause harm

## 7. Skin and Soft Tissue Infections

### 7.1 Cellulitis and Erysipelas

#### Clinical Differentiation
- **Erysipelas:** Superficial, well-demarcated, raised borders; usually GAS (*Streptococcus pyogenes*)
- **Cellulitis:** Deeper, poorly demarcated; mixed streptococcal and staphylococcal

#### Treatment
- **Mild (outpatient):** Cephalexin 500 mg QID OR Dicloxacillin 500 mg QID x 5-7 days
- **If MRSA risk or penicillin allergy:** TMP-SMX DS 1-2 tabs BID OR Doxycycline 100 mg BID
- **Note:** TMP-SMX and doxycycline have limited streptococcal coverage; add amoxicillin if concern for streptococcal component
- **Severe (inpatient):** Cefazolin OR oxacillin/nafcillin IV; add vancomycin if MRSA concern
- **Elevate affected limb; mark extent of erythema**

### 7.2 Impetigo
- **Non-bullous:** *S. aureus* or *S. pyogenes*; mupirocin 2% ointment TID x 5 days (limited lesions); oral cephalexin if extensive
- **Bullous:** *S. aureus* (exfoliative toxins); oral dicloxacillin or cephalexin
- **Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis:** Can occur after impetigo (unlike pharyngitis)

### 7.3 MRSA (Methicillin-Resistant *S. aureus*)

#### Risk Factors
- Prior MRSA infection/colonization, recent hospitalization, long-term care residence, injection drug use, close contact with MRSA, athletes, military

#### Treatment for Purulent SSTI
- **Abscess:** Incision and drainage (I&D) is primary treatment; antibiotics for surrounding cellulitis or severe infection
- **Oral options:** TMP-SMX DS 1-2 tabs BID, Doxycycline 100 mg BID, Clindamycin 300-450 mg TID-QID
- **IV options:** Vancomycin, Daptomycin, Linezolid, Ceftaroline
- **Decolonization:** Mupirocin nasal ointment + chlorhexidine body wash for recurrent infections

### 7.4 Abscess
- **Primary treatment:** Incision and drainage
- **Indications for adjunctive antibiotics:** Surrounding cellulitis, systemic symptoms, immunocompromise, facial/hand/genital location, failed I&D alone, multiple lesions, extremes of age
- **Antibiotic duration:** 5-10 days after I&D

### 7.5 Necrotizing Fasciitis [^286^][^293^]

#### Clinical Clues ("Hard Signs")
- Rapidly spreading erythema/edema beyond visible infection
- Severe pain out of proportion to examination
- Bullae, skin necrosis, ecchymosis, crepitus
- Systemic toxicity (fever, tachycardia, hypotension, altered mental status)
- LRINEC score can help risk-stratify (WBC, CRP, Na, glucose, Cr, Hgb)

#### Treatment
- **Surgical emergency:** Immediate surgical debridement is mandatory and life-saving
- **Empiric antibiotics (broad spectrum):**
  - Vancomycin OR Linezolid (MRSA coverage)
  - PLUS Piperacillin-tazobactam OR Carbapenem (gram-negative and anaerobic coverage)
  - PLUS Clindamycin (toxin suppression, streptococcal coverage)
- **Documented GAS necrotizing fasciitis:** Penicillin + Clindamycin
- **Duration:** Until definitive debridement and clinical improvement, typically 7-14 days; narrow based on cultures
- **Mortality:** 20-40% even with optimal treatment

### 7.6 Diabetic Foot Infections [^279^]

#### Classification (IDSA/IWGDF)
- **Uninfected:** No systemic or local symptoms
- **Mild:** Superficial, limited to skin/subcutaneous tissue; erythema <2 cm
- **Moderate:** Erythema >2 cm OR deeper structures (tendon, joint, bone)
- **Severe:** Systemic toxicity or metabolic instability

#### Treatment Principles
- **Mild:** Oral antibiotics targeting Staph and Strep; cephalexin or amoxicillin-clavulanate x 1-2 weeks
- **Moderate:** Broad-spectrum oral or IV; cover MRSA if risk factors; 1-3 weeks
- **Severe:** Broad-spectrum IV; include Pseudomonas coverage if risk factors; 2-4 weeks
- **Osteomyelitis:** Assess with probe-to-bone test, MRI; treat 4-6 weeks if bone not resected; surgical evaluation
- **Wound care:** Debridement, offloading, glycemic control essential
- **MRSA coverage:** If prior MRSA, high prevalence, or severe infection (vancomycin, linezolid, doxycycline, TMP-SMX)

## 8. Travel Medicine

### 8.1 Pre-Travel Consultation Framework
- Timing: Ideally 4-6 weeks before travel (some vaccines require multiple doses)
- Assess: Destination, duration, activities, accommodations, medical history, medications, pregnancy, immune status
- Documentation: International Certificate of Vaccination or Prophylaxis (ICVP) where required

### 8.2 Malaria Prophylaxis

#### Risk Assessment
- Malaria risk varies by country, region, season, and altitude
- Check CDC Yellow Book or WHO malaria maps for current risk by destination

#### Chemoprophylaxis Options

| Drug | Regimen | Start | Duration | Contraindications/Cautions |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Atovaquone-proguanil (Malarone)** | Daily | 1-2 days before | 7 days after | Pregnancy (avoid in 1st trimester unless necessary); CrCl <30 |
| **Doxycycline** | Daily | 1-2 days before | 4 weeks after | Photosensitivity; esophagitis; pregnancy; children <8 years |
| **Mefloquine** | Weekly | >=2 weeks before | 4 weeks after | Neuropsychiatric history; cardiac conduction abnormalities |
| **Tafenoquine (Arakoda)** | Daily loading then weekly | 3 days before | 1 dose after (last day of exposure) | G6PD deficiency screening REQUIRED; hemolytic anemia risk; contraindicated in pregnancy |
| **Primaquine** | Daily | 1-2 days before | 7 days after | G6PD testing required; only for primary P. vivax/ovale prophylaxis in some areas |

#### Key Points
- No chemoprophylaxis is 100% effective; mosquito bite prevention essential
- P. falciparum is most deadly; chloroquine resistance is widespread
- Standby emergency treatment (artemether-lumefantrine or atovaquone-proguanil) for remote travel >24 hours from medical care

### 8.3 Traveler's Diarrhea

#### Prevention
- Food and water precautions: "Boil it, cook it, peel it, or forget it"
- Hand hygiene
- Bismuth subsalicylate (Pepto-Bismol) for short-term prophylaxis if high-risk travel
- Antibiotic prophylaxis NOT routinely recommended; consider for immunocompromised or critical travel

#### Treatment
- **Mild (1-2 loose stools/day, no fever):** Loperamide, hydration, bismuth subsalicylate
- **Moderate (>=3 loose stools/day, distressing symptoms):** Azithromycin 500 mg daily x 1-3 days OR Rifaximin 200 mg TID x 3 days; add loperamide if no blood/fever
- **Severe (bloody diarrhea, fever, severe abdominal pain):** Azithromycin 500 mg daily x 3 days; avoid loperamide; seek medical care
- **Fluoroquinolone resistance** is widespread (especially Campylobacter in South/Southeast Asia); azithromycin preferred

### 8.4 Yellow Fever [^272^][^273^]
- Live attenuated vaccine; single dose provides lifelong protection
- ICVP valid for life (booster requirement eliminated from IHR in 2016)
- Required for entry to some countries; recommended for travel to endemic/transitional risk areas
- **Contraindications:** Age <6 months, severe immunosuppression, thymus disorder, primary immunodeficiency
- **Precautions:** Age 6-8 months, >=60 years, pregnancy, breastfeeding, asymptomatic HIV with CD4 >200
- Risk of viscerotropic disease (YEL-AVD) and neurotropic disease (YEL-AND) - rare but serious

### 8.5 Dengue
- No routine pre-travel prophylaxis (except Qdenga/TAK-003 vaccine in some endemic regions for select travelers)
- Prevention: Mosquito avoidance (Aedes aegypti daytime biter)
- Clinical: Fever, severe headache, retro-orbital pain, myalgia, arthralgia, rash
- **Warning signs:** Abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, mucosal bleeding, lethargy, hepatomegaly
- Severe dengue (plasma leakage, hemorrhage, organ impairment) - hospitalization required

### 8.6 Zika
- Prevention: Mosquito avoidance; sexual transmission possible
- **Pregnancy:** Avoid travel to endemic areas if possible; if travel necessary, strict mosquito precautions; male partners should use condoms or abstain for 3 months after return; females should wait 2 months before conceiving
- Most infections asymptomatic or mild; Guillain-Barre syndrome is rare complication

### 8.7 Travel Vaccinations Summary

| Vaccine | Indication | Dosing | Duration |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hepatitis A** | Most travelers to developing countries | 2 doses (0, 6-12 months) | Lifelong |
| **Hepatitis B** | Long-term travel, healthcare work, adventure travel, sexual exposure | 2 doses (Heplisav-B: 0, 1 month) or 3 doses (0, 1, 6 months) | Lifelong |
| **Typhoid** | Travel to endemic areas with poor sanitation | Oral (4 doses every other day) or IM single dose | Oral: 5 years; IM: 2 years |
| **Japanese Encephalitis** | Rural/farm exposure in endemic Asia for >=1 month | 2 doses (0, 28 days) | Booster at 1 year, then every 10 years |
| **Rabies (pre-exposure)** | Adventure travel, remote areas, animal exposure | 3 doses (0, 7, 21 or 28 days) | Simplify post-exposure management |
| **Meningococcal ACWY** | Hajj/Umrah, meningitis belt of Africa, epidemics | Single dose | 3-5 years |
| **Polio** | Travel to polio-endemic or outbreak areas | Single booster if completed primary series | Lifetime |
| **MMR** | Travelers without immunity | 1-2 doses | Lifelong |

## 9. Tick-Borne Diseases

### 9.1 Lyme Disease (*Borrelia burgdorferi*) [^277^][^278^]

#### Epidemiology
- Most common vector-borne disease in US
- Endemic areas: Northeast, Mid-Atlantic, Upper Midwest, parts of West Coast, Europe, Asia
- Vector: *Ixodes scapularis* (black-legged/deer tick), *Ixodes pacificus*
- Transmission typically requires tick attachment >=36 hours

#### Clinical Stages

| Stage | Features | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| **Early localized** | Erythema migrans (EM) rash - expanding, often target-like; may have fever, malaise | Doxycycline 100 mg BID x 10 days OR Amoxicillin 500 mg TID x 14 days |
| **Early disseminated** | Multiple EM lesions, cranial nerve palsy (CN VII), meningitis, carditis | Doxycycline 100 mg BID x 14-21 days; IV ceftriaxone for severe meningitis/carditis |
| **Late disseminated** | Arthritis (especially knee), encephalomyelitis, peripheral neuropathy | Oral doxycycline 28 days for arthritis; IV ceftriaxone 2-4 weeks for neurologic |

#### EM Rash Treatment Details [^277^]
- **Adults:** Doxycycline 100 mg BID x 10-14 days (preferred, shorter course); Amoxicillin 500 mg TID x 14 days; Cefuroxime 500 mg BID x 14 days
- **Children:** Doxycycline 4.4 mg/kg/day divided BID (up to 100 mg/dose) x 10-14 days; Amoxicillin 50 mg/kg/day divided TID x 14 days
- **Shorter duration preferred** when different durations shown effective
- **Pregnancy:** Amoxicillin or cefuroxime (doxycycline generally avoided though may be used if necessary)
- Azithromycin is less effective and reserved for true contraindications to first-line agents

#### Single-Dose Doxycycline Prophylaxis
- 200 mg single dose for adults (4.4 mg/kg up to 200 mg for children) within 72 hours of tick removal
- Criteria: Tick identified as *Ixodes* species, attached >=36 hours, endemic area, prophylaxis can be given within 72 hours

#### Post-Treatment Lyme Disease Syndrome (PTLDS)
- 10-20% of patients have persistent symptoms (fatigue, pain, cognitive issues) after treatment
- No evidence that additional antibiotics confer benefit
- Manage symptoms with NSAIDs, physical therapy, supportive care
- Referral to rheumatology/neurology as appropriate

### 9.2 Babesiosis (*Babesia microti*)
- Vector: Same *Ixodes* ticks as Lyme disease
- Clinical: Fever, chills, hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, elevated LFTs
- **Diagnosis:** Blood smear (intraerythrocytic parasites), PCR, serology
- **Treatment (mild-moderate):** Atovaquone 750 mg BID + Azithromycin 500 mg day 1, then 250 mg daily x 7-10 days
- **Treatment (severe):** Clindamycin IV + Quinine PO x 7-10 days
- **Duration:** At least 7-10 days; extend if immunocompromised
- Exchange transfusion for high-grade parasitemia (>10%), severe hemolysis, or organ failure
- Test for co-infection with Lyme and anaplasmosis

### 9.3 Anaplasmosis (*Anaplasma phagocytophilum*)
- Clinical: Fever, headache, myalgia, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, elevated transaminases
- **Diagnosis:** PCR during acute illness; serology (4-fold rise in antibody titer)
- **Treatment:** Doxycycline 100 mg BID x 10-14 days (first-line for all ages including children; <8 years if tick-borne rickettsial disease suspected)
- Start empirically if clinical suspicion; do not delay for laboratory confirmation
- Defervescence typically within 24-48 hours of treatment

### 9.4 Ehrlichiosis (*Ehrlichia chaffeensis*, *E. ewingii*)
- Vector: Lone star tick (*Amblyomma americanum*)
- Clinical similar to anaplasmosis: fever, headache, myalgia, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, elevated LFTs
- **Treatment:** Doxycycline 100 mg BID x 7-14 days (same as anaplasmosis)
- **RMSF rule:** When considering tick-borne disease, doxycycline is first-line for all ages (benefits outweigh risks of tooth staining in children)

### 9.5 Co-Infection Considerations
- *Ixodes* ticks can transmit Lyme, babesiosis, and anaplasmosis simultaneously
- Consider co-infection in patients from endemic areas with:
  - Severe/relapsing illness despite Lyme treatment (babesiosis)
  - Leukopenia/thrombocytopenia with Lyme (anaplasmosis)
  - Hemolytic anemia with Lyme (babesiosis)

## 10. Other Important Infections

### 10.1 *Clostridioides difficile* Infection (CDI) [^95^]

#### Classification
- **Non-severe:** WBC <15,000 AND creatinine <1.5x baseline
- **Severe:** WBC >=15,000 OR creatinine >=1.5x baseline
- **Severe-complicated/Fulminant:** Hypotension, shock, ileus, megacolon

#### Treatment (Updated IDSA/SHEA and ESCMID Guidelines)

| Severity | First-Line | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Initial episode, non-severe** | Fidaxomicin 200 mg BID x 10 days | Preferred over vancomycin due to lower recurrence |
| **Initial episode, severe** | Fidaxomicin 200 mg BID x 10 days | Preferred |
| **Alternative** | Vancomycin 125 mg PO QID x 10 days | Acceptable if fidaxomicin unavailable |
| **Severe-complicated** | Vancomycin 500 mg PO/NG QID + Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h + Surgery consult | If ileus: add vancomycin enemas |
| **First recurrence** | Fidaxomicin (standard or extended-pulsed regimen) | Extended-pulsed: 200 mg BID x 5 days, then daily x 20 days |
| **Multiple recurrences** | Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) OR Bezlotoxumab + standard antibiotic | FMT most effective for >=3 recurrences |

#### Key Points [^95^]
- Fidaxomicin is now first-line for initial episodes (both non-severe and severe) due to superior prevention of recurrence
- Metronidazole is no longer preferred (IDSA/SHEA); only for situations where vancomycin/fidaxomicin unavailable
- Bezlotoxumab (monoclonal antibody against toxin B) reduces recurrence when added to standard antibiotic therapy
- FMT retains role in multiple recurrences; timing in severe-complicated disease still being studied
- Avoid antiperistaltic agents if toxic megacolon suspected

### 10.2 Community-Acquired Pneumonia (CAP) [^25^]

#### Common Pathogens
- **Typical:** *Streptococcus pneumoniae* (most common), *Haemophilus influenzae*, *Moraxella catarrhalis*, *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Klebsiella pneumoniae*
- **Atypical:** *Mycoplasma pneumoniae*, *Chlamydophila pneumoniae*, *Legionella pneumophila*
- **Viruses:** Influenza, SARS-CoV-2, RSV, adenovirus, parainfluenza

#### Severity Assessment
- **CURB-65:** Confusion, Urea >7 mmol/L, Respiratory rate >=30, Blood pressure (SBP <90 or DBP <=60), Age >=65
  - 0-1: Low risk - outpatient
  - 2: Intermediate - consider short hospitalization
  - 3-5: High risk - hospitalize, consider ICU
- **PSI (Pneumonia Severity Index):** More complex; also validated for prognosis

#### Outpatient Treatment [^25^]

| Patient Type | Regimen |
|---|---|
| **No comorbidities** | Amoxicillin 1 g TID OR Doxycycline 100 mg BID OR Macrolide (if local pneumococcal resistance <25%) |
| **With comorbidities** (heart, lung, liver, kidney disease, diabetes, alcoholism, malignancy) | Amoxicillin-clavulanate 875/125 mg BID OR Cephalosporin + Macrolide OR Doxycycline; OR Respiratory fluoroquinolone monotherapy (levofloxacin 750 mg daily, moxifloxacin 400 mg daily) |

#### Inpatient Treatment [^25^]

| Setting | Regimen |
|---|---|
| **Non-severe** | Beta-lactam + Macrolide (azithromycin) OR Respiratory fluoroquinolone monotherapy |
| **Severe** | Beta-lactam + Macrolide (STRONG recommendation) OR Beta-lactam + Fluoroquinolone |
| **MRSA risk** | Add vancomycin or linezolid; obtain cultures/nasal PCR; de-escalate if negative |
| **Pseudomonas risk** | Add piperacillin-tazobactam, cefepime, or meropenem; obtain cultures |

- **Beta-lactam options:** Ceftriaxone 1-2 g daily, Ampicillin-sulbactam, Cefotaxime, Ceftaroline
- **Corticosteroids:** NOT recommended routinely; may be considered in refractory septic shock
- **Duration:** Minimum 5 days; extend based on clinical response; afebrile 48-72 hours and clinically stable
- **Follow-up imaging:** NOT routinely recommended

### 10.3 Endocarditis Prophylaxis [^282^][^284^][^300^]

#### Current AHA Guidelines (2021 Update)
Prophylaxis is suggested ONLY for patients at **highest risk of adverse outcome** from viridans group streptococcal IE undergoing dental procedures involving manipulation of gingival tissue, periapical region, or perforation of oral mucosa.

#### Cardiac Conditions Requiring Prophylaxis
- Prosthetic cardiac valve or prosthetic material used for valve repair
- Previous infective endocarditis
- Unrepaired cyanotic congenital heart disease (CHD), including palliative shunts/conduits
- Completely repaired CHD with prosthetic material within first 6 months of procedure
- Repaired CHD with residual defects at site of prosthetic patch/device
- Cardiac transplant recipients with valvulopathy

#### Regimens (Single Dose, 30-60 Minutes Before Procedure) [^300^]

| Patient Situation | Regimen |
|---|---|
| **Standard (oral)** | Amoxicillin 2 g orally |
| **Unable to take oral** | Ampicillin 2 g IV/IM OR Cefazolin/Ceftriaxone 1 g IV/IM |
| **Penicillin-allergic (oral)** | Cephalexin 2 g orally OR Azithromycin/Clarithromycin 500 mg orally |
| **Penicillin-allergic (IV/IM)** | Cefazolin/Ceftriaxone 1 g IV/IM OR Clindamycin 600 mg IV/IM |

**Note:** Clindamycin is no longer routinely recommended as an alternative due to higher rates of adverse reactions including *C. difficile* infection. Cephalexin is preferred for penicillin-allergic patients who have not had anaphylaxis.

#### Procedures NOT Requiring Prophylaxis
- Anesthetic injections through noninfected tissue
- Dental radiographs
- Placement of orthodontic brackets
- Shedding of primary teeth
- Tonsillectomy, respiratory procedures (not involving mucosal incision), GI/GU procedures

### 10.4 Bacterial Meningitis [^281^]

#### Empiric Treatment by Age

| Age Group | Empiric Regimen |
|---|---|
| **Neonates (0-1 month)** | Ampicillin + Cefotaxime +/- Gentamicin (Listeria, GBS, gram-negatives) |
| **1 month - 50 years** | Vancomycin + Ceftriaxone (or Cefotaxime) + Ampicillin (Listeria if risk factors) |
| **>50 years** | Vancomycin + Ceftriaxone + Ampicillin (Listeria coverage) |
| **Immunocompromised** | Vancomycin + Ceftriaxone + Ampicillin +/- Antipseudomonal |
| **Head trauma/surgery/CSF shunt** | Vancomycin + Cefepime or Meropenem |

#### Pathogen-Specific Therapy

| Pathogen | Treatment | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| *S. pneumoniae* (PCN-susceptible) | Penicillin G or Ampicillin | 10-14 days |
| *S. pneumoniae* (PCN-resistant) | Ceftriaxone +/- Vancomycin | 10-14 days |
| *N. meningitidis* | Ceftriaxone or Penicillin G | 5-7 days |
| *H. influenzae* | Ceftriaxone or Cefotaxime | 7-10 days |
| *L. monocytogenes* | Ampicillin + Gentamicin | >=21 days |
| GBS (*S. agalactiae*) | Ampicillin + Gentamicin | 14-21 days |

#### Adjunctive Dexamethasone [^281^]
- **Strong recommendation:** Start with or before first antibiotic dose in suspected bacterial meningitis
- **Dose:** Dexamethasone 0.15 mg/kg IV q6h x 2-4 days (10 mg IV q6h for adults)
- **Specific benefit:** Reduces mortality and neurologic sequelae in pneumococcal meningitis
- **Timing critical:** Must start BEFORE or WITH first antibiotic dose
- **Do NOT use** if meningococcal epidemic suspected or if already started antibiotics
- Discontinue if pathogen is not *S. pneumoniae*

#### General Principles (WHO)
- Start empiric antibiotics ideally within 1 hour of admission
- Blood cultures before antibiotics if no delay
- LP should be performed promptly but should NOT delay antibiotics if critically ill
- If LP delayed due to CT/concerns, start antibiotics immediately

### 10.5 Osteomyelitis [^279^]

#### Classification
- **Hematogenous:** Often affects long bones (children) or vertebral bodies (adults)
- **Contiguous:** Adjacent to soft tissue infection or hardware; common in diabetic foot
- **Vascular insufficiency:** Associated with peripheral vascular disease, diabetes

#### Treatment Principles
- **Acute hematogenous:** IV antibiotics x 2-4 weeks, then oral to complete 4-6 weeks
- **Chronic/contiguous:** 4-6 weeks minimum; often requires surgical debridement
- **Diabetic foot osteomyelitis:** Treat 4-6 weeks if bone retained; 2-5 days post-excision if bone fully resected
- **Oral options for chronic osteomyelitis:** Doxycycline +/- metronidazole, TMP-SMX, Amoxicillin-clavulanate (all guided by culture)
- **IV options:** Flucloxacillin, Ceftriaxone, Ertapenem, Vancomycin (MRSA), Piperacillin-tazobactam (broad spectrum)
- **Surgical debridement** is often necessary for chronic osteomyelitis or when hardware present
- **Probe-to-bone test:** Positive test in diabetic foot ulcer has high positive predictive value for osteomyelitis

### 10.6 Red Flags Across All Sections
- **Sepsis/Septic shock:** Altered mental status, hypotension (SBP <90), tachycardia, poor perfusion
- **Immunocompromised host with infection:** Unusual pathogens, atypical presentations
- **Travel-related fever:** Malaria, typhoid, dengue in returned travelers - require urgent evaluation
- **Necrotizing infection:** Rapid progression, severe pain, skin changes
- **Meningitis signs:** Altered mental status, photophobia, neck stiffness, petechial rash

## 11. Antimicrobial Stewardship

### 11.1 Core Principles

#### Appropriate Selection
- Obtain cultures before starting antibiotics when clinically feasible (do NOT delay antibiotics in critically ill patients for cultures)
- Use local antibiogram data to guide empiric therapy
- Follow IDSA guidelines for specific infection syndromes
- Choose narrowest appropriate spectrum based on suspected pathogens
- Consider patient allergies, renal/hepatic function, pregnancy, drug interactions

#### De-escalation
- Review culture and susceptibility results within 24-48 hours
- Narrow antibiotic spectrum based on identified pathogen and sensitivities
- Discontinue empiric coverage for resistant organisms if not identified
- CAP guidelines specifically recommend de-escalation of MRSA and Pseudomonas coverage if cultures negative and rapid PCR negative [^25^]

#### Appropriate Duration
- Follow evidence-based duration recommendations for specific infections
- Shorter durations are often as effective and reduce resistance/adverse events:
  - Cystitis: 3-5 days (nitrofurantoin 5 days, TMP-SMX 3 days, fosfomycin single dose)
  - Pyelonephritis: 5-7 days (fluoroquinolones), 7 days (beta-lactams)
  - CAP: minimum 5 days; stop when afebrile 48-72 hours and clinically stable
  - Cellulitis: 5-7 days
  - Intra-abdominal: 4 days (adequate source control)

#### IV-to-Oral Conversion
- Switch to oral when: clinically improving, able to tolerate oral intake, no GI malabsorption, bioavailable oral option available
- Many agents have excellent oral bioavailability: fluoroquinolones, TMP-SMX, metronidazole, linezolid, doxycycline, clindamycin

### 11.2 Antibiotic Resistance Patterns (Current Trends)

#### Extended-Spectrum Beta-Lactamase (ESBL)-Producing Enterobacteriaceae
- Increasing prevalence globally
- Community-acquired UTIs with ESBL E. coli more common
- Treatment: Carbapenems (ertapenem, meropenem) preferred for severe infections; oral options limited (fosfomycin, nitrofurantoin may retain activity for cystitis)

#### Carbapenem-Resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE)
- Major global health threat
- Treatment: Colistin, polymyxin B, tigecycline, ceftazidime-avibactam, meropenem-vaborbactam, imipenem-relebactam, cefiderocol
- Consult infectious disease specialists

#### MRSA
- Community-acquired MRSA (CA-MRSA): SCCmec type IV, PVL-positive
- Hospital-acquired MRSA: SCCmec type II, multidrug-resistant
- Oral treatment options: TMP-SMX, doxycycline/minocycline, clindamycin (check D-test for inducible resistance), linezolid
- IV treatment: Vancomycin (monitor trough levels), daptomycin (not for pneumonia), linezolid, ceftaroline, telavancin, dalbavancin, oritavancin

#### Fluoroquinolone Resistance
- High rates in E. coli (>20% in many regions), limiting TMP-SMX and fluoroquinolone utility for empiric UTI therapy
- Fluoroquinolone-associated adverse effects: tendinopathy, QT prolongation, aortic aneurysm risk, *C. difficile*, neuropathy; FDA black box warnings
- Reserve fluoroquinolones for situations where no alternatives exist

### 11.3 Biomarker-Guided Therapy
- **Procalcitonin:** NOT recommended to decide whether to initiate antibiotics in CAP [^25^]; may be useful in guiding discontinuation in respiratory infections and sepsis
- **C-reactive protein (CRP):** Can support clinical judgment but should not replace it

### 11.4 Documentation and Communication
- Document indication, expected duration, and plan for de-escalation at time of initiation
- Antibiotic time-outs at 48-72 hours
- Communicate allergy histories clearly (distinguish true allergy from intolerance)

#### Key Vaccine Notes

##### Pneumococcal (Adults >=65 Years)
- **Option 1:** Single dose PCV20 or PCV21
- **Option 2:** PCV15 first, then PPSV23 at least 1 year later
- **If previously received PPSV23:** PCV20 or PCV21 at least 1 year after PPSV23

##### Zoster (Shingrix) - Recombinant
- 2 doses (0.5 mL each) given 2-6 months apart
- Recommended for all adults >=50 years and immunocompromised adults >=19 years
- Even if history of shingles or prior Zostavax vaccination
- Efficacy: >90% prevention of shingles and post-herpetic neuralgia

##### Hepatitis B (Universal Recommendation)
- All adults aged 19-59 years should receive HepB vaccine
- Adults >=60: risk-based or upon patient request
- Heplisav-B (2 doses at 0, 1 month) or PreHevbrio (3 doses at 0, 1, 6 months)

##### RSV (New)
- Adults >=75 years: 1 dose
- Adults 60-74 years at increased risk (chronic cardiopulmonary disease, immunocompromise, diabetes, nursing home residence): shared clinical decision
- Pregnancy: seasonal administration during weeks 32-36 gestation (Abrysvo)

##### COVID-19 (2024-2025 Vaccines) [^294^][^298^]
- **Moderna (Spikevax):** mRNA vaccine; single dose for most adults (or 2-dose series if unvaccinated)
- **Pfizer-BioNTech (Comirnaty):** mRNA vaccine; similar schedule
- **Novavax:** Protein subunit vaccine; 2-dose primary series, then boosters
- Adults >=65: 2 doses of 2024-25 vaccine, 6 months apart (minimum 2 months)
- Immunocompromised: additional doses may be given based on shared clinical decision-making

### 12.2 Catch-Up Schedules

#### Tdap/Td
- If never received Tdap: 1 dose Tdap, then Td or Tdap booster every 10 years
- Tetanus wound management: Tdap/Td if >5 years since last dose for dirty wounds; >10 years for clean wounds

#### HPV
- If series started before age 15: 2 doses (0, 6-12 months)
- If series started at age 15 or older: 3 doses (0, 1-2 months, 6 months)
- Immunocompromised: always 3 doses

#### MMR
- Adults born >=1957 without evidence of immunity: 1-2 doses
- Healthcare workers: 2 doses
- Students in post-secondary education: 2 doses

#### Varicella
- Adults without evidence of immunity: 2 doses (4-8 weeks apart)
- Pregnant women without immunity: vaccinate postpartum

### 12.3 Special Populations

#### Pregnancy
- **Tdap:** During every pregnancy, 27-36 weeks gestation
- **Influenza:** Inactivated vaccine any trimester
- **RSV:** Abrysvo during weeks 32-36 gestation (seasonal)
- **Hepatitis B:** If at risk or HBsAg-negative with unknown status
- **Avoid:** Live vaccines (MMR, Varicella, LAIV, Yellow fever unless required for travel)

#### Immunocompromised
- Avoid live vaccines
- May need additional vaccine doses (e.g., HepB 4 doses, HPV 3 doses)
- Inactivated influenza recommended over live
- Pneumococcal and meningococcal vaccines especially important

#### Healthcare Workers
- Hepatitis B (document immunity)
- MMR (2 doses or immunity)
- Varicella (2 doses or immunity)
- Tdap
- Annual influenza
- COVID-19

#### Asplenia / Persistent Complement Deficiency
- MenACWY: 2-dose primary series, then boosters every 5 years
- MenB: 2-3 dose primary series, then boosters
- Pneumococcal: all recommended doses
- Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib) if not previously vaccinated

# Chapter 1: Headache Disorders

## 1.1 Overview

Headache is among the most common presenting complaints in clinical practice, affecting approximately 50% of the global adult population annually. The International Classification of Headache Disorders, 3rd Edition (ICHD-3), categorizes headaches into primary headache disorders (tension-type, migraine, cluster, and other trigeminal autonomic cephalalgias) and secondary headache disorders (attributable to underlying pathology such as trauma, vascular disorders, infection, or neoplasm) [^1^].

**Epidemiology:**
- Tension-type headache: Lifetime prevalence ~70-80% (most common)
- Migraine: ~12% globally; 3:1 female-to-male ratio
- Cluster headache: ~0.1%; predominantly affects men (4:1 ratio)
- Medication overuse headache: Affects ~1-2% of the population

## 1.2 Clinical Approach and Red Flags

### 1.2.1 The SNOOP4 Mnemonic for Secondary Headache

| Letter | Feature | Possible Etiology | Action Required |
|--------|---------|-------------------|-----------------|
| **S** | **Systemic symptoms** (fever, weight loss, fatigue) or secondary risk factors (HIV, malignancy, immunosuppression) | Meningitis, encephalitis, temporal arteritis, metastatic disease, opportunistic infection | **Urgent evaluation** |
| **N** | **Neurologic signs or symptoms** (confusion, seizures, focal deficits, altered consciousness) | Mass lesion, stroke, subdural hematoma, CNS infection | **Emergency imaging** |
| **O** | **Onset sudden** ("thunderclap," reaching maximum intensity within seconds to a minute) | Subarachnoid hemorrhage, arterial dissection, venous sinus thrombosis, RCVS | **Emergency evaluation** |
| **O** | **Older age** (new onset after age 50) | Temporal (giant cell) arteritis, mass lesion, subdural hematoma | **Prompt evaluation with ESR/CRP and imaging** |
| **P** | **Prior history change** (progressive pattern, change in frequency/severity/character) | Subdural hematoma, mass lesion, medication overuse | **Evaluation with imaging** |
| **P** | **Posture dependent** (worse with Valsalva, standing, or lying) | Chiari malformation, mass lesion, CSF leak, colloid cyst | **Imaging with contrast** |
| **P** | **Precipitated by exertion** or sexual activity | Subarachnoid hemorrhage, RCVS, arterial dissection | **Emergency evaluation** |
| **P** | **Pregnancy or postpartum** | Preeclampsia/eclampsia, cerebral venous thrombosis, PRES, pituitary apoplexy | **Urgent obstetric and neurologic evaluation** |

### 1.2.2 When to Seek Emergency Care

**Call emergency services immediately for:**
- "Worst headache of my life" (thunderclap onset)
- New headache with fever and neck stiffness
- New headache with confusion, seizure, or focal weakness/numbness
- New headache after head trauma (especially if on anticoagulants)
- New headache in pregnancy (especially with visual changes, edema, or hypertension)
- New headache in cancer or immunosuppressed patients
- Headache with progressive visual loss or papilledema

### 1.2.3 Indications for Neuroimaging

Imaging (MRI preferred; CT if emergent) is indicated for:
- New headache with abnormal neurologic examination
- Onset after age 50 with new headache
- Sudden onset (thunderclap) headache
- Headache with fever or meningeal signs
- Headache in known cancer or immunosuppression
- Progressive or change in established headache pattern
- Post-traumatic headache with worsening
- Headache triggered by cough, exertion, or sexual activity

## 1.3 Tension-Type Headache (TTH)

### 1.3.1 Definition and Clinical Features

TTH is characterized by bilateral, pressing or tightening (non-pulsating) pain of mild-to-moderate intensity, not aggravated by routine physical activity. Nausea and vomiting are absent; photophobia or phonophobia may be present but not both [^1^].

**Subtypes:**
- Infrequent episodic TTH: <1 day/month
- Frequent episodic TTH: 1-14 days/month
- Chronic TTH: ≥15 days/month for >3 months

### 1.3.2 Management

**Acute Treatment:**
- Simple analgesics: Acetaminophen 500-1000 mg, ibuprofen 200-400 mg, or naproxen 250-500 mg at headache onset
- Avoid combination analgesics and opioids (medication overuse risk)
- Limit acute medications to <15 days/month

**Preventive Therapy** (indicated for chronic TTH or frequent episodic TTH with significant disability):

| Medication | Dosing | Evidence Level | Key Considerations |
|------------|--------|---------------|-------------------|
| Amitriptyline | 10-75 mg at bedtime | A | Most evidence; anticholinergic side effects; start low |
| Venlafaxine XR | 75-150 mg daily | B | Particularly effective for chronic TTH |
| Mirtazapine | 15-30 mg at bedtime | C | May help if comorbid insomnia/depression |
| Acupuncture | Per protocol | B | Multiple sessions required |

**Non-Pharmacologic:**
- Regular sleep schedule (7-9 hours/night)
- Regular aerobic exercise (30 minutes, 3-5x/week)
- Stress management (mindfulness, relaxation techniques)
- Hydration and regular meals
- Physical therapy for cervical muscle tension
- Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) for chronic TTH

## 1.4 Migraine

### 1.4.1 Definition and Classification

Migraine is a recurrent headache disorder with attacks lasting 4-72 hours when untreated. Characteristic features include unilateral location, pulsating quality, moderate-to-severe pain intensity, aggravation by routine physical activity, and association with nausea/vomiting and/or photophobia and phonophobia [^1^][^2^].

**Migraine with Aura:** Recurrent, fully reversible visual, sensory, or other CNS symptoms developing gradually over ≥5 minutes, each symptom lasting 5-60 minutes. Visual aura is most common (scintillating scotoma, fortification spectra).

**Chronic Migraine:** ≥15 headache days/month for >3 months, with ≥8 days meeting migraine criteria.

### 1.4.2 Acute Treatment (Stepwise Approach)

| Step | Severity | First-Line | Adjuncts |
|------|----------|-----------|----------|
| 1 | Mild-to-moderate | NSAIDs (ibuprofen 400-800 mg, naproxen 500 mg), acetaminophen (1000 mg), or aspirin (900-1000 mg) | Metoclopramide 10 mg for nausea |
| 2 | Moderate-to-severe | **Triptans:** Sumatriptan 50-100 mg PO or 6 mg SC; zolmitriptan 2.5-5 mg; rizatriptan 10 mg; eletriptan 40 mg [^2^] | Antiemetic (metoclopramide 10 mg or prochlorperazine 10 mg) |
| 3 | Severe/refractory | SC sumatriptan 6 mg; DHE IV/IM; anti-DHF nasal spray | IV fluids if dehydrated |

**Triptan Selection Guide:**

| Triptan | Formulation | Onset | Half-life | Notes |
|---------|------------|-------|-----------|-------|
| Sumatriptan | PO 50-100 mg, SC 6 mg, NS 20 mg | SC fastest (10 min) | 2-2.5 hours | Generic available; SC most effective |
| Zolmitriptan | PO 2.5-5 mg, ODT 2.5-5 mg, NS 5 mg | 30-45 min | 3 hours | ODT and NS options; good for nausea |
| Rizatriptan | PO 10 mg, ODT 10 mg | 30 min | 2-2.5 hours | Contraindicated with propranolol |
| Eletriptan | PO 40-80 mg | 30 min | 4-5 hours | Longer duration; less recurrence |
| Almotriptan | PO 12.5 mg | 30-60 min | 3-4 hours | Favorable tolerability profile |

**CGRP-Targeted Acute Therapies:**

| Agent | Class | Dose | Key Advantages | Key Considerations |
|-------|-------|------|---------------|-------------------|
| Rimegepant | Gepant (CGRP receptor antagonist) | 75 mg ODT | No vasoconstriction; safe in CVD; can be used for prevention | Only one dose per 24 hours |
| Ubrogepant | Gepant | 50-100 mg | No cardiovascular contraindications | Hepatic impairment dosing adjustment |
| Zavegepant | Gepant (intranasal) | 10 mg intranasal | Rapid absorption; no GI issues | Intranasal administration |
| Lasmiditan | Ditan (5-HT1F agonist) | 50-200 mg | No vasoconstriction; no BP effects | **Cannot drive for 8 hours after dose** |

> **Key Point:** Gepants and ditans represent significant advances for patients with cardiovascular disease in whom triptans are contraindicated. They do NOT cause vasoconstriction [^3^].

**Triptan Contraindications:** Ischemic heart disease, coronary vasospasm (Prinzmetal angina), prior MI, uncontrolled hypertension (>140/90 on therapy), hemiplegic migraine, basilar-type migraine, cerebrovascular disease, peripheral vascular disease, severe hepatic or renal impairment, pregnancy (relative), concurrent ergotamine use.

### 1.4.3 Migraine Preventive Therapy

**Indications:** ≥4 migraine days/month, disabling attacks, medication overuse, contraindication to acute therapy, patient preference [^2^].

| Class | Medication | Dosing | Evidence | Key Considerations |
|-------|-----------|--------|----------|-------------------|
| **CGRP mAbs** | Erenumab | 70-140 mg SC monthly | A | First mAb approved; well tolerated |
| | Fremanezumab | 225 mg SC monthly OR 675 mg q3mo | A | Quarterly dosing option |
| | Galcanezumab | 120 mg SC monthly | A | Also approved for episodic cluster |
| | Eptinezumab | 100-300 mg IV q3mo | A | IV infusion; every 3 months |
| **Oral Gepants** | Atogepant | 10-60 mg daily | A | Convenient daily oral |
| | Rimegepant | 75 mg every other day | A | Dual acute/preventive |
| **Beta-blockers** | Propranolol | 80-240 mg/day | A | Avoid in asthma, bradycardia, PVD |
| | Metoprolol | 100-200 mg/day | A | More cardioselective |
| | Timolol | 20-40 mg/day | A | Non-selective |
| **Anticonvulsants** | Topiramate | 25-100 mg/day | A | Weight loss side effect; teratogenic; cognitive slowing |
| | Valproate | 500-1500 mg/day | A | **Contraindicated in pregnancy**; weight gain; tremor |
| **OnabotulinumtoxinA** | Botox | 155-195 units IM q12 weeks | A | Chronic migraine only (≥15 days/month); insurance criteria apply |
| **TCAs** | Amitriptyline | 10-50 mg qHS | B | Sedating; helpful with comorbid insomnia |
| **SNRIs** | Venlafaxine XR | 75-150 mg/day | B | Helpful with comorbid depression/anxiety |
| **ARBs** | Candesartan | 4-16 mg daily | B | Good tolerability; check BP |

**Preventive Therapy Selection by Comorbidity:**

| Comorbidity | Preferred Agent | Avoid |
|-------------|----------------|-------|
| Obesity/overweight | Topiramate, bupropion | Valproate, amitriptyline, mirtazapine |
| Underweight/low BMI | Valproate, amitriptyline | Topiramate |
| Depression/anxiety | Venlafaxine, amitriptyline | Beta-blockers |
| Hypertension | Beta-blockers, candesartan | None specific |
| Insomnia | Amitriptyline, propranolol (evening) | Beta-blockers (morning dosing) |
| Pregnancy (planning) | Beta-blockers (propranolol, metoprolol) | Valproate, topiramate, CGRP mAbs (limited data) |
| Cardiovascular disease | CGRP mAbs, gepants, topiramate | Triptans (for acute); beta-blockers with caution |
| Renal impairment | Caution with topiramate | Dose-adjust renally cleared drugs |

### 1.4.4 Medication Overuse Headache

**Definition:** Headache occurring ≥15 days/month in a patient with pre-existing headache disorder, associated with regular overuse of acute headache medication for >3 months [^1^].

**Thresholds for Overuse:**

| Medication Class | Overuse Threshold |
|-----------------|-------------------|
| Triptans | ≥10 days/month |
| Ergot alkaloids | ≥10 days/month |
| Combination analgesics | ≥10 days/month |
| Opioids | ≥10 days/month |
| Simple analgesics (acetaminophen, single NSAID) | ≥15 days/month |

**Management:**
- Patient education about overuse mechanism
- Gradual withdrawal of overused medication (abrupt for simple analgesics; tapered for opioids, barbiturates)
- Bridge therapy (prednisone 60 mg x 2 days, tapering) during withdrawal
- Initiate or optimize preventive therapy BEFORE or DURING withdrawal
- Opioids should NEVER be used for headache
- Set clear limits: acute medications limited to 2-3 days/week maximum

## 1.5 Cluster Headache

### 1.5.1 Definition and Clinical Features

Cluster headache is the most painful primary headache disorder. It presents as severe unilateral orbital, supraorbital, or temporal pain lasting 15-180 minutes, occurring up to 8 times daily, with ipsilateral cranial autonomic features [^4^][^5^].

**Key Features:**
- Excruciating intensity (described as "suicidal" or "ice pick in the eye")
- Restlessness during attacks (patients pace, rock, bang head) -- distinguishes from migraine where patients prefer to lie still
- Ipsilateral autonomic symptoms: Lacrimation, conjunctival injection, nasal congestion/rhinorrhea, eyelid edema, forehead/facial sweating, miosis, ptosis
- Clock-like regularity (same time daily)
- Triggers: Alcohol, nitroglycerin, strong odors

**Subtypes:** Episodic (clusters lasting 7 days to 1 year, separated by pain-free periods ≥1 month) vs. chronic (no remission or remissions <1 month for >1 year).

### 1.5.2 Acute (Abortive) Treatment

| Treatment | Dosing | Evidence | Notes |
|-----------|--------|----------|-------|
| **100% Oxygen** | 12-15 L/min via non-rebreather mask for 15-20 minutes | A | First-line; most effective and safest; aborts ~70% of attacks |
| **Sumatriptan SC** | 6 mg SC; may repeat once after 1 hour | A | Most effective pharmacologic abortive |
| **Zolmitriptan NS** | 5-10 mg intranasal | B | Alternative if no IV access |
| **DHE** | 1 mg IV/IM | B | Effective alternative |

### 1.5.3 Preventive Therapy

| Medication | Dosing | Evidence | Key Monitoring |
|------------|--------|----------|---------------|
| **Verapamil** | Start 80 mg TID; titrate to 240-480 mg/day | A (first-line) | **ECG before each dose increase**; monitor for bradycardia, heart block |
| **Lithium** | 300-1200 mg/day; target 0.4-1.2 mEq/L | B | Renal function, thyroid (TSH), lithium levels |
| **Topiramate** | 25 mg daily; titrate to 100-400 mg/day | B | Cognitive effects, kidney stones, weight loss |
| **Galcanezumab** | 300 mg/month SC | A | FDA-approved for episodic cluster |
| **Greater occipital nerve block** | Bupivacaine + methylprednisolone | B | Transitional/bridge therapy |

**Transitional (Bridge) Therapy:**
- Prednisone 50-80 mg/day tapered over 10-12 days
- Suboccipital corticosteroid injection
- Purpose: Provide relief while preventive therapy takes effect (often 1-2 weeks)

> **CRITICAL SAFETY ALERT:** Cluster headache has one of the highest associations with suicidal ideation of any medical condition. Screen ALL patients for depression and suicidal thoughts. Immediate psychiatric evaluation if suicidal ideation is present [^4^].

## 1.6 Thunderclap Headache and Subarachnoid Hemorrhage

### 1.6.1 Definition

Thunderclap headache reaches maximum intensity within seconds to a minute. Primary thunderclap headache is a diagnosis of exclusion [^6^].

### 1.6.2 Differential Diagnosis

| Condition | Clues | Workup |
|-----------|-------|--------|
| **Subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH)** | "Worst headache of life," meningismus, decreased consciousness | Non-contrast CT; LP if CT negative |
| **Cervical artery dissection** | Neck pain, Horner syndrome, recent neck trauma/manipulation | CTA head/neck |
| **Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis** | Papilledema, seizures, progressive headache, risk factors (pregnancy, OCPs, thrombophilia) | CTV/MRV with contrast |
| **RCVS** | Recurrent thunderclap headaches over days-weeks; often postpartum | CTA/MRA (segmental vasoconstriction) |
| **Hypertensive emergency** | BP >180/120, end-organ symptoms | BP management, CT to rule out PRES |
| **Acute angle-closure glaucoma** | Eye pain, blurred vision, halos, nausea, fixed mid-dilated pupil | Tonometry, ophthalmology emergency |
| **Meningoencephalitis** | Fever, altered mental status, meningeal signs | LP, empiric antibiotics |
| **Spontaneous intracranial hypotension** | Orthostatic headache (worse upright, better supine), recent LP or procedure | MRI with contrast (pachymeningeal enhancement) |

### 1.6.3 SAH Workup

- **Immediate non-contrast CT head:** Sensitivity >95% within 6 hours of symptom onset [^6^]
- **If CT negative and <6 hours from onset:** CT angiography or lumbar puncture (assess for xanthochromia)
- **If CT negative and >6 hours from onset:** Lumbar puncture is mandatory to assess for xanthochromia (bilirubin from RBC breakdown)
- **CT angiography:** To identify aneurysm source if SAH confirmed

# Chapter 2: Seizures and Epilepsy

## 2.1 Overview

Epilepsy is defined as a disorder of the brain characterized by an enduring predisposition to generate epileptic seizures and by the neurobiologic, cognitive, psychological, and social consequences of this condition [^7^]. A diagnosis of epilepsy requires either: (1) at least two unprovoked seizures occurring >24 hours apart, (2) one unprovoked seizure with a high probability of recurrence (>60% over 10 years), or (3) diagnosis of an epilepsy syndrome.

**Epidemiology:**
- Lifetime prevalence: ~3% (1 in 26 people will develop epilepsy)
- Incidence bimodal: peaks in infancy/early childhood and after age 65
- ~65-70% achieve seizure freedom with AED monotherapy

### 2.1.1 ILAE 2017 Classification of Seizures

| Category | Type | Characteristics |
|----------|------|-----------------|
| **Focal onset** | Aware | Consciousness preserved throughout |
| | Impaired awareness | Altered consciousness during seizure |
| | Motor onset | Tonic, clonic, atonic, myoclonic, hyperkinetic, automatisms |
| | Non-motor onset | Sensory, emotional, autonomic, behavior arrest |
| **Generalized onset** | Motor | Tonic-clonic, tonic, clonic, myoclonic, myoclonic-tonic-clonic, atonic (drop attacks) |
| | Non-motor (absence) | Typical (brief staring, 3 Hz spike-wave), atypical |
| **Unknown onset** | | Motor (spasms, tonic-clonic) or non-motor (behavior arrest) |

## 2.2 First Seizure Workup

### 2.2.1 History

Characterize the event (obtain detailed witness description):
- Circumstances (sleep deprivation, alcohol, illness, medication changes)
- Pre-ictal symptoms (aura), ictal features (type of movements, eye deviation, incontinence, tongue biting -- lateral tongue biting favors seizure over syncope)
- Post-ictal features (confusion duration, Todd's paralysis, headache)
- Prior unrecognized possible seizures, family history, febrile seizures
- Alcohol/drug use, medications (bupropion, tramadol, fluoroquinolones, cephalosporins)

### 2.2.2 Investigations

| Test | Rationale | Notes |
|------|-----------|-------|
| **EEG** | Identify epileptiform activity | Obtain within 24-48 hours of seizure to increase yield; sleep-deprived EEG if initial normal |
| **Brain MRI** (preferred) | Identify structural lesions | Mesial temporal sclerosis, tumors, vascular malformations, cortical dysplasia |
| **CT head** | If MRI unavailable or emergent | Hemorrhage, large mass, acute stroke |
| **Laboratory** | Identify metabolic/toxic causes | CBC, CMP, magnesium, glucose, LFTs, toxicology screen, pregnancy test |
| **Lumbar puncture** | If infection suspected | Fever, meningismus, immunocompromised |
| **ECG** | Rule out arrhythmia | Syncope can mimic seizure; prolonged QT risk |

### 2.2.3 Recurrence Risk

Risk of recurrence after first unprovoked seizure is ~40-50% over 2 years [^7^].

**Risk factors for recurrence:**
- Abnormal EEG (epileptiform discharges)
- Prior brain injury (stroke, trauma, infection)
- Abnormal neuroimaging
- Nocturnal seizure
- Todd's paralysis
- Family history of epilepsy

**Decision to Start AED After First Seizure:**
- Generally recommended if: abnormal EEG, abnormal MRI, high-risk occupation (commercial driver, pilot), strong patient preference
- May defer if: normal EEG, normal MRI, clear provoked seizure (with reversible cause corrected), low patient preference

## 2.3 Antiepileptic Drug (AED) Selection

### 2.3.1 Selection by Seizure Type

| Seizure Type | First-Line Options | Second-Line/Add-on | Agents to Avoid |
|-------------|-------------------|-------------------|-----------------|
| **Focal (partial) seizures** | Lamotrigine, levetiracetam, carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine | Lacosamide, brivaracetam, zonisamide, topiramate, perampanel, valproate | -- |
| **Generalized tonic-clonic** | Valproate, lamotrigine, levetiracetam | Topiramate, zonisamide, perampanel | Carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine (may worsen some generalized seizures) |
| **Absence seizures** | Ethosuximide, valproate, lamotrigine | Zonisamide, clonazepam | Carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, phenytoin, gabapentin, pregabalin |
| **Myoclonic seizures** | Valproate, levetiracetam | Topiramate, zonisamide, clonazepam | Carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, phenytoin |
| **Juvenile myoclonic epilepsy** | Valproate, lamotrigine, levetiracetam | Topiramate, zonisamide | Carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine (often worsen) |

### 2.3.2 Key AED Properties and Monitoring

| Agent | Mechanism | Dosing | Key Side Effects | Special Considerations |
|-------|-----------|--------|-----------------|----------------------|
| **Levetiracetam** | SV2A binding | 500-3000 mg/day (BID) | Behavioral (irritability, aggression), somnolence | Renal dosing; IV available; behavioral SEs common |
| **Lamotrigine** | Sodium channel blockade | 25-200 mg/day (BID) | Rash, Stevens-Johnson syndrome | **Slow titration required** (rash risk in first 8 weeks) |
| **Valproate** | Multiple (Na+, GABA) | 500-1500 mg/day | Weight gain, tremor, hair loss, hepatotoxicity, thrombocytopenia | **Avoid in pregnancy** (neural tube defects); check LFTs, ammonia |
| **Carbamazepine** | Sodium channel blockade | 400-1200 mg/day | Hyponatremia, rash, dizziness, hepatotoxicity | Autoinduction; many drug interactions (CYP3A4); check Na+, CBC, LFTs |
| **Oxcarbazepine** | Sodium channel blockade | 600-2400 mg/day | Hyponatremia (more than CBZ), dizziness, rash | Fewer drug interactions than CBZ; check Na+ |
| **Topiramate** | Multiple (Na+, Ca2+, GABA, AMPA) | 25-400 mg/day | Cognitive slowing, word-finding difficulty, paresthesias, kidney stones, weight loss | Teratogenic; check bicarbonate (metabolic acidosis) |
| **Lacosamide** | Sodium channel slow inactivation | 200-400 mg/day (BID) | Dizziness, diplopia, PR prolongation | Check ECG; IV available |
| **Zonisamide** | Multiple (Na+, Ca2+, carbonic anhydrase) | 100-400 mg/day | Cognitive effects, kidney stones, metabolic acidosis, weight loss | Sulfa allergy; check bicarbonate |
| **Perampanel** | AMPA receptor antagonist | 2-12 mg daily | Behavioral (aggression, hostility, dizziness) | Black box warning for serious psychiatric events |
| **Ethosuximide** | T-type calcium channel | 500-1500 mg/day | GI upset, drowsiness, headache | First-line for absence only |

### 2.3.3 AED Titration Principles

- Start at low dose; titrate gradually to minimize side effects
- Increase every 1-2 weeks based on tolerability
- Aim for minimum effective dose; push to therapeutic range if seizures continue
- Monotherapy preferred over polytherapy
- If first AED fails, switch to second (monotherapy trial); if second fails, consider combination therapy
- Consider drug-drug interactions, hepatic/renal function, pregnancy potential

## 2.4 Status Epilepticus

### 2.4.1 Definition

Status epilepticus (SE) is defined as continuous seizure activity lasting >5 minutes, or recurrent seizures without recovery of consciousness between attacks. Types include convulsive (tonic-clonic) SE (most common and most dangerous), non-convulsive SE, and focal SE with impaired awareness [^8^].

> **EMERGENCY ALERT:** Status epilepticus is a life-threatening neurologic emergency with mortality ~15-20%. Treatment should NOT be delayed for diagnostic testing. Time is brain -- neuronal injury begins within minutes of continuous seizure activity.

### 2.4.2 Management Algorithm

| Phase | Timeframe | Treatment |
|-------|-----------|-----------|
| **Pre-hospital / Initial** | 0-5 min | Position patient on side, protect airway, give oxygen, check blood glucose, establish IV access. If glucose <60 mg/dL: give IV dextrose. |
| **Early SE** | 5-20 min | **Benzodiazepine (do NOT delay):** IV lorazepam 4 mg (repeat x1 in 5-10 min if needed) OR IM midazolam 10 mg (preferred if no IV access) OR buccal/nasal midazolam 10 mg [^8^][^9^] |
| **Established SE** | 20-40 min | **Second-line AED (all equally effective per ESETT trial):** Levetiracetam 60 mg/kg IV (max 4500 mg) over 5-15 min OR fosphenytoin 20 mg PE/kg at 150 mg/min OR valproate 40 mg/kg IV over 10 min [^9^] |
| **Refractory SE** | 40-60 min | **Third-line (anesthesia):** Propofol infusion OR midazolam infusion (0.2 mg/kg bolus, then 0.05-2 mg/kg/hr); continuous EEG monitoring; endotracheal intubation |
| **Super-refractory SE** | >24 hours | Ketamine infusion, barbiturate coma (pentobarbital), immunotherapy (if autoimmune etiology), therapeutic hypothermia, surgical evaluation |

**Key Management Points:**
- Do NOT delay benzodiazepines for any reason -- every minute of ongoing seizure causes additional neuronal injury
- ESETT trial showed levetiracetam, fosphenytoin, and valproate have similar efficacy and safety as second-line agents [^9^]
- Address underlying cause simultaneously: electrolytes (correct Na+, glucose, Mg2+), infection, stroke, trauma, metabolic derangement
- Protect airway; be prepared to intubate if airway compromise or refractory SE requiring anesthesia
- Continuous EEG monitoring for refractory SE to detect non-convulsive seizures

## 2.5 Epilepsy and Pregnancy

### 2.5.1 Preconception Counseling

- Optimize seizure control BEFORE conception (best outcomes with seizure freedom)
- Folic acid supplementation: 0.4-5 mg daily, beginning BEFORE conception (reduces neural tube defect risk)
- Review AED regimen: prefer agents with lowest teratogenic risk
- Discuss that most women with epilepsy have healthy pregnancies and babies

### 2.5.2 AED Selection in Pregnancy

| AED | MCM Risk | Key Concerns | Recommendation |
|-----|----------|-------------|----------------|
| **Levetiracetam** | ~2-2.5% | Lowest risk; compatible with breastfeeding | **Preferred first-line** |
| **Lamotrigine** | ~2-3% (dose-dependent; >200 mg/day higher risk) | Levels decrease significantly in pregnancy; monitor levels monthly | **Preferred**; requires level monitoring |
| **Oxcarbazepine** | ~2-3% | Less data than lamotrigine; hyponatremia | Reasonable option |
| **Carbamazepine** | ~2-3% | Neural tube defects; vitamin K supplementation needed | Acceptable if already controlled |
| **Topiramate** | ~4-5% | Low birth weight, oral clefts, neurodevelopmental concerns | **Avoid if possible** |
| **Valproate** | ~10% | Neural tube defects, neurodevelopmental impairment, autism risk | **CONTRAINDICATED in pregnancy** -- avoid in women of childbearing potential unless no alternative |
| **Phenobarbital** | ~6-8% | Neurodevelopmental impairment | **Avoid** |

**General Principles:**
- Monotherapy preferred over polytherapy
- Use lowest effective dose of the safest effective AED
- **Do NOT discontinue AED abruptly** (risk of breakthrough seizures, including status epilepticus, which is harmful to both mother and fetus)
- Vitamin K 10 mg orally daily in the last month of pregnancy for women on enzyme-inducing AEDs (carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbital, primidone)
- Newborn receives vitamin K 1 mg IM at birth
- Most AEDs are compatible with breastfeeding; encourage breastfeeding with monitoring for infant sedation

# Chapter 3: Stroke

## 3.1 Overview

Stroke is a clinical syndrome characterized by rapidly developing signs of focal (or global) disturbance of cerebral function lasting >24 hours or leading to death, with no apparent cause other than vascular origin. It is the second leading cause of death worldwide and a major cause of disability [^11^].

**Classification:**
- Ischemic stroke: ~85% (thrombotic, embolic, lacunar)
- Hemorrhagic stroke: ~15% (intracerebral hemorrhage ~10%, subarachnoid hemorrhage ~5%)

### 3.1.1 Rapid Recognition: BE-FAST

| Letter | Sign | Description |
|--------|------|-------------|
| **B** | **Balance** | Sudden loss of balance or coordination |
| **E** | **Eyes** | Sudden vision changes (double vision, vision loss) |
| **F** | **Face** | Facial droop (ask patient to smile) |
| **A** | **Arm** | Arm weakness or numbness (ask patient to raise both arms) |
| **S** | **Speech** | Slurred speech, difficulty finding words, inability to speak |
| **T** | **Time** | Time to call emergency services -- note time last known well |

## 3.2 Ischemic Stroke

### 3.2.1 IV Thrombolysis

> **EMERGENCY PROTOCOL:** Every minute of delayed reperfusion costs approximately 2 million neurons. "Time is brain."

| Parameter | Standard Window | Extended Window |
|-----------|----------------|-----------------|
| **Time from LKN** | ≤4.5 hours | 4.5-24 hours (with advanced imaging) |
| **Agent** | Tenecteplase 0.25 mg/kg IV bolus (preferred) OR Alteplase 0.9 mg/kg (10% bolus, 90% over 60 min) | Same agents |
| **Tenecteplase advantages** | Single IV bolus; easier administration; non-inferior/superior outcomes; Class 1 recommendation [^12^] | -- |
| **Eligibility** | Age ≥18, disabling deficit, measurable deficit on NIHSS | Advanced imaging (CTP/MRI DWI-FLAIR mismatch) showing salvageable tissue |

**Absolute Contraindications to IV tPA/Tenecteplase:**
- Active internal bleeding
- Intracranial hemorrhage on CT
- Recent intracranial or intraspinal surgery, head trauma, or stroke within 3 months
- Severe uncontrolled hypertension (SBP >185 or DBP >110 mmHg despite treatment)
- Platelets <100,000/mm3
- INR >1.7 on warfarin or current direct oral anticoagulant use (with exceptions)
- Blood glucose <50 mg/dL
- Large intracranial mass or edema
- Aortic arch dissection

**Extended Window Cautions (>3-4.5 hours):**
- Age >80 years
- NIHSS >25
- Combination of diabetes + prior stroke
- Any anticoagulant use (regardless of INR [^11^]

### 3.2.2 Mechanical Thrombectomy

| Window | Criteria | Evidence Level |
|--------|----------|---------------|
| **0-6 hours** | Large vessel occlusion (LVO) of ICA, M1, or basilar artery; age ≥18; NIHSS ≥6; ASPECTS ≥6; pre-stroke mRS 0-1 | Class 1, Level A |
| **6-24 hours** | DAWN/DEFUSE-3 criteria: Clinical-imaging mismatch (small core, large penumbra) | Class 1, Level A |
| **Large core (ASPECTS 3-5)** | Age <80, NIHSS ≥6, no significant mass effect | Class 1 (SELECT2, ANGEL-ASPECT trials) |
| **Basilar artery occlusion** | NIHSS ≥10, mild ischemic damage on imaging, within 24 hours | Class 1 (ATTENTION, BAOCHE trials) [^12^][^13^] |

**Blood Pressure Targets:**
- Pre-thrombolysis: SBP <185 mmHg, DBP <110 mmHg
- Post-recanalization: Avoid SBP <140 mmHg (class III harm from ENCHANTED2/MT trial)
- Goal for first 24 hours post-treatment: <180/105 mmHg, then individualized

### 3.2.3 Post-Acute Management

**General Care (First 24-72 Hours):**
- Neuro checks every 15 min during tPA infusion, then hourly
- Blood pressure management: Labetalol, nicardipine, or clevidipine as needed
- Temperature management: Treat fever (>38°C) -- each 1°C increases infarct volume by ~20%
- Glycemic control: Target 140-180 mg/dL
- Swallow screening before any oral intake (aspiration risk)
- DVT prophylaxis (LMWH or SCDs) after 24 hours if no hemorrhage
- Early mobilization when safe

## 3.3 Hemorrhagic Stroke

### 3.3.1 Intracerebral Hemorrhage (ICH)

**Initial Management:**
- Urgent non-contrast CT to confirm
- Immediate reversal of anticoagulation:
  - Warfarin: IV vitamin K 10 mg + 4-factor PCC
  - Dabigatran: Idarucizumab 5 g IV
  - Apixaban/rivaroxaban: Andexanet alfa (low dose if last dose <8h AND <10 mg; high dose if last dose <8h AND ≥10 mg) or 4-factor PCC if andexanet unavailable
- Blood pressure management: Target SBP 140-160 mmHg (INTERACT-2, ATACH-2 trials) -- rapid but controlled reduction
- Neurosurgical consultation for: cerebellar hemorrhage >3 cm, supratentorial hematoma with mass effect/herniation, intraventricular hemorrhage with hydrocephalus

### 3.3.2 Subarachnoid Hemorrhage (SAH)

**Management:**
- CT head: >95% sensitive within 6 hours; LP or CTA if CT negative and >6 hours
- CTA to identify aneurysm source
- **Nimodipine 60 mg PO/NG every 4 hours for 21 days** -- reduces risk of vasospasm and delayed cerebral ischemia (DIND)
- Secure aneurysm: Endovascular coiling preferred over surgical clipping for most ruptured aneurysms
- Monitor in ICU for vasospasm (transcranial Doppler, clinical exam)

## 3.4 Transient Ischemic Attack (TIA)

### 3.4.1 Definition

A transient episode of neurologic dysfunction caused by focal brain, spinal cord, or retinal ischemia without acute infarction. By tissue-based definition, symptoms may last <24 hours (most resolve within 1 hour) without evidence of acute infarction on imaging [^11^].

### 3.4.2 ABCD2 Score for Risk Stratification

| Factor | Points |
|--------|--------|
| **A**ge ≥60 years | 1 |
| **B**lood pressure ≥140/90 mmHg (at initial evaluation) | 1 |
| **C**linical features: Unilateral weakness | 2 |
| **C**linical features: Speech disturbance without weakness | 1 |
| **D**uration: ≥60 minutes | 2 |
| **D**uration: 10-59 minutes | 1 |
| **D**iabetes mellitus | 1 |

| Total Score | Risk Category | 2-Day Stroke Risk | Recommended Action |
|-------------|--------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| 0-3 | Low risk | ~1% | Outpatient workup within 48 hours |
| 4-5 | Moderate risk | ~4% | Urgent evaluation; consider admission |
| 6-7 | High risk | ~8% | Hospital admission for urgent workup |

### 3.4.3 TIA Workup

| Test | Purpose |
|------|---------|
| Brain MRI with DWI | Rule out silent acute infarcts (may change management even if clinically resolved) |
| CTA head/neck or MRA | Identify intracranial/extracranial stenosis or occlusion |
| Echocardiography | Evaluate for cardiac source of embolism |
| ECG + continuous cardiac monitoring (24-48 hours) | Detect atrial fibrillation |
| Laboratory | Lipids, HbA1c, CBC, CMP, TSH |

## 3.5 Secondary Prevention of Ischemic Stroke/TIA

| Modality | Recommendation | Evidence |
|----------|---------------|----------|
| **Antiplatelet** | Aspirin 81 mg daily (start within 24-48 hours if no hemorrhage); clopidogrel 75 mg if aspirin allergy | Class 1 |
| **Dual antiplatelet** | Aspirin + clopidogrel for 21 days (CHANCE trial) to 90 days (POINT trial) for minor stroke (NIHSS ≤3) or high-risk TIA, then single agent | Class 1 |
| **Anticoagulation (for AF)** | DOACs preferred over warfarin (apixaban, rivaroxaban, dabigatran, edoxaban) | Class 1 |
| **High-intensity statin** | Atorvastatin 40-80 mg or rosuvastatin 20-40 mg daily regardless of baseline LDL | Class 1 |
| **Blood pressure** | Target <130/80 mmHg (per AHA/ACC) | Class 1 |
| **Diabetes** | Metformin first-line; GLP-1 agonists or SGLT2 inhibitors may have added cerebrovascular benefit | Class 2a |
| **Lifestyle** | Mediterranean diet, exercise 150 min/week, weight loss, smoking cessation, alcohol moderation | Class 1 |
| **Carotid revascularization** | CEA or stenting for symptomatic 50-99% stenosis; perform within 2 weeks for high-grade stenosis | Class 1 |

**Additional Considerations:**
- Patent foramen ovale (PFO) closure: Indicated for age 18-60 with cryptogenic stroke and high-risk PFO features (atrial septal aneurysm, large shunt)
- Left atrial appendage occlusion: Alternative to anticoagulation in AF patients with contraindications to long-term anticoagulation
- Identify and treat uncommon causes: Cervical artery dissection (antiplatelet vs. anticoagulation), vasculitis, hypercoagulable states

# Chapter 4: Dizziness and Vertigo

## 4.1 Overview

Dizziness is a nonspecific term that requires careful characterization. Accurate diagnosis depends on distinguishing between four subtypes [^14^]:

1. **Vertigo:** Illusion of movement (spinning, tilting, swaying) -- suggests vestibular system involvement
2. **Presyncope/Lightheadedness:** Feeling of impending faint -- suggests cardiovascular or metabolic cause
3. **Disequilibrium:** Unsteadiness or imbalance when walking -- suggests neurologic, musculoskeletal, or proprioceptive cause
4. **Nonspecific dizziness:** Vague, poorly described symptoms -- may suggest anxiety, medication effects, or systemic illness

## 4.2 Peripheral vs. Central Vertigo

| Feature | Peripheral (Vestibular) | Central (CNS/Posterior Fossa) |
|---------|------------------------|-------------------------------|
| **Onset** | Sudden, severe | Gradual or sudden |
| **Severity** | Intense vertigo | Mild-to-moderate |
| **Duration** | Episodic (seconds to days) | Continuous |
| **Nystagmus** | Unidirectional, horizontal, fatigable | Bidirectional, gaze-evoked, pure vertical/torsional |
| **Associated symptoms** | Hearing loss, tinnitus, aural fullness | Diplopia, dysarthria, dysphagia, weakness, ataxia, headache |
| **Head impulse test** | **Abnormal** (corrective saccade) | **Normal** |
| **Test of skew** | Absent | **May be present** |
| **Prognosis** | Generally benign (except sudden hearing loss) | Potentially life-threatening |

### 4.2.1 HINTS Exam (Acute Vestibular Syndrome)

For patients with acute onset, continuous vertigo lasting >24 hours with nystagmus and head-motion intolerance (acute vestibular syndrome), the HINTS exam can differentiate peripheral from central causes:

| Component | Peripheral Finding | Central Finding |
|-----------|-------------------|-----------------|
| **H**ead **I**mpulse | Abnormal (catch-up saccade) | Normal (no saccade) |
| **N**ystagmus | Unidirectional, fast phase away from affected ear | Bidirectional or direction-changing |
| **T**est of **S**kew | No vertical misalignment | Vertical skew deviation |

**HINTS has >99% sensitivity and >97% specificity for detecting stroke -- more sensitive than MRI in the first 24-48 hours** [^14^].

### 4.2.2 Posterior Circulation Stroke Red Flags

Vertigo with ANY of the following suggests posterior circulation (cerebellar or brainstem) stroke and requires emergent evaluation:

- Vertical nystagmus
- Skew deviation on alternate cover test
- Severe gait ataxia (unable to sit or stand unassisted)
- New severe headache or neck pain
- Diplopia, dysarthria, dysphagia
- Focal weakness or sensory loss
- Altered mental status
- HINTS exam suggesting central cause

> **EMERGENCY ALERT:** Acute vertigo with any focal neurologic deficit, severe gait ataxia, or headache/neck pain is posterior circulation stroke until proven otherwise. Do NOT attribute to "labyrinthitis" without thorough evaluation.

## 4.3 Benign Paroxysmal Positional Vertigo (BPPV)

### 4.3.1 Overview

BPPV is the most common cause of recurrent vertigo. Brief episodes of spinning vertigo (seconds to 1 minute) are triggered by specific head position changes. It results from otoconia (calcium carbonate crystals) becoming dislodged from the utricle and entering the semicircular canals [^14^][^15^].

**Risk factors:** Age >50, head trauma, viral infection, prolonged bed rest, Meniere's disease, vestibular neuritis, osteoporosis.

### 4.3.2 Diagnosis

**Dix-Hallpike Maneuver** (for posterior canal BPPV):
1. Patient sits on examination table, head rotated 45° toward the side being tested
2. Patient rapidly lies back with head extended 20-30° off the table edge
3. Observe for nystagmus and vertigo
4. **Positive:** Upbeating torsional nystagmus with fast phase toward the affected ear, latency of several seconds, fatigability within 30-60 seconds

**Supine Roll Test** (for horizontal canal BPPV): Patient lies supine, head rapidly rotated 90° to each side; observe for horizontal nystagmus.

### 4.3.3 Treatment

| Canal Affected | Maneuver | Success Rate |
|---------------|----------|-------------|
| **Posterior canal** (most common, ~85%) | **Epley (canalith repositioning)** | 70-90% after single treatment |
| **Horizontal canal** | Barbecue roll (Lempert maneuver) | 75-90% |
| **Anterior canal** (rare) | Reverse Epley or Yacovino | 70-80% |

**Epley Maneuver Steps:**
1. Dix-Hallpike position (head turned 45° toward affected ear, reclined)
2. Hold 30-60 seconds after nystagmus stops
3. Rotate head 90° toward unaffected ear (now 45° past midline)
4. Hold 30-60 seconds
5. Roll patient onto side (nose toward floor)
6. Hold 30-60 seconds
7. Sit patient up, chin tucked
8. Instruct patient to sleep semi-recumbent for 2 nights; avoid provocative positions for 1 week

**Important:** Canalith repositioning is first-line; medications (meclizine, antiemetics) are NOT recommended as primary treatment for BPPV. Meclizine 25-100 mg may be used briefly for severe nausea only [^15^].

**Brandt-Daroff Exercises:** Alternative when affected side is unclear or Epley contraindicated; patient performs at home 3 times daily.

## 4.4 Vestibular Neuritis

### 4.4.1 Definition

Acute onset of sustained, severe vertigo lasting days to weeks due to inflammation of the vestibular nerve (likely viral/post-viral). Distinguished from labyrinthitis by preserved hearing [^14^].

**Clinical Features:**
- Sudden, severe continuous vertigo with nausea/vomiting
- Worse with head movement
- Spontaneous unidirectional horizontal nystagmus (fast phase away from affected ear)
- Positive head impulse test toward affected side
- Normal hearing
- Gradual improvement over days to weeks

### 4.4.2 Management

| Treatment | Details | Evidence |
|-----------|---------|----------|
| **Corticosteroids** | Methylprednisolone 100 mg daily x 5 days, then taper (if within 3 days of onset) | May improve vestibular recovery |
| **Symptomatic** | Betahistine 16 mg TID; antiemetics (ondansetron 4-8 mg, prochlorperazine 10 mg) | Symptom control |
| **Vestibular rehabilitation** | Exercises to promote central compensation | Strongly recommended; begin early |
| **Supportive** | Avoid driving until symptoms resolve; fall precautions | Safety |

## 4.5 Meniere's Disease

### 4.5.1 Definition

Episodic vertigo (20 minutes to 12 hours) accompanied by fluctuating sensorineural hearing loss, tinnitus, and aural fullness. Believed due to endolymphatic hydrops [^14^].

### 4.5.2 Management

**First-Line (Conservative):**
- Dietary sodium restriction (<1.5-2 g/day)
- Avoid caffeine, alcohol, and tobacco
- Manage stress

**Medical Therapy:**
- Betahistine 16-48 mg/day in divided doses (may reduce vertigo frequency)
- Diuretics (hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg/triamterene)

**Refractory Disease:**
- Intratympanic gentamicin (chemical labyrinthectomy -- destroys vestibular function; risk of hearing loss)
- Intratympanic dexamethasone (preserves hearing; less effective than gentamicin)
- Surgery: Endolymphatic sac decompression, vestibular nerve section, labyrinthectomy (for non-hearing ear)

## 4.6 Key Differential Diagnosis Table

| Condition | Duration | Trigger | Hearing Loss | Key Features | Treatment |
|-----------|----------|---------|-------------|-------------|-----------|
| **BPPV** | Seconds | Head position change | No | Brief spinning; positive Dix-Hallpike | Epley maneuver |
| **Vestibular neuritis** | Days-weeks | Head movement | No | Severe continuous vertigo; post-viral | Steroids (early); vestibular rehab |
| **Meniere's disease** | 20 min - 12 hours | Spontaneous | Yes (fluctuating SNHL) | Tinnitus, aural fullness, vertigo | Salt restriction; betahistine |
| **Vestibular migraine** | Hours-days | Variable | No | Headache history; photophobia; motion sensitivity | Migraine prevention |
| **Posterior circulation stroke** | Continuous | None | Rare | Focal neurologic signs; severe ataxia | Emergency stroke protocol |
| **PPPD** (Persistent postural-perceptual dizziness) | Months-years | Variable | No | Chronic non-spinning dizziness; worse upright; visual stimulation | CBT; SSRI/SNRI; vestibular rehab |

# Chapter 5: Peripheral Neuropathy

## 5.1 Overview

Peripheral neuropathy refers to disorders of the peripheral nervous system. Clinical presentation depends on fiber type involvement: large fiber involvement causes numbness, tingling, and imbalance; small fiber involvement causes pain, burning, and autonomic symptoms [^16^].

### 5.1.1 Pattern Recognition

| Pattern | Description | Common Causes |
|---------|-------------|---------------|
| **Symmetric distal polyneuropathy** (stocking-glove) | Length-dependent; starts in feet, progresses proximally | Diabetes, B12 deficiency, alcohol, toxins, drugs |
| **Mononeuropathy** | Single nerve involvement | Entrapment (CTS), trauma, vasculitis |
| **Mononeuritis multiplex** | Multiple non-contiguous nerves | Vasculitis, diabetes, Lyme disease, leprosy |
| **Radiculopathy** | Nerve root distribution | Disc herniation, spinal stenosis, compressive lesion |
| **Axonal vs. demyelinating** | Based on nerve conduction studies | Axonal: DM, toxins, HIV. Demyelinating: GBS, CIDP, hereditary |

## 5.2 Diabetic Peripheral Neuropathy (DPN)

### 5.2.1 Overview

DPN is the most common cause of peripheral neuropathy in developed countries, present in approximately 50% of patients with long-standing diabetes. It is the leading cause of non-traumatic lower limb amputation [^16^].

### 5.2.2 Screening

| Diabetes Type | When to Start | Frequency | Tests |
|--------------|--------------|-----------|-------|
| Type 2 DM | At diagnosis | Annually | 10-g monofilament, 128 Hz tuning fork, pinprick, ankle reflexes |
| Type 1 DM | 5 years after diagnosis | Annually | Same as above |

**Annual Comprehensive Foot Exam:**
- Inspection (ulcers, deformities, calluses, infection)
- 10-g monofilament testing (at least 4 sites per foot)
- Vibration perception (128 Hz tuning fork)
- Pinprick sensation
- Ankle reflexes
- Pulses (dorsalis pedis, posterior tibial)

### 5.2.3 Painful DPN Management

**Stepwise Pharmacologic Treatment:**

| Class | Agent | Dosing | Evidence | Key Considerations |
|-------|-------|--------|----------|-------------------|
| **SNRIs** | Duloxetine | Start 30 mg, titrate to 60-120 mg daily | A; FDA-approved | Start at 30 mg for 1 week; GI side effects |
| **Gabapentinoids** | Pregabalin | 25-600 mg/day (divided BID-TID) | A; FDA-approved | Sedation, dizziness, weight gain; renal dosing |
| | Gabapentin | 300-3600 mg/day (TID) | A | Renal dosing required; slower titration |
| **TCAs** | Amitriptyline | 10-100 mg at bedtime | A (most evidence) | Anticholinergic effects; cardiac risks in elderly |
| | Nortriptyline | 10-100 mg at bedtime | B | Better tolerated than amitriptyline |
| **Topical** | Capsaicin 8% patch | One 60-minute application q3 months | A; FDA-approved | Applied in clinic; burning sensation initially |
| | Lidocaine 5% patch | 1-3 patches x 12 hours/day | B | Localized pain only |
| **Sodium channel blockers** | Carbamazepine | 200-1200 mg/day | B | AAN guideline-supported; drug interactions |

**Important Principles:**
- Start one agent; titrate to maximum tolerated dose before switching
- If partial response, add agent from a different class (combination therapy)
- **Avoid opioids** (including tramadol, tapentadol) -- not recommended due to lack of long-term efficacy data and risk of abuse/complications [^16^]
- Refractory pain: Consider spinal cord stimulation, CBT, physical therapy

**Prevention of DPN Progression:**
- Tight glycemic control (most effective in T1DM)
- Blood pressure control (<130/80 mmHg)
- Statin therapy
- Smoking cessation
- Regular foot care and self-examination education

## 5.3 B12 Deficiency Neuropathy

### 5.3.1 Clinical Features

B12 deficiency affects the dorsal columns (posterior columns) of the spinal cord, causing:
- Paresthesias in hands and feet (glove-stocking distribution)
- Loss of vibration and proprioception (large fiber sensory loss)
- Sensory ataxia (wide-based, unsteady gait, worse with eyes closed -- positive Romberg sign)
- Optic neuropathy (visual loss)
- Cognitive changes ("megaloblastic madness" -- memory loss, irritability, psychosis)
- Macrocytic anemia may be absent in up to 30% of cases

### 5.3.2 Diagnosis

- **Serum B12:** <200 pg/mL (definite deficiency); 200-400 pg/mL with symptoms (possible deficiency)
- **Confirmatory:** Elevated methylmalonic acid (MMA) and homocysteine (more sensitive than B12 level)
- **CBC:** May show macrocytosis (MCV >100 fL) or be normal
- **Causes:** Pernicious anemia (autoimmune), malabsorption (celiac, Crohn's, gastric bypass), dietary deficiency (veganism), medication-induced (metformin, PPIs, H2-blockers)

> **Key Clinical Pearl:** Check B12 in ALL patients presenting with peripheral neuropathy, especially those on metformin or PPIs. Up to 30% have normal MCV.

### 5.3.3 Treatment

- **B12 1000 mcg IM daily x 1 week, then weekly x 1 month, then monthly indefinitely** (for pernicious anemia or malabsorption)
- High-dose oral B12 (1000-2000 mcg/day) may be effective if intrinsic factor present
- Neurologic deficits may improve with treatment but may be irreversible if prolonged
- Treat underlying cause; screen for pernicious anemia (anti-intrinsic factor antibodies)

## 5.4 Guillain-Barre Syndrome (GBS)

### 5.4.1 Overview

GBS is an acute inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy characterized by rapidly progressive, symmetric ascending weakness and areflexia. It is often triggered by infection (Campylobacter jejuni, CMV, EBV, influenza, Zika virus, COVID-19) [^17^][^18^].

**Subtypes:**
- **AIDP** (Acute Inflammatory Demyelinating Polyradiculoneuropathy): Most common in North America/Europe
- **AMAN** (Acute Motor Axonal Neuropathy): Associated with C. jejuni; more common in Asia; axonal variant
- **Miller Fisher Syndrome:** Ophthalmoplegia, ataxia, areflexia; anti-GQ1b antibody; may progress to GBS

### 5.4.2 Diagnostic Criteria

- Progressive weakness of both legs and arms (ascending: legs before arms)
- Areflexia or hyporeflexia (may be normal early)
- Albuminocytologic dissociation in CSF (elevated protein, normal cell count <10)
- Nerve conduction studies showing demyelination (prolonged distal latencies, slowed conduction velocities, temporal dispersion, conduction block)
- Autonomic dysfunction (arrhythmias, labile BP, ileus, urinary retention)

### 5.4.3 Management

| Treatment | Dosing | Notes |
|-----------|--------|-------|
| **IVIG** (preferred) | 2 g/kg total dose divided over 2-5 days | Preferred due to ease of administration and fewer complications |
| **Plasma Exchange** | 4-6 exchanges of 1-1.5 plasma volumes over 7-14 days | Equally effective to IVIG |
| **Corticosteroids** | NOT effective | Do NOT use |

**Supportive Care:**
- Monitor FVC, MIP, MEP at least every 4-6 hours
- ICU admission if FVC <15 mL/kg, rapid progression, or bulbar involvement
- Prevent complications: DVT prophylaxis, pressure injury prevention, pain management (neuropathic pain is common)
- Autonomic monitoring (cardiac telemetry for arrhythmias, BP lability)
- Physical therapy when stable

> **EMERGENCY MONITORING:** GBS can cause respiratory failure. FVC should be monitored regularly; intubate electively if FVC <15 mL/kg or declining rapidly. Approximately 20-30% require mechanical ventilation.

**Prognosis:** Recovery begins 2-4 weeks after nadir; 80% walk independently at 6 months; 5-10% mortality. Residual weakness, fatigue, and neuropathic pain may persist.

## 5.5 Carpal Tunnel Syndrome (CTS)

### 5.5.1 Overview

CTS is the most common entrapment neuropathy, caused by compression of the median nerve at the wrist. Prevalence is ~3-5% in the general population, higher in women and with increasing age.

### 5.5.2 Clinical Features

- Numbness, tingling, and pain in the thumb, index finger, middle finger, and radial half of the ring finger
- Symptoms worse at night (waking patient from sleep); improve with shaking hands
- Thenar weakness and atrophy in severe/long-standing cases
- Worsened by wrist flexion or extension

### 5.5.3 Physical Examination

| Test | Technique | Positive Finding |
|------|-----------|-----------------|
| **Phalen maneuver** | Hold wrist in flexion for 60 seconds | Reproduction of symptoms |
| **Tinel sign** | Tap over carpal tunnel (palmar wrist crease) | Tingling/shock sensation radiating to fingers |
| **Durkan test** | Direct compression over carpal tunnel for 30 seconds | Reproduction of symptoms |
| **Thenar atrophy** | Visual inspection | Wasting of thenar eminence (late finding) |

### 5.5.4 Diagnosis and Management

**Diagnosis:**
- Clinical diagnosis based on symptoms and physical exam
- Nerve conduction studies/EMG to confirm and grade severity
- Ultrasound: Enlarged median nerve cross-sectional area (>10 mm2)

**Management:**

| Severity | Treatment |
|----------|-----------|
| **Mild** | Wrist splinting at night (neutral position), activity modification, NSAIDs |
| **Moderate** | Night splinting + corticosteroid injection into carpal tunnel |
| **Severe** (thenar atrophy, constant numbness) | **Surgical carpal tunnel release** (open or endoscopic) |

**Corticosteroid Injection:** 1 mL triamcinolone (40 mg/mL) or methylprednisolone injected into carpal tunnel (ultrasound-guided improves accuracy). Provides temporary relief; may be repeated.

**Surgical Indications:** Failed conservative therapy, severe disease (thenar atrophy), progressive neurologic deficit on EMG.

# Chapter 6: Cognitive Impairment

## 6.1 Overview

Cognitive impairment represents a spectrum from normal aging through mild cognitive impairment (MCI) to dementia. Distinguishing between these conditions, and between dementia and delirium, is critical for appropriate management.

### 6.1.1 Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) vs. Dementia

| Feature | MCI | Dementia |
|---------|-----|----------|
| Cognitive decline | Objectively measurable in ≥1 domain | Deficits in ≥2 cognitive domains |
| Functional independence | **Preserved** | **Impaired** -- interferes with daily functioning |
| Activities of daily living | Intact | Requires assistance with IADLs and eventually ADLs |
| Insight | Usually preserved | Often impaired |
| Annual progression to dementia | ~10-15% | Already established |

**MCI Subtypes:**
- Amnestic MCI (single-domain or multi-domain): Primarily memory impairment; highest risk for Alzheimer's disease progression
- Non-amnestic MCI: Impairment in language, executive function, or visuospatial skills; may progress to non-AD dementia

### 6.1.2 Delirium vs. Dementia

| Feature | Delirium | Dementia |
|---------|----------|----------|
| **Onset** | Hours to days (acute) | Months to years (insidious) |
| **Course** | Fluctuating -- worse at night (sundowning) | Slowly progressive |
| **Attention** | Markedly impaired (inattentive, easily distracted) | Normal early; impaired late |
| **Consciousness** | Altered (hyperactive or hypoactive) | Clear until end-stage |
| **Orientation** | Disoriented | Preserved early |
| **Hallucinations** | Common (visual) | Uncommon (except Lewy body dementia) |
| **Reversibility** | **Often reversible** | Irreversible (with rare exceptions) |
| **Cause** | Medical illness, medications, metabolic derangement | Neurodegenerative process |

> **DELIRIUM IS A MEDICAL EMERGENCY.** It indicates serious underlying pathology and is associated with increased mortality, prolonged hospitalization, and risk of permanent cognitive decline. Always evaluate for underlying causes [^19^][^20^].

### 6.1.3 Delirium Screening and Management

**Screening Tools:**
- CAM (Confusion Assessment Method): Most widely used
- 4AT: Rapid screening tool (1 minute); does not require specialist training
- bCAM: Brief CAM for emergency department

**Common Causes (I WATCH DEATH Mnemonic):**

| Letter | Cause |
|--------|-------|
| I | Infection (UTI, pneumonia, sepsis) |
| W | Withdrawal (alcohol, benzodiazepines, opioids) |
| A | Acute metabolic (electrolytes, glucose, hepatic, renal) |
| T | Toxins/Drugs (anticholinergics, sedatives, opioids, steroids) |
| C | CNS pathology (stroke, bleed, meningitis, seizure) |
| H | Hypoxia |
| D | Deficiencies (thiamine, B12, folate) |
| E | Endocrinopathies (thyroid, adrenal, glucose) |
| A | Acute vascular (stroke, MI, hypertensive emergency) |
| T | Trauma, Temperature, Tumor |
| H | Heavy metals, other toxins |

**Delirium Management:**
- Treat underlying cause
- Non-pharmacologic first: Reorientation (clock, calendar, family photos), sleep hygiene, early mobilization, vision/hearing aids, minimize restraints, avoid unnecessary lines/tubes
- Medications only if severe agitation endangering patient or others: Low-dose haloperidol (0.5-1 mg PO/IM) or atypical antipsychotics (quetiapine 12.5-50 mg, olanzapine 2.5-5 mg) for short-term use only
- **Avoid benzodiazepines** (except for alcohol/benzodiazepine withdrawal) and anticholinergic medications

## 6.2 Alzheimer's Disease (AD)

### 6.2.1 Overview

Alzheimer's disease is the most common cause of dementia, accounting for 60-70% of cases. It is characterized by amyloid-beta plaques and neurofibrillary tau tangles with insidious onset and progressive memory impairment [^21^].

**Clinical Features by Stage:**

| Stage | Cognitive | Functional | Behavioral |
|-------|-----------|------------|------------|
| **Mild (Early)** | Episodic memory loss, word-finding difficulty, subtle executive dysfunction | IADL impairment (finances, medications, driving) | Depression, anxiety, social withdrawal |
| **Moderate (Middle)** | Progressive memory loss, aphasia, apraxia, agnosia, visuospatial deficits | ADL impairment (bathing, dressing, toileting) needs supervision | Agitation, wandering, sundowning, hallucinations (later) |
| **Severe (Late)** | Minimal recognition, nonverbal, loss of procedural memory | Fully dependent for all ADLs | Severe behavioral disturbances, seizures, primitive reflexes |

**Neurologic Exam:** Normal motor and sensory function until late stages; frontal release signs (grasp, palmomental) in moderate-to-severe disease; gait disorder late.

### 6.2.2 Diagnostic Workup

| Test | Purpose |
|------|---------|
| **Cognitive screening** | MoCA (preferred over MMSE; more sensitive for MCI), SLUMS |
| **Laboratory** | TSH, B12, CBC, CMP, HbA1c, syphilis serology, HIV (if risk factors) -- rule out reversible causes |
| **Brain MRI** (preferred) | Hippocampal/medial temporal atrophy; rule out vascular dementia, NPH, tumor, subdural |
| **Amyloid confirmation** | Amyloid PET scan OR CSF analysis (low Aβ42, elevated p-tau/t-tau) |
| **APOE genotyping** | For treatment decision-making (ARIA risk with anti-amyloid therapies) |
| **Plasma biomarkers** | p-tau217, p-tau181 (emerging; high sensitivity for amyloid pathology) |

### 6.2.3 Disease-Modifying Therapies (Anti-Amyloid Antibodies)

> **IMPORTANT:** These therapies slow decline by approximately 25-35% over 18 months. They do NOT reverse, cure, or stop Alzheimer's disease. They are indicated for early AD (MCI due to AD or mild dementia) with confirmed amyloid pathology [^21^][^22^][^23^].

| Agent | Mechanism | Dosing | Key Risks | Monitoring |
|-------|-----------|--------|-----------|------------|
| **Lecanemab** (Leqembi) | Anti-amyloid monoclonal antibody | 10 mg/kg IV q2 weeks | ARIA-E (edema), ARIA-H (microhemorrhage), infusion reactions | MRI at baseline, before infusions 5, 9, 13, then every 6 months |
| **Donanemab** (Kisunla) | Anti-amyloid mAb (N3pG, targets modified amyloid) | 700 mg → 1400 mg IV q4 weeks | ARIA-E, ARIA-H, infusion reactions | MRI monitoring schedule; can stop when amyloid negative |

**ARIA (Amyloid-Related Imaging Abnormalities):**
- ARIA-E: Vasogenic edema on T2/FLAIR MRI; often asymptomatic; more common in APOE ε4 carriers
- ARIA-H: Microhemorrhage or superficial siderosis on gradient echo/SWI
- Management: Pause infusion if symptomatic ARIA; MRI monitoring; rarely requires steroids or hospitalization
- **APOE ε4 homozygotes at highest risk** -- APOE genotyping recommended before initiation

### 6.2.4 Symptomatic Treatments

**Cholinesterase Inhibitors:**

| Agent | Dosing | Common Side Effects | Notes |
|-------|--------|-------------------|-------|
| Donepezil | 5-10 mg daily (bedtime) | Nausea, diarrhea, bradycardia, insomnia, vivid dreams | Once daily; available generic |
| Rivastigmine | 3-12 mg/day (BID or transdermal patch) | Similar to donepezil; GI upset with oral form | Patch reduces GI side effects |
| Galantamine | 8-24 mg/day (BID or ER daily) | Similar to above | Also modulates nicotinic receptors |

**NMDA Receptor Antagonist:**
- Memantine 5-20 mg/day -- indicated for moderate-to-severe AD; may be combined with cholinesterase inhibitor; well tolerated

### 6.2.5 Behavioral and Psychological Symptoms of Dementia (BPSD)

| Symptom | First-Line Approach | Pharmacologic (if needed) |
|---------|-------------------|--------------------------|
| **Agitation/aggression** | Non-pharmacologic (reassurance, distraction, environmental modification) | Citalopram 10-20 mg; trazodone 25-100 mg; consider antipsychotics only if severe |
| **Depression** | Supportive therapy, activity engagement | Sertraline 25-100 mg; mirtazapine 7.5-15 mg |
| **Anxiety** | Reassurance, calming environment | SSRIs; buspirone |
| **Psychosis** (hallucinations, delusions) | Reassurance, ensure safety | **Quetiapine** 12.5-100 mg (lowest antipsychotic dose possible); **avoid in Lewy body dementia** |
| **Sleep disturbance** | Sleep hygiene, light exposure, daytime activity | Trazodone 25-50 mg; melatonin 3-6 mg |
| **Wandering** | Safety measures, GPS tracking, structured activities | -- |

> **BLACK BOX WARNING:** Antipsychotics in elderly with dementia are associated with increased mortality and cerebrovascular events. Use only for severe symptoms after non-pharmacologic measures fail, at lowest effective dose, for shortest duration necessary.

## 6.3 Vascular Dementia

### 6.3.1 Clinical Features

- **Stepwise decline** (distinct from AD's gradual progression)
- Prominent executive dysfunction (planning, organization, judgment)
- Gait disturbance early ("marche a petits pas" -- small-stepped gait)
- Urinary urgency and incontinence early
- Pseudobulbar palsy (emotional lability -- involuntary crying/laughing)
- History of strokes or TIAs
- Focal neurologic signs on examination

### 6.3.2 Diagnosis

MRI findings: Significant white matter hyperintensities (leukoaraiosis), multiple lacunar infarcts, strategic infarcts (thalamus, angular gyrus), cortical infarcts.

### 6.3.3 Management

Aggressive vascular risk factor modification is the cornerstone:
- Blood pressure control (<130/80 mmHg)
- High-intensity statin therapy
- Diabetes management (HbA1c target individualized)
- Antiplatelet therapy (if non-cardioembolic)
- Anticoagulation (if atrial fibrillation)
- Smoking cessation, exercise, Mediterranean diet
- Cholinesterase inhibitors may provide modest benefit (less robust evidence than in AD)

## 6.4 Lewy Body Dementia (LBD)

### 6.4.1 Clinical Features

LBD is the second most common neurodegenerative dementia after AD. Diagnosis requires dementia plus core clinical features [^21^].

**Core Clinical Features** (must have 2 for probable, 1 for possible):
1. **Fluctuating cognition:** Pronounced variations in attention and alertness; may have episodes of confusion interspersed with relative lucidity
2. **Recurrent visual hallucinations:** Well-formed, detailed (people, animals); often early feature
3. **REM sleep behavior disorder:** May precede dementia by years
4. **Spontaneous Parkinsonism:** Bradykinesia, rigidity, tremor (develops AFTER or concurrently with cognitive decline)

**Suggestive Features:** Severe neuroleptic sensitivity, repeated falls, syncope, autonomic dysfunction (orthostatic hypotension, constipation, urinary incontinence), hyposmia, delusions.

### 6.4.2 Management

> **CRITICAL SAFETY ALERT:** Patients with LBD have severe sensitivity to antipsychotics, which can cause neuroleptic malignant syndrome, irreversible Parkinsonism, sedation, and increased mortality. **Avoid all antipsychotics if possible.**

| Symptom | Management |
|---------|------------|
| **Cognitive decline** | Rivastigmine (most evidence in LBD); donepezil acceptable |
| **Visual hallucinations** (distressing) | Rivastigmine; quetiapine (lowest dose possible) if absolutely necessary |
| **Parkinsonism** | Levodopa (low doses); avoid dopamine antagonists |
| **REM sleep behavior disorder** | Melatonin 3-12 mg; clonazepam 0.25-1 mg (fall risk) |
| **Autonomic dysfunction** | Orthostatic hypotension: compression stockings, midodrine, fludrocortisone |
| **Antipsychotic necessity** | **Pimavanserin** (FDA-approved for PDP hallucinations); quetiapine (lowest dose) |

## 6.5 Frontotemporal Dementia (FTD)

### 6.5.1 Clinical Variants

| Variant | Core Features | Affected Brain Regions |
|---------|-------------|----------------------|
| **Behavioral variant (bvFTD)** | Personality changes, disinhibition, apathy, loss of empathy, compulsive behaviors, hyperorality, executive dysfunction | Frontal and anterior temporal lobes |
| **Nonfluent/agrammatic variant PPA** | Effortful, halting speech; agrammatism; preserved comprehension | Left posterior frontal (Broca's area) |
| **Semantic variant PPA** | Loss of word meaning, impaired single-word comprehension, fluent but empty speech, prosopagnosia | Anterior temporal lobes (bilateral, often left > right) |

### 6.5.2 Management

- **No disease-modifying therapy** currently available
- Supportive care and behavioral management
- SSRIs (sertraline, escitalopram) may help compulsive behaviors and overeating
- **Avoid cholinesterase inhibitors** -- generally not beneficial and may worsen behavior
- Genetic counseling (C9orf72, MAPT, GRN mutations in familial cases)
- Early safety planning (financial, driving, firearm removal)
- Support groups for caregivers

## 6.6 Prevention of Cognitive Decline

| Intervention | Evidence | Recommendation |
|-------------|----------|---------------|
| **Physical exercise** | Strongest evidence | 150 min/week moderate aerobic activity + resistance training |
| **Cognitive engagement** | Moderate | Lifelong learning, social activity, cognitively stimulating activities |
| **Vascular risk control** | Strong | Control BP, diabetes, lipids from midlife |
| **Mediterranean/DASH/MIND diet** | Moderate | Plant-rich, whole grains, fish, olive oil; limit processed foods |
| **Adequate sleep** | Moderate | 7-9 hours/night; treat sleep apnea |
| **Hearing aids** | Moderate | Treat hearing loss (social isolation increases dementia risk) |
| **Limit alcohol** | Moderate | Moderate consumption; avoid heavy drinking |
| **Smoking cessation** | Strong | Quit at any age |
| **Manage depression** | Moderate | Treat depression (may be risk factor and early symptom) |
| **Social engagement** | Moderate | Maintain social connections |

# Chapter 7: Depression

## 7.1 Overview

Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) is a leading cause of disability worldwide. Diagnosis requires ≥5 of the following symptoms for ≥2 consecutive weeks, causing clinically significant distress or impairment, with at least one symptom being depressed mood OR anhedonia:

1. Depressed mood most of the day, nearly every day
2. Anhedonia (markedly diminished interest/pleasure)
3. Significant weight change or appetite disturbance
4. Insomnia or hypersomnia
5. Psychomotor agitation or retardation
6. Fatigue or loss of energy
7. Feelings of worthlessness or excessive guilt
8. Diminished concentration or indecisiveness
9. Recurrent thoughts of death or suicidal ideation

**Prevalence:** Lifetime ~16-20%; 1-year prevalence ~7-8%. Female-to-male ratio ~2:1.

## 7.2 Screening and Assessment

### 7.2.1 PHQ-9 (Patient Health Questionnaire-9)

The PHQ-9 is the most widely validated depression screening tool in primary care.

| Score | Severity | Recommended Action |
|-------|----------|-------------------|
| 0-4 | Minimal | None; may monitor |
| 5-9 | Mild | Watchful waiting; counseling; follow-up in 2-4 weeks |
| 10-14 | Moderate | Initiate treatment (medication +/− psychotherapy) |
| 15-19 | Moderately severe | Active treatment; consider referral |
| 20-27 | Severe | Active treatment; referral strongly recommended |

> **CRITICAL:** Item 9 on the PHQ-9 asks about suicidal ideation. **Any score >0 on this item requires immediate clinical assessment of suicide risk.**

### 7.2.2 Suicide Risk Assessment

**Risk Factors (SAD PERSONS Mnemonic):**

| Letter | Risk Factor |
|--------|-------------|
| S | Sex (male; though females attempt more, males complete more) |
| A | Age (adolescents/young adults, elderly >65) |
| D | Depression (and other mental illness) |
| P | Previous attempt (strongest single predictor) |
| E | Ethanol/substance abuse |
| R | Rational thinking loss (psychosis, command hallucinations) |
| S | Social support lacking |
| O | Organized plan and access to means |
| N | No spouse (divorced, widowed, single) |
| S | Sickness/chronic illness |

**Protective Factors:** Strong therapeutic alliance, family/children, religious/spiritual beliefs, pregnancy, future orientation, responsibility for others, fear of death/disability.

**Assessment of Active Suicidal Ideation:**
- Ideation: Does the patient have thoughts of suicide?
- Intent: Does the patient intend to act on these thoughts?
- Plan: Is there a specific plan?
- Means: Does the patient have access to lethal means?
- Timeline: When does the patient plan to act?
- Protective factors: What prevents the patient from acting?

**Immediate Actions for High Risk:**
1. Do NOT leave the patient alone
2. Reduce access to lethal means (firearms, medications, sharp objects)
3. Consider involuntary hospitalization if imminent risk
4. Contact psychiatric emergency services
5. Document assessment thoroughly

## 7.3 Pharmacologic Treatment

### 7.3.1 First-Line Antidepressants: SSRIs and SNRIs

| Class | Agent | Starting Dose | Therapeutic Range | Half-Life | Key Advantages | Key Disadvantages |
|-------|-------|--------------|-------------------|-----------|---------------|-------------------|
| **SSRIs** | Sertraline | 25-50 mg | 50-200 mg | 26 hr | Favorable profile; less sedation; multiple indications | GI upset initially |
| | Escitalopram | 10 mg | 10-20 mg | 27-32 hr | Most selective SSRI; good tolerability; fewer drug interactions | QTc prolongation at higher doses |
| | Fluoxetine | 20 mg | 20-80 mg | 1-3 days (active metabolite 4-16 days) | Activating; long half-life (good for nonadherence); CYP2D6 inhibitor | Long half-life (drug interactions); activating |
| | Citalopram | 20 mg | 20-40 mg (max 40 mg) | 35 hr | Well tolerated | **Max 40 mg** (QTc risk); sedation |
| | Paroxetine | 20 mg | 20-60 mg | 21 hr | Good for anxiety, panic | Most anticholinergic SSRI; weight gain; sexual dysfunction; difficult withdrawal |
| **SNRIs** | Venlafaxine XR | 37.5-75 mg | 75-375 mg | 5-11 hr | Higher doses affect NE; good for severe depression | Hypertension at higher doses; significant withdrawal syndrome |
| | Duloxetine | 30-60 mg | 60-120 mg | 12 hr | Also approved for neuropathic pain, fibromyalgia, GAD | Hepatic metabolism; avoid in liver disease |
| | Desvenlafaxine | 50 mg | 50-100 mg | 11 hr | Active metabolite of venlafaxine | Less dose flexibility |

### 7.3.2 Treatment Principles

1. **Select based on:** Side effect profile, patient preference, comorbidities, prior response, family history of response, potential drug interactions
2. **Start low, go slow:** Start at half normal dose in elderly and anxious patients (minimize activation)
3. **Titrate:** Every 2-4 weeks based on response and tolerability
4. **Adequate trial:** 4-6 weeks at therapeutic dose before declaring non-response
5. **Continue:** At full therapeutic dose for 6-12 months after remission (first episode); longer for recurrent episodes
6. **Taper gradually:** To avoid discontinuation syndrome (especially paroxetine, venlafaxine); reduce by 25-50% every 1-2 weeks

### 7.3.3 Second-Line and Augmentation Strategies

| Agent | Dosing | Best For | Cautions |
|-------|--------|----------|----------|
| **Bupropion XL** | 150-450 mg daily | Sexual dysfunction on SSRI, fatigue, weight gain, smoking cessation | **Avoid in anxiety disorders, seizure disorders, eating disorders** (lowers seizure threshold); activating |
| **Mirtazapine** | 7.5-45 mg qHS | Insomnia, poor appetite, anxiety, nausea | Weight gain, sedation (less at higher doses) |
| **Vortioxetine** | 5-20 mg daily | Cognitive symptoms of depression, sexual dysfunction | Nausea initially; complex mechanism |
| **Aripiprazole** (augmentation) | 2-15 mg daily | Treatment-resistant depression | Akathisia; metabolic monitoring |
| **Quetiapine XR** (augmentation) | 150-300 mg daily | Treatment-resistant depression, insomnia | Sedation, metabolic effects |
| **Lithium** (augmentation) | 300-1800 mg daily; target 0.6-1.0 mEq/L | Augmentation (strongest evidence); suicide prevention | Renal function, thyroid monitoring |
| **Thyroid hormone (T3)** | 25-50 mcg daily | Augmentation when lithium contraindicated | Check thyroid function; cardiac monitoring |

## 7.4 Treatment-Resistant Depression (TRD)

### 7.4.1 Definition

Failure to achieve remission after ≥2 adequate antidepressant trials (sufficient dose for sufficient duration, typically 6-8 weeks) of different pharmacologic classes [^24^].

### 7.4.2 Stepwise Approach

| Step | Intervention | Details |
|------|-------------|---------|
| 1 | **Optimize current treatment** | Verify adherence, ensure adequate dose and duration, check drug levels if available |
| 2 | **Switch antidepressant** | Different mechanism (SSRI → SNRI, bupropion, mirtazapine) |
| 3 | **Augmentation** | Add lithium, aripiprazole, thyroid hormone, buspirone |
| 4 | **Combination therapy** | Two antidepressants from different classes |
| 5 | **Neuromodulation** | **ECT** (gold standard for severe/psychotic/suicidal depression); **rTMS** (fewer side effects); VNS |
| 6 | **Novel agents** | Esketamine intranasal (Spravato) -- approved for TRD; requires REMS monitoring; ketamine IV (off-label) |
| 7 | **Investigational** | Dextromethorphan-bupropion (Auvelity); psilocybin (investigational) |

**Esketamine (Spravato):**
- Intranasal NMDA receptor antagonist; FDA-approved for TRD
- Administered in certified healthcare settings; requires REMS program
- Dosing: 56 mg or 84 mg intranasal twice weekly for 4 weeks, then weekly, then every 2 weeks
- Side effects: Dissociation, sedation, blood pressure elevation; monitor for 2 hours post-dose

## 7.5 Perinatal Depression

### 7.5.1 Screening

All pregnant and postpartum patients should be screened using EPDS (Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale) or PHQ-9 at least once during pregnancy and again postpartum.

### 7.5.2 Treatment

| Severity | First-Line |
|----------|-----------|
| Mild-to-moderate | Psychotherapy (CBT, IPT) |
| Moderate-to-severe | SSRIs + psychotherapy |
| Severe/psychotic | Hospitalization; ECT (safe in pregnancy) |

**Antidepressant Selection in Pregnancy:**
- **Sertraline:** Preferred (lowest breast milk levels; most pregnancy safety data)
- **Paroxetine:** Reasonable for preconception/early pregnancy (note: avoid in first trimester due to cardiac defect signal in older data)
- **Fluoxetine:** Avoid if breastfeeding (long half-life in infant)
- **Bupropion:** Lower milk levels; may be used if breastfeeding
- **Avoid:** Benzodiazepines (except short-term); avoid abrupt discontinuation of antidepressants

**Risks of Untreated Depression:**
- Preterm birth, low birth weight
- Impaired maternal-infant bonding
- Developmental effects on child
- Postpartum complications
- Suicide risk (leading cause of maternal death in some countries)

## 7.6 Depression in Special Populations

### 7.6.1 Geriatric Depression

- May present with somatic complaints, cognitive complaints ("pseudodementia"), irritability, or apathy rather than sadness
- Start at half normal dose; slower titration
- Preferred agents: Sertraline, escitalopram, mirtazapine
- Avoid: Paroxetine (anticholinergic), TCAs (orthostasis, cardiac effects, cognitive effects)
- Monitor for falls, hyponatremia (SIADH), weight changes
- Rule out medical causes: Hypothyroidism, B12 deficiency, medication effects, undiagnosed medical illness

### 7.6.2 Adolescent Depression

- Fluoxetine is FDA-approved for MDD in ages 8-18 (only SSRI with this indication)
- Black box warning for increased suicidal ideation in patients <25 years
- Close monitoring required, especially in first 4 weeks and after dose changes
- Psychotherapy (CBT, IPT) as first-line for mild cases; combination with medication for moderate-to-severe

# Chapter 8: Anxiety Disorders

## 8.1 Overview

Anxiety disorders are the most common psychiatric disorders, with lifetime prevalence ~25-30%. They include generalized anxiety disorder (GAD), panic disorder, social anxiety disorder, specific phobias, and separation anxiety disorder. This chapter focuses on the most common adult anxiety disorders [^25^][^26^].

**Key Principles Across All Anxiety Disorders:**
- Start SSRIs at half the usual starting dose (minimizes initial activation/jitteriness)
- Therapeutic effect may take 4-8 weeks (longer than for depression)
- Warn patients about initial increase in anxiety for the first 1-2 weeks
- CBT skills are durable and prevent relapse
- Avoid benzodiazepines for long-term management
- Continue treatment for at least 12 months after response before considering taper

## 8.2 Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD)

### 8.2.1 Diagnosis

Excessive anxiety and worry occurring more days than not for ≥6 months, difficult to control, associated with ≥3 of: restlessness, fatigue, difficulty concentrating, irritability, muscle tension, sleep disturbance [^25^].

### 8.2.2 Screening: GAD-7

| Score | Interpretation | Action |
|-------|---------------|--------|
| 0-4 | Minimal anxiety | Monitor |
| 5-9 | Mild anxiety | Watchful waiting; counseling |
| 10-14 | Moderate anxiety | Initiate treatment (CBT or medication) |
| 15-21 | Severe anxiety | Active treatment (medication + CBT) |

### 8.2.3 Treatment

| Severity | First-Line | Notes |
|----------|-----------|-------|
| Mild | CBT, self-help, mindfulness, exercise | Watchful waiting acceptable |
| Moderate | SSRI/SNRI OR CBT | Both equally effective; combination may be superior |
| Severe | SSRI/SNRI + CBT | Combination therapy |

**Medications for GAD:**

| Class | Agent | Dosing | Notes |
|-------|-------|--------|-------|
| **SSRIs** | Sertraline | 25-200 mg daily | Start at 25 mg for 1 week |
| | Escitalopram | 5-20 mg daily | Start at 5 mg for 1 week |
| **SNRIs** | Venlafaxine XR | 75-225 mg daily | Also FDA-approved for GAD |
| | Duloxetine | 30-120 mg daily | Also approved for GAD |
| **Gabapentinoids** | Pregabalin | 75-300 mg BID | Schedule V controlled substance; risk of abuse/dependence |
| **Buspirone** | Buspirone | 15-60 mg/day (BID-TID) | Non-sedating; no abuse potential; slow onset (2-4 weeks) |

**Duration:** Continue for at least 12 months after response; high relapse rate if discontinued early. Taper gradually over weeks to months.

## 8.3 Panic Disorder

### 8.3.1 Diagnosis

Recurrent unexpected panic attacks followed by ≥1 month of persistent concern about additional attacks, worry about implications, or maladaptive behavior change [^25^][^26^].

**Panic Attack Symptoms (≥4 required):**
- Palpitations, pounding heart
- Sweating
- Trembling/shaking
- Shortness of breath
- Choking sensation
- Chest pain/discomfort
- Nausea/abdominal distress
- Dizziness, lightheadedness
- Derealization/depersonalization
- Fear of losing control
- Fear of dying
- Paresthesias
- Chills or hot flashes

### 8.3.2 Treatment

| Modality | Approach | Evidence |
|----------|----------|----------|
| **CBT (panic-focused)** | Psychoeducation, interoceptive exposure, cognitive restructuring, breathing retraining | As effective as medication; more durable; treatment of choice |
| **SSRIs** | Sertraline, paroxetine, fluoxetine, escitalopram | First-line pharmacotherapy |
| **SNRIs** | Venlafaxine XR 75-225 mg | FDA-approved for panic disorder |
| **Combination** | SSRI/SNRI + CBT | Best outcomes for severe panic disorder |
| **Short-term only** | Benzodiazepines for acute crisis | **Avoid long-term use** (dependence, tolerance) |

**Continue treatment:** ≥6-12 months after remission before considering taper.

### 8.3.3 Medical Mimics of Panic Attacks

Before diagnosing panic disorder, rule out: Thyrotoxicosis, pheochromocytoma, cardiac arrhythmia (SVT, WPW), mitral valve prolapse, medication effects (stimulants, caffeine), substance withdrawal, seizure (temporal lobe), hypoglycemia, anemia, COPD.

## 8.4 Social Anxiety Disorder (Social Phobia)

### 8.4.1 Diagnosis

Marked fear or anxiety about social situations involving possible scrutiny by others; fear of acting in a way that will be negatively evaluated; anxiety out of proportion to actual threat; avoidance or endured with intense distress for ≥6 months [^26^].

### 8.4.2 Treatment

| Approach | Details | Evidence |
|----------|---------|----------|
| **CBT (exposure-based)** | Systematic exposure to feared social situations; cognitive restructuring | First-line; as effective as medication; more durable |
| **SSRIs** | Sertraline, paroxetine, fluoxetine, escitalopram | All FDA-approved for SAD |
| **SNRIs** | Venlafaxine XR 75-225 mg | FDA-approved for SAD |
| **Performance anxiety only** | Propranolol 10-40 mg 30-60 min before event | Beta-blocker for situational anxiety only |
| **Gabapentinoids** | Gabapentin, pregabalin | May be helpful for social anxiety |

Continue treatment ≥12 months after response.

## 8.5 Specific Phobias

### 8.5.1 Treatment

**CBT with systematic desensitization and graded exposure is the treatment of choice.** SSRIs are generally NOT effective as monotherapy for specific phobias.

**Exposure Therapy Components:**
1. Psychoeducation about phobic response
2. Construction of fear hierarchy
3. Systematic graded exposure (in vivo > imaginal > VR)
4. Cognitive restructuring of catastrophic beliefs
5. Relaxation techniques as needed

Beta-blockers or benzodiazepines may be used for specific, infrequent situations (e.g., flying) but are not long-term solutions.

## 8.6 Medication Comparison Table for Anxiety Disorders

| Medication | GAD | Panic | Social Anxiety | Notes |
|------------|-----|-------|---------------|-------|
| Sertraline | Yes | Yes | Yes | Start low (25 mg) |
| Escitalopram | Yes | Yes | Yes | Start low (5 mg) |
| Fluoxetine | Yes | Yes | Yes | Long half-life; activating |
| Paroxetine | Yes | Yes | Yes | Most sedation; difficult withdrawal |
| Venlafaxine XR | Yes (FDA) | Yes (FDA) | Yes (FDA) | HTN risk at high doses |
| Duloxetine | Yes (FDA) | No | No | Also for pain |
| Pregabalin | Yes | No | No | Controlled substance |
| Buspirone | Yes | No | No | No abuse potential |
| Propranolol | No | No | Performance only | Situational use only |

# Chapter 9: Bipolar Disorder

## 9.1 Overview

Bipolar disorder is characterized by episodes of mania or hypomania alternating with depressive episodes. Lifetime prevalence is ~1-2% for bipolar I and ~0.5-1% for bipolar II. Onset typically in late adolescence to early adulthood [^27^].

### 9.1.1 Mania vs. Hypomania

| Feature | Mania | Hypomania |
|---------|-------|-----------|
| **Duration** | ≥7 days (or any duration if hospitalized) | ≥4 consecutive days |
| **Severity** | Severe functional impairment; may be psychotic | Noticeable change, but functioning largely maintained |
| **Psychosis** | May be present (delusions, hallucinations) | **Absent by definition** |
| **Hospitalization** | Often required | Not required by definition |
| **Functional impact** | Marked interference with work, social, relationships | Some increase in functioning or mild impairment |

**Diagnostic Criteria for Manic Episode:**
Elevated, expansive, or irritable mood + increased energy/activity + ≥3 of the following (4 if mood is only irritable):
- Inflated self-esteem or grandiosity
- Decreased need for sleep (feels rested after 3 hours)
- Pressured speech (more and faster)
- Flight of ideas or racing thoughts
- Distractibility
- Increased goal-directed activity or psychomotor agitation
- Excessive involvement in risky activities (spending, sex, reckless driving)

## 9.2 Acute Mania Management

### 9.2.1 Hospitalization Criteria

- Severe mania with dangerous behavior
- Psychotic features
- Suicidal or homicidal ideation
- Inability to care for self (not eating, not sleeping)
- Substance intoxication complicating management
- Medical complications

### 9.2.2 Pharmacologic Management

| Severity | First-Line | Adjunct |
|----------|-----------|---------|
| **Moderate mania** | Atypical antipsychotic (olanzapine, risperidone, quetiapine, aripiprazole) OR valproate OR lithium | Short-term benzodiazepine for agitation/insomnia |
| **Severe mania** | Combination: lithium or valproate + atypical antipsychotic | Benzodiazepine for agitation |
| **Psychotic mania** | Atypical antipsychotic + lithium or valproate | ECT if refractory |

> **CRITICAL:** Avoid antidepressants during acute mania -- they may worsen or prolong the manic episode.

## 9.3 Bipolar Depression

### 9.3.1 Treatment

| Approach | Options | Evidence |
|----------|---------|----------|
| **First-line** | Quetiapine (monotherapy) | Strong evidence |
| | Olanzapine-fluoxetine combination (OFC, Symbyax) | FDA-approved |
| | Lurasidone 20-120 mg/day (with lithium or valproate) | FDA-approved |
| | Lamotrigine | Effective for preventing depressive episodes; slow titration |
| **Second-line** | Lithium (may reduce suicide risk) | Gold standard for maintenance |
| | Adjunctive antidepressant | Only with mood stabilizer; risk of switch to mania |

### 9.3.2 Caution with Antidepressants

- Risk of mood switch from depression to mania/hypomania
- Use only WITH a mood stabilizer or atypical antipsychotic
- Avoid in rapid cycling (≥4 episodes/year)
- Avoid in patients with history of antidepressant-induced mania
- Monitor closely for emergence of manic symptoms

## 9.4 Maintenance Therapy

| Agent | Indication | Monitoring Requirements |
|-------|-----------|------------------------|
| **Lithium** | First-line maintenance; anti-suicidal properties; reduces mortality | Serum levels (target 0.6-1.0 mEq/L), renal function (eGFR), thyroid (TSH), calcium, weight |
| **Valproate** | Alternative to lithium; effective for rapid cycling | LFTs, platelet count, weight, ammonia levels; **teratogenic** |
| **Lamotrigine** | Bipolar depression prevention; depressive episode prophylaxis | **Slow titration required** (rash/Stevens-Johnson risk); no blood levels needed |
| **Atypical antipsychotics** | Aripiprazole, olanzapine, quetiapine, risperidone, ziprasidone | Metabolic monitoring (weight, glucose, lipids, BP) every 3-6 months |

### 9.4.1 Lithium Monitoring

| Parameter | Baseline | Follow-Up |
|-----------|----------|-----------|
| Lithium level | -- | Every 3-6 months (target 0.6-1.0 mEq/L for maintenance) |
| Renal function (eGFR, creatinine) | Yes | Every 3-6 months |
| Thyroid function (TSH) | Yes | Every 6 months |
| Calcium | Yes | Every 6 months |
| Weight/BMI | Yes | Every visit |
| Pregnancy test (women of childbearing potential) | Yes | As indicated |

### 9.4.2 Rapid Cycling

Defined as ≥4 mood episodes (depression, mania, hypomania, or mixed) in 12 months.

**Management:**
1. Discontinue antidepressants (may induce cycling)
2. Optimize mood stabilizer (lithium + valproate combination often needed)
3. Add lamotrigine for depressive episode prevention
4. Check thyroid function; treat hypothyroidism (can worsen cycling)
5. Consider atypical antipsychotic adjunct
6. Evaluate for substance use (cocaine, alcohol can cause rapid cycling)

## 9.5 Physical Health Monitoring

All patients with bipolar disorder on maintenance therapy require regular monitoring due to metabolic risks:

| Parameter | Frequency |
|-----------|-----------|
| Weight/BMI | Every visit (initially monthly) |
| Fasting glucose or HbA1c | Every 3-6 months |
| Lipid profile | Every 6-12 months |
| Blood pressure | Every visit |
| Smoking status | Annually |
| Alcohol/substance use | Every visit |
| ECG | Baseline and periodically (especially with lithium, antipsychotics) |

# Chapter 10: Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD)

## 10.1 Overview

PTSD develops after exposure to actual or threatened death, serious injury, or sexual violence. The disorder is characterized by four symptom clusters persisting >1 month and causing significant distress or impairment [^28^][^29^].

**Lifetime Prevalence:** ~6-8% in the general population; higher in military veterans, first responders, and survivors of sexual assault.

### 10.1.1 Diagnostic Criteria (DSM-5)

| Cluster | Symptoms |
|---------|----------|
| **Intrusion** | Recurrent, involuntary memories; distressing dreams; flashbacks; intense psychological distress at reminders; physiological reactivity |
| **Avoidance** | Avoidance of distressing memories, thoughts, feelings; avoidance of external reminders |
| **Negative alterations in cognition/mood** | Inability to remember important aspects; negative beliefs about self/world; distorted blame; persistent negative emotions; diminished interest; detachment; restricted affect |
| **Marked alterations in arousal/reactivity** | Irritable behavior/anger outbursts; reckless/self-destructive behavior; hypervigilance; exaggerated startle; difficulty concentrating; sleep disturbance |

**Duration:** >1 month (symptoms <1 month = acute stress disorder)

### 10.1.2 Screening

- PCL-5 (PTSD Checklist for DSM-5): Score ≥33 suggests PTSD
- PC-PTSD-5: Brief 5-item screen for primary care
- CAPS-5: Gold standard diagnostic interview (clinician-administered)

## 10.2 Treatment

### 10.2.1 First-Line Treatment

**VA/DoD and APA Guidelines recommend trauma-focused psychotherapy as first-line when available** [^28^][^29^]:

| Therapy | Approach | Sessions |
|---------|----------|----------|
| **Prolonged Exposure (PE)** | Gradual, repeated exposure to trauma memories and reminders | 8-15 sessions |
| **Cognitive Processing Therapy (CPT)** | Cognitive restructuring of trauma-related beliefs; written trauma account | 12 sessions |
| **Eye Movement Desensitization and Reprocessing (EMDR)** | Bilateral stimulation while processing traumatic memories | 6-12 sessions |
| **Trauma-focused CBT** | Combination of exposure and cognitive techniques | Variable |

**Pharmacotherapy** (alternative or adjunct):

| Class | Agent | Dosing | Evidence |
|-------|-------|--------|----------|
| **SSRIs** | Sertraline | 50-200 mg daily | FDA-approved for PTSD |
| | Paroxetine | 20-60 mg daily | FDA-approved for PTSD |
| | Fluoxetine | 20-60 mg daily | Strong evidence |
| **SNRIs** | Venlafaxine XR | 75-300 mg daily | Strong evidence |

Continue pharmacotherapy for ≥6-12 months after response; high relapse rate with early discontinuation.

### 10.2.2 Prazosin for Nightmares

Prazosin is an alpha-1 adrenergic antagonist that reduces PTSD-related nightmares and sleep disturbance [^28^][^29^][^30^].

| Parameter | Details |
|-----------|---------|
| **Dosing** | Start 1 mg at bedtime; titrate by 1 mg every 2 weeks to 3-15 mg |
| **Mechanism** | Reduces CNS noradrenergic activity; decreases nightmares and sleep disruption |
| **Evidence** | Mixed -- initial positive trials; larger VA Cooperative Study (N=304) negative for overall PTSD symptoms but showed benefit for nightmares |
| **Current guidelines** | VA/DoD: Insufficient evidence to recommend for or against. AASM: Continues to recommend as first-line option |
| **Clinical approach** | Trial may be reasonable; discontinue if no benefit after 4-8 weeks at therapeutic dose |

### 10.2.3 Important Treatment Considerations

| Consideration | Guidance |
|--------------|----------|
| **Benzodiazepines** | **Contraindicated** -- may interfere with fear extinction, worsen outcomes, high abuse potential [^29^] |
| **Sleep disturbance** | Prazosin, trazodone 50-150 mg, mirtazapine 7.5-15 mg, CBT-I |
| **Comorbid depression** | SSRIs address both PTSD and depression |
| **Substance use** | Common comorbidity; treat concurrently; avoid sedating agents |
| **Cannabis** | Not recommended as self-medication; may interfere with treatment |
| **Duration** | Treat for ≥12 months; high relapse rate |
| **Treatment-resistant PTSD** | Consider adding second-generation antipsychotic (risperidone, quetiapine), MDMA-assisted therapy (investigational, breakthrough therapy designation) |

# Chapter 11: Substance Use Disorders

## 11.1 Alcohol Use Disorder (AUD)

### 11.1.1 Screening and Diagnosis

**Screening Tools:**

| Tool | Items | Cutoff | Use |
|------|-------|--------|-----|
| **AUDIT-C** | 3 | Men ≥4, Women ≥3 | Brief screening in primary care |
| **AUDIT** | 10 | ≥8 indicates hazardous drinking; ≥16 indicates dependence | Comprehensive assessment |
| **Single-item** | 1 | ≥5 drinks/day (men), ≥4 drinks/day (women), ≥1 time in past year | Ultra-brief screening |

**DSM-5 Severity:**
- Mild: 2-3 criteria
- Moderate: 4-5 criteria
- Severe: ≥6 criteria

### 11.1.2 Treatment

| Modality | Approach | Evidence |
|----------|----------|----------|
| **Brief intervention** | Motivational interviewing (5-15 minutes) | Effective for risky drinking; reduces consumption by ~20-30% |
| **Psychosocial** | AA, SMART Recovery, CBT, contingency management | Foundation of treatment; improves outcomes |

**FDA-Approved Medications for AUD:**

| Medication | Mechanism | Dosing | Efficacy | Key Considerations |
|------------|-----------|--------|----------|-------------------|
| **Naltrexone** | Mu-opioid antagonist | 50 mg daily OR 380 mg IM monthly (Vivitrol) | Reduces cravings and heavy drinking days | Requires opioid-free period (7-10 days) before starting; blocks opioid analgesics |
| **Acamprosate** | GABA/glutamate modulation | 666 mg TID (2 tablets) | Maintains abstinence; most effective after detox | Avoid in severe renal impairment (CrCl <30) |
| **Disulfiram** | Acetaldehyde dehydrogenase inhibitor | 250-500 mg daily | Aversive therapy (flushing, nausea if alcohol consumed) | Hepatotoxic; requires supervised administration; many drug interactions; **requires complete abstinence commitment** |

**Selection:**
- Patient preferring reduced drinking: Naltrexone
- Patient aiming for abstinence: Acamprosate or naltrexone
- Highly motivated patient with supervised administration: Disulfiram
- Combination naltrexone + acamprosate may be superior for some patients

**Important:** All medications are more effective when combined with behavioral support/counseling.

### 11.1.3 Alcohol Withdrawal

| Severity | Symptoms | Management |
|----------|----------|------------|
| **Mild** (CIWA <8) | Tremor, anxiety, insomnia, mild autonomic hyperactivity | Outpatient: Chlordiazepoxide 25-50 mg Q6H or lorazepam taper; thiamine 100 mg daily; folic acid; multivitamin |
| **Moderate** (CIWA 8-15) | Marked tremor, diaphoresis, tachycardia, mild agitation | Inpatient: Benzodiazepine taper (lorazepam, diazepam, or chlordiazepoxide); CIWA-based dosing |
| **Severe** (CIWA >15) | Hallucinations, severe agitation, seizures, delirium tremens | **ICU:** High-dose benzodiazepines; phenobarbital if refractory; propofol for DTs; thiamine IV before glucose |

> **EMERGENCY ALERT:** Delirium tremens has mortality ~5-15% without treatment. Characterized by confusion, agitation, fever, tachycardia, hypertension, and hallucinations occurring 48-96 hours after last drink. Requires ICU-level care.

**All alcohol withdrawal patients must receive:**
- Thiamine 100-300 mg IV before any glucose-containing fluids (prevents Wernicke encephalopathy)
- Folic acid 1 mg daily
- Multivitamin
- Electrolyte repletion (Mg, K, Phos)
- Fluid resuscitation

## 11.2 Opioid Use Disorder (OUD)

### 11.2.1 Overview

OUD is a chronic, relapsing disorder characterized by compulsive opioid use despite negative consequences. The opioid epidemic continues to cause unprecedented overdose deaths, primarily driven by illicit fentanyl and synthetic opioids [^31^][^32^][^33^].

### 11.2.2 Medications for Opioid Use Disorder (MOUD)

> **2024 UPDATED GUIDELINES:** Buprenorphine and methadone are BOTH first-line options (methadone elevated to equal status). Both reduce mortality by ~50% compared to no treatment. Psychosocial treatment should be offered as adjunct but should NOT be mandatory for medication access [^31^].

| Medication | Mechanism | Administration | Key Advantages | Key Considerations |
|------------|-----------|---------------|----------------|-------------------|
| **Buprenorphine** | Partial mu-agonist; kappa-antagonist | Office-based (DEA waiver); SL film/tablet; monthly injection available | Ceiling effect on respiratory depression (safer in overdose); office-based prescribing; blocks other opioids | Requires moderate withdrawal before initiation (COWS ≥8); precipitated withdrawal risk |
| **Methadone** | Full mu-agonist | Opioid Treatment Program (OTP) only; daily observed dosing initially | Higher retention rates; long-acting; blocks withdrawal 24+ hours | QTc prolongation; respiratory depression (no ceiling); strict regulatory requirements |
| **Naltrexone XR** | Mu-antagonist | Monthly IM injection; any licensed prescriber | No abuse potential; monthly dosing convenient | Requires 7-10 days opioid-free before initiation; blocks opioid analgesia; lower adherence |

### 11.2.3 Buprenorphine Induction Protocol

| Day | Protocol |
|-----|----------|
| **Pre-induction** | Patient must be in moderate withdrawal (COWS ≥8) to avoid precipitated withdrawal |
| **Day 1** | Initial dose: 2-4 mg SL; may repeat in 1-2 hours if withdrawal persists; total day 1 dose: 4-8 mg |
| **Day 2** | Total daily dose based on day 1 response; typically 8-16 mg |
| **Target maintenance** | 8-16 mg/day (range 4-24 mg); once-daily or divided BID |
| **Monitoring** | Weekly initially, then biweekly, then monthly; urine drug testing; pill counts |

**Precipitated Withdrawal Warning:** If buprenorphine is given while full agonist still occupies receptors, the partial antagonist properties can precipitate severe withdrawal. Wait until COWS ≥8 (moderate withdrawal: yawning, lacrimation, rhinorrhea, piloerection, restlessness, dilated pupils) before first dose.

### 11.2.4 Harm Reduction

| Strategy | Details |
|----------|---------|
| **Naloxone distribution** | Provide naloxone to ALL patients with OUD and household members; train on recognition of overdose and administration |
| **Syringe services programs** | Reduce HIV and HCV transmission; linkage to care |
| **Fentanyl test strips** | Allow testing of drugs for fentanyl contamination |
| **Low threshold access** | Reduce barriers to MOUD initiation |
| **Good Samaritan laws** | Legal protections for calling 911 during overdose |

### 11.2.5 Opioid Overdose Recognition and Response

**Signs:** Unresponsiveness, pinpoint pupils, respiratory depression (slow, shallow, or absent breathing), cyanosis, bradycardia, hypotension, snoring/gurgling sounds.

**Response:**
1. Call emergency services immediately
2. Administer naloxone (2 mg intranasal or 0.4 mg IM/IV); repeat every 2-3 minutes if no response
3. Provide rescue breathing if trained
4. Place in recovery position
5. Naloxone duration (30-90 minutes) is shorter than most opioids -- monitor for recurrent sedation

## 11.3 Tobacco Cessation

### 11.3.1 First-Line Pharmacotherapy

**2024 WHO Guideline recommends all adults who use tobacco be offered help to quit** [^34^][^35^].

| Medication | Mechanism | Dosing | NNT | Notes |
|------------|-----------|--------|-----|-------|
| **Varenicline** | Partial nicotinic agonist | 0.5 mg daily x 3 days, then 0.5 mg BID x 4 days, then 1 mg BID for 12 weeks; start 1 week before quit date | ~8 | **Most effective single agent**; nausea is common side effect |
| **Combination NRT** | Patch (long-acting) + gum/lozenge (short-acting) | Patch: 21 mg → 14 mg → 7 mg stepwise; Gum 2-4 mg PRN | ~12 | **Most effective NRT approach** |
| **Bupropion SR** | Norepinephrine-dopamine reuptake inhibitor | 150 mg daily x 3 days, then 150 mg BID; start 1-2 weeks before quit date | ~12 | Contraindicated in seizure disorders, eating disorders, MAOI use |
| **Cytisine** | Plant-derived partial nicotinic agonist | Per protocol | Moderate | Available in some countries; over-the-counter |

**Combination Pharmacotherapy:**
- Varenicline + NRT: Superior to either alone
- Bupropion + NRT: Superior to either alone
- Combining long-acting (patch) + short-acting (gum, lozenge, inhaler) NRT: Superior to single NRT

**Key Principles:**
- All medications are more effective with behavioral support/counseling
- Combining pharmacotherapy with counseling increases quit rates 2-3x
- No significant safety concerns with NRT in patients with cardiovascular disease
- Set a quit date (within 2 weeks of starting medication)
- Most relapses occur within first 2 weeks -- intensive support during this period is critical

**E-Cigarettes:** Not recommended as first-line. May be less harmful than combustible cigarettes but are not harmless. Use only if other methods have failed and patient is unable to quit.

# Chapter 12: Sleep Disorders

## 12.1 Insomnia

### 12.1.1 Definition

Insomnia is difficulty initiating or maintaining sleep despite adequate opportunity and circumstances for sleep, resulting in daytime impairment, occurring ≥3 nights per week for ≥3 months [^36^][^37^].

### 12.1.2 Treatment

**CBT-I (Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Insomnia) is the first-line, strongly recommended treatment** [^37^][^38^].

**CBT-I Components:**

| Component | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Stimulus control** | Reassociate bed with sleep; go to bed only when sleepy; use bed only for sleep and sex; get out of bed if awake >20 minutes; fixed wake time |
| **Sleep restriction** | Limit time in bed to actual sleep time; temporarily reduces total sleep to consolidate sleep; then gradually increase |
| **Cognitive restructuring** | Challenge maladaptive beliefs about sleep; reduce sleep-related anxiety |
| **Relaxation training** | Progressive muscle relaxation, diaphragmatic breathing, guided imagery |
| **Sleep hygiene education** | Regular schedule, limit caffeine/alcohol, optimize bedroom environment |

> **Important:** Sleep hygiene education alone is NOT sufficient treatment for chronic insomnia.

### 12.1.3 Pharmacotherapy (When CBT-I Unavailable or Insufficient)

| Class | Agent | Indication | Dosing | Key Considerations |
|-------|-------|-----------|--------|-------------------|
| **Orexin receptor antagonists** | Suvorexant | Onset + maintenance | 10-20 mg | No tolerance development; may cause next-day somnolence |
| | Lemborexant | Onset + maintenance | 5-10 mg | Similar to suvorexant |
| | Daridorexant | Onset + maintenance | 25-50 mg | Newer agent; improved next-day alertness profile |
| **Z-drugs** | Zolpidem | Sleep onset | 5-10 mg (women: 5 mg) | **Black box warning for complex sleep behaviors**; avoid in elderly; avoid alcohol |
| | Eszopiclone | Onset + maintenance | 1-3 mg | Metallic taste; avoid in elderly |
| | Zaleplon | Sleep onset | 5-10 mg | Ultra-short acting; minimal next-day effects |
| **Benzodiazepines** | Temazepam | Onset + maintenance | 7.5-30 mg | Tolerance, dependence, falls; **avoid in elderly** |
| **Low-dose doxepin** | Doxepin | Sleep maintenance | 3-6 mg | Low-dose antihistamine effect; anticholinergic at higher doses |
| **Melatonin receptor agonist** | Ramelteon | Sleep onset | 8 mg | No abuse potential; minimal side effects; less effective |

**Medications to AVOID for Chronic Insomnia:**
- Trazodone (insufficient evidence, orthostatic hypotension, priapism risk)
- Diphenhydramine/doxylamine (anticholinergic, cognitive impairment, paradoxical reactions)
- Benzodiazepines in elderly (falls, cognitive effects, dependence)
- Alcohol (fragmented sleep, dependence)

### 12.1.4 AASM Guideline Recommendations

| Recommendation | Strength |
|---------------|----------|
| CBT-I as first-line treatment | Strong |
| CBT-I alone preferred over combination | Conditional |
| Combination CBT-I + medication over medication alone | Conditional |
| Limit hypnotic use to shortest duration necessary | Strong |

## 12.2 Obstructive Sleep Apnea (OSA)

### 12.2.1 Screening: STOP-BANG Questionnaire

| Component | Question | Score |
|-----------|----------|-------|
| **S** | Do you **S**nore loudly? | 1 |
| **T** | Do you often feel **T**ired, fatigued, or sleepy during the day? | 1 |
| **O** | Has anyone **O**bserved you stop breathing during sleep? | 1 |
| **P** | Do you have or are you being treated for high blood **P**ressure? | 1 |
| **B** | **B**MI >35 kg/m2? | 1 |
| **A** | **A**ge >50 years? | 1 |
| **N** | **N**eck circumference >16 inches (40 cm)? | 1 |
| **G** | **G**ender male? | 1 |

**Interpretation:**
- Score 0-2: Low risk
- Score 3-4: Intermediate risk
- Score 5-8: High risk
- Score ≥5-6: Very high risk

### 12.2.2 Diagnosis

- Polysomnography (PSG) is the gold standard
- Home sleep apnea testing (HSAT) acceptable for uncomplicated, high pre-test probability cases
- Apnea-Hypopnea Index (AHI): Normal <5; Mild 5-14; Moderate 15-29; Severe ≥30

### 12.2.3 Treatment

| Treatment | Indication | Details |
|-----------|-----------|---------|
| **CPAP** (Continuous Positive Airway Pressure) | Moderate-severe OSA; mild OSA with symptoms | First-line, most effective; adherence ≥4 hours/night for ≥70% of nights |
| **Oral appliance** (mandibular advancement device) | Mild-moderate OSA; CPAP-intolerant | Dentist-fitted; less effective than CPAP but better tolerated |
| **Weight loss** | All overweight/obese patients | ≥10% weight loss significantly reduces AHI; bariatric surgery if BMI >40 |
| **Positional therapy** | Positional OSA (supine AHI >2x lateral) | Devices to prevent supine sleeping |
| **Myofunctional therapy** | Adjunct | Upper airway exercises |
| **Surgery** | Refractory OSA | UPPP, maxillomandibular advancement, hypoglossal nerve stimulation |

**CPAP Adherence Improvement:**
- Early, intensive support and troubleshooting
- Humidification, mask fitting, pressure adjustments
- Regular follow-up in first month
- Telemedicine monitoring
- Treat nasal congestion (intranasal corticosteroids, saline rinses)

### 12.2.4 OSA Consequences

| System | Consequences |
|--------|-------------|
| **Cardiovascular** | Hypertension, atrial fibrillation, heart failure, coronary artery disease, stroke |
| **Metabolic** | Insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, weight gain |
| **Neurocognitive** | Daytime sleepiness, impaired cognition, motor vehicle accidents, depression |
| **Quality of life** | Reduced productivity, relationship strain, decreased quality of life |

## 12.3 Restless Legs Syndrome (RLS)

### 12.3.1 Diagnostic Criteria (URGE Mnemonic)

| Letter | Feature |
|--------|---------|
| **U** | **U**rge to move the legs (usually accompanied by uncomfortable sensations) |
| **R** | **R**est-induced (worse during rest/inactivity) |
| **G** | **G**ets better with movement (relieved by walking/stretching) |
| **E** | **E**vening/night worsening (circadian component) |

### 12.3.2 Evaluation

- Check ferritin: Treat if <75-100 μg/L (iron is a cofactor for dopamine synthesis)
- Rule out secondary causes: Iron deficiency, renal failure, pregnancy, neuropathy, medication-induced (antihistamines, SSRIs/SNRIs, dopamine antagonists)
- Screen for OSA (RLS and PLMD commonly coexist with OSA)

### 12.3.3 Treatment (2024 AASM Updated Guidelines)

> **Updated Recommendation:** Dopaminergic agents (pramipexole, ropinirole) are NO LONGER recommended as first-line due to risk of augmentation (worsening of symptoms over time) [^40^][^41^].

**First-Line (Strong Recommendation):**

| Class | Agent | Dosing | Notes |
|-------|-------|--------|-------|
| **Gabapentinoids** | Gabapentin enacarbil | 600-1200 mg evening | FDA-approved for RLS |
| | Pregabalin | 75-450 mg/day (evening) | Effective; may cause sedation |
| | Gabapentin | 300-2400 mg/day (evening) | Less expensive alternative |
| **IV Iron** | Ferric carboxymaltose | Per protocol | Strongly recommended if ferritin <75-100 μg/L |

**Avoid/Address First:**
- Alcohol, caffeine (especially evening)
- Antihistamines (diphenhydramine, hydroxyzine -- worsen RLS)
- Serotonergic medications may worsen RLS (discuss risk/benefit if treating depression)
- Untreated OSA

**Second-Line:**
- Low-dose dopamine agonists (pramipexole 0.125-0.5 mg, ropinirole 0.25-4 mg) -- only if gabapentinoids contraindicated or ineffective; monitor closely for augmentation
- Opioids (low-dose oxycodone): Refractory cases only

## 12.4 Narcolepsy

### 12.4.1 Clinical Features

Narcolepsy is a disorder of REM sleep regulation characterized by:
- **Excessive daytime sleepiness (EDS):** Irresistible sleep attacks
- **Cataplexy:** Sudden loss of muscle tone triggered by emotions (laughter, surprise, anger)
- **Sleep paralysis:** Inability to move when falling asleep or waking
- **Hypnagogic/hypnopompic hallucinations:** Vivid dream-like experiences at sleep-wake transitions
- **Disrupted nocturnal sleep:** Frequent awakenings

### 12.4.2 Diagnosis

- Polysomnography followed by Multiple Sleep Latency Test (MSLT)
- Mean sleep latency <8 minutes on MSLT; ≥2 sleep-onset REM periods (SOREMPs)
- CSF hypocretin-1 level <110 pg/mL (for type 1 narcolepsy with cataplexy)

### 12.4.3 Treatment

| Symptom | Treatment Options |
|---------|-------------------|
| **Excessive daytime sleepiness** | Modafinil 100-400 mg; armodafinil 150-250 mg; pitolisant (H3 antagonist); solriamfetol (DNRI); methylphenidate; sodium oxybate |
| **Cataplexy** | Sodium oxybate (sodium oxybate is also approved for EDS); venlafaxine; pitolisant; protriptyline |
| **Sleep paralysis/hallucinations** | Sodium oxybate; REM-suppressing antidepressants |
| **Supportive** | Scheduled naps (15-20 minutes), good sleep hygiene, safety (driving restrictions until controlled) |

# Chapter 13: ADHD and Eating Disorders

## 13.1 Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD)

### 13.1.1 Adult ADHD Assessment

**Diagnostic Criteria (Adults):** Requires ≥5 symptoms of inattention and/or ≥5 symptoms of hyperactivity-impulsivity (compared to ≥6 in children) present before age 12, in ≥2 settings, causing functional impairment [^44^][^45^].

**Inattention Symptoms:**
- Careless mistakes; difficulty sustaining attention
- Poor organization; avoids mental effort
- Loses things; easily distracted; forgetful

**Hyperactivity-Impulsivity Symptoms:**
- Fidgeting; leaving seat; unable to play/relax quietly
- On the go; talking excessively
- Blurting out answers; difficulty waiting turn; interrupting

**Assessment Tools:**
- ASRS-v1.1 (Adult ADHD Self-Report Scale): 6-item screening
- DIVA-5: Semi-structured diagnostic interview
- Collateral history from family member/partner
- School records if available (document childhood symptoms)

**Differential Diagnosis:** Anxiety disorders, depression, bipolar disorder, substance use disorders, sleep disorders (especially OSA), thyroid dysfunction, autism spectrum disorder, personality disorders, medication effects.

### 13.1.2 Pharmacologic Treatment

#### First-Line: Stimulants [^44^][^45^][^46^]

Stimulants are effective in approximately 70% of patients with ADHD.

| Agent | Starting Dose | Typical Range | Duration | Notes |
|-------|--------------|---------------|----------|-------|
| **Methylphenidate IR** (Ritalin) | 5 mg BID-TID | 10-60 mg/day | 3-4 hours | Short-acting; multiple doses needed |
| **Methylphenidate ER** (Concerta) | 18-36 mg daily | 18-72 mg daily | 10-12 hours | Once daily; osmotic delivery system |
| **Methylphenidate LA** (Ritalin LA) | 20 mg daily | 20-60 mg daily | 6-8 hours | Bimodal release |
| **Lisdexamfetamine** (Vyvanse) | 30 mg daily | 30-70 mg daily | 10-12 hours | Prodrug; lower abuse potential; once daily |
| **Mixed amphetamine salts** (Adderall XR) | 10-20 mg daily | 5-40 mg/day | 10-12 hours | Most widely prescribed |
| **Dexmethylphenidate** (Focalin) | 5 mg BID | 5-20 mg/day | 4-6 hours (IR); 8-12 hours (XR) | More potent enantiomer |

**Stimulant Trial Approach:**
1. Trial methylphenidate-based product first (lower abuse potential)
2. If inadequate response or poor tolerability, trial amphetamine-based product
3. If one class fails, try the other class (some patients respond to only one)
4. Titrate every 1-2 weeks based on response and side effects

**Common Side Effects:** Decreased appetite, insomnia, headache, irritability, anxiety, increased heart rate/BP, dry mouth, GI upset.

**Monitoring:**
- Baseline and periodic BP, HR, weight
- Assess for misuse/diversion (prescription drug monitoring programs)
- Height/weight in children
- ECG if indicated (history of sudden cardiac death, structural heart disease, arrhythmias, syncope, family history of early cardiac death)

#### Second-Line: Non-Stimulants [^44^][^45^][^46^]

| Agent | Mechanism | Dosing | Key Advantages | Key Disadvantages |
|-------|-----------|--------|---------------|-------------------|
| **Atomoxetine** | SNRI (norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor) | 40 mg → 80-100 mg daily | Once daily; no abuse potential | Takes 2-4 weeks for effect; GI upset; rare liver injury |
| **Guanfacine ER** | Alpha-2A agonist | 1-4 mg daily | May help emotional dysregulation; no abuse potential | Sedation, hypotension, bradycardia; must taper |
| **Clonidine ER** | Alpha-2A agonist | 0.1-0.4 mg daily | Similar to guanfacine | More sedation; must taper (rebound hypertension risk) |
| **Bupropion** | NDRI | 150-450 mg/day | Off-label; may help comorbid depression | Lower efficacy than stimulants; avoid with seizure history |

**Non-Stimulant Selection:**
- Comorbid anxiety: Atomoxetine, guanfacine
- Comorbid depression: Bupropion, atomoxetine
- Substance use disorder: Atomoxetine, guanfacine (non-controlled substances)
- Cardiovascular concerns: Atomoxetine, guanfacine
- Evening symptoms: Guanfacine (long-acting)
- Stimulant augmentation: Guanfacine or clonidine may be added to stimulant

### 13.1.3 Non-Pharmacologic Treatment

| Modality | Approach |
|----------|----------|
| **CBT adapted for ADHD** | Organization, time management, planning skills, problem-solving |
| **Coaching** | ADHD-specific coaching for daily functioning |
| **Skills training** | Executive function skills development |
| **Workplace/school accommodations** | Extended time, quiet environment, written instructions, breaks |
| **Exercise** | Regular aerobic exercise improves attention and executive function |
| **Mindfulness** | Mindfulness-based interventions improve attention regulation |
| **Support groups** | Peer support and shared strategies |

### 13.1.4 Special Populations

| Population | Considerations |
|-----------|---------------|
| **Pregnancy** | Generally avoid stimulants; if needed, lowest effective dose; risk category C |
| **Elderly** | Start lower doses; monitor cardiovascular effects; risk of falls |
| **Substance use disorder** | Non-stimulants preferred; if stimulant needed, lisdexamfetamine (lower abuse potential) with close monitoring |
| **Cardiovascular disease** | Cardiology consultation; ECG; monitor BP/HR closely |

## 13.2 Eating Disorders

### 13.2.1 Anorexia Nervosa (AN)

**Diagnostic Criteria (DSM-5):**
- Restriction of energy intake leading to significantly low body weight (BMI <18.5 in adults)
- Intense fear of gaining weight or becoming fat
- Disturbance in self-perceived weight/shape, undue influence on self-evaluation, or denial of seriousness
- Amenorrhea (DSM-IV) -- NO LONGER REQUIRED in DSM-5

**Medical Complications:**

| System | Complications |
|--------|--------------|
| **Cardiovascular** | Bradycardia, hypotension, orthostatic hypotension, QTc prolongation, arrhythmias, cardiomyopathy, sudden death |
| **Metabolic** | Electrolyte abnormalities (hypokalemia, hypophosphatemia, hypomagnesemia), refeeding syndrome |
| **Hematologic** | Leukopenia, anemia, thrombocytopenia |
| **GI** | Gastroparesis, constipation, elevated transaminases, superior mesenteric artery syndrome |
| **Endocrine** | Amenorrhea, osteoporosis, hypothyroidism (sick euthyroid), elevated cortisol |
| **Neurologic** | Brain atrophy (partially reversible), seizures, peripheral neuropathy |
| **Psychiatric** | Depression, anxiety, OCD, suicidality |

> **CRITICAL SAFETY ALERT:** AN has the highest mortality rate of any psychiatric disorder (~5-10% per decade). Cardiac complications and suicide are leading causes of death.

### 13.2.2 Refeeding Syndrome

Refeeding syndrome is a potentially fatal metabolic disturbance that occurs when nutrition is reintroduced after prolonged starvation.

**Prevention:**
- Start nutrition slowly: 1000-1200 kcal/day initially
- Increase by 200-300 kcal every 3-5 days
- Monitor phosphorus, potassium, magnesium, glucose DAILY for first week
- Supplement prophylactically if at high risk
- **Cardiac monitoring:** Continuous telemetry during acute refeeding

### 13.2.3 AN Treatment

| Setting | Criteria | Treatment |
|---------|----------|-----------|
| **Outpatient** | BMI >17, hemodynamically stable, no electrolyte abnormalities, motivated | Eating-disorder-focused CBT, FBT for adolescents, MANTRA, SSCM |
| **Intensive outpatient/partial hospitalization** | BMI 15-17, some medical stability, failed outpatient | Structured meals + therapy 5-7 days/week |
| **Inpatient/residential** | BMI <13 (or <75% expected), rapid weight loss, hemodynamic instability, ECG abnormalities, electrolyte disturbances, failed lower level of care | Medical stabilization + nutritional rehabilitation + therapy |

**Medications:** Generally NOT effective for weight restoration in AN. May treat comorbid depression/anxiety with SSRIs once weight partially restored.

### 13.2.4 Bulimia Nervosa (BN)

**Diagnostic Criteria:**
- Recurrent episodes of binge eating (eating large amounts in discrete period with sense of loss of control)
- Recurrent inappropriate compensatory behaviors (self-induced vomiting, laxatives, diuretics, fasting, excessive exercise) ≥1x/week for 3 months
- Self-evaluation unduly influenced by body shape/weight
- Does not occur exclusively during episodes of anorexia

**Medical Complications:**
- Electrote disturbances: Hypokalemia (most dangerous), hypochloremic metabolic alkalosis (from vomiting)
- Dental erosion (perimolysis from gastric acid)
- Parotid enlargement ("chipmunk cheeks")
- Russell sign (calluses on dorsal hand from self-induced vomiting)
- Esophageal tears (Mallory-Weiss syndrome), Barrett's esophagus
- Arrhythmias (from electrolyte disturbances), cardiomyopathy (ipecac)
- Constipation (laxative abuse), dehydration

**Treatment:**

| Approach | Details | Evidence |
|----------|---------|----------|
| **CBT-BN** (cognitive behavioral therapy) | 16-20 sessions over 4-5 months; structured, manualized | **First-line; most effective** for reducing binge/purge [^42^] |
| **Fluoxetine** | 60 mg daily (higher than depression dose) | **Only FDA-approved medication for BN**; reduces binge/purge frequency [^42^] |
| **Guided self-help** | CBT-based self-help with brief supportive sessions | Effective for milder cases |
| **Interpersonal psychotherapy (IPT)** | 16-20 sessions | Alternative to CBT; slower onset but comparable at 12 months |

**Important:** CBT-BN is more effective than medication alone. Combining CBT with fluoxetine may be optimal for severe BN.

### 13.2.5 Binge Eating Disorder (BED)

**Diagnostic Criteria:**
- Recurrent episodes of binge eating (loss of control, larger than normal amount) ≥1x/week for 3 months
- **NO regular compensatory behaviors** (distinguishes from BN)
- Associated with marked distress
- Three of: eating rapidly, eating until uncomfortably full, eating large amounts when not hungry, eating alone due to embarrassment, feeling disgusted/depressed/guilty after overeating

**Treatment:**

| Approach | Details | Evidence |
|----------|---------|----------|
| **CBT-BED** | Individual or group; 16-20 sessions | **First-line** [^43^] |
| **Guided self-help** | CBT-based materials with brief support | Effective for many patients |
| **Lisdexamfetamine** | 50-70 mg daily | **FDA-approved** for moderate-severe BED; reduces binge days |
| **Topiramate** | 100-400 mg/day | Effective but cognitive side effects, weight loss, paresthesias |
| **SSRIs** | Sertraline, fluoxetine, escitalopram | May reduce binge frequency; less robust evidence |

**Important Notes:**
- Psychological treatments effectively reduce binge eating but have limited effect on body weight
- Address obesity separately (if present) through lifestyle modification, GLP-1 agonists, or metabolic/bariatric surgery referral
- Lisdexamfetamine is the only FDA-approved medication specifically for BED

### 13.2.6 Eating Disorder Screening in Primary Care

| Tool | Purpose | Items |
|------|---------|-------|
| **SCOFF questionnaire** | Brief screening for eating disorders | 5 items; score ≥2 suggests ED |
| **Eating Disorder Examination (EDE-Q)** | Self-report assessment of ED psychopathology | 28 items; used in research and clinical settings |
| **Medical monitoring** | Weight, vitals (including orthostatics), ECG, electrolytes | Essential for all patients with suspected ED |

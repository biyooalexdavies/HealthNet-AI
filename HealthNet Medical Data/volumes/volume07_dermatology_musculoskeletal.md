# Chapter 1: Eczema (Atopic Dermatitis)

## 1.1 Overview

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a chronic, relapsing inflammatory skin disease characterized by intense pruritus, eczematous morphology, and skin barrier dysfunction. It represents one of the most common skin conditions globally, affecting up to 20% of children and 7-10% of adults. The disease demonstrates a bimodal distribution with peaks in infancy and early adulthood, and approximately 70% of childhood cases remit by adolescence.

### Pathophysiology

AD arises from a complex interplay of multiple factors:

- **Type 2 inflammation:** Key cytokines IL-4, IL-13, and IL-31 drive pruritus, barrier dysfunction, and inflammation. Th2 lymphocyte polarization dominates the acute phase, with progressive involvement of Th1, Th17, and Th22 pathways in chronic disease.
- **Skin barrier defects:** Loss-of-function mutations in the filaggrin gene (FLG) impair epidermal barrier integrity, increasing transepidermal water loss and allergen penetration. Filaggrin mutations are present in 10-30% of European AD patients and confer stronger disease persistence.
- **Cutaneous microbiome dysbiosis:** *Staphylococcus aureus* colonization occurs in >90% of lesional skin and 70% of non-lesional skin in AD patients, compared to ~10% in healthy controls. *S. aureus* superantigens exacerbate inflammation.
- **Immune dysregulation:** Cutaneous T-cell infiltration, dendritic cell activation, and mast cell involvement perpetuate the inflammatory cycle. Pruritus leads to scratching, which further damages the barrier and drives the "itch-scratch cycle." [^1^]

### Diagnostic Features (Hanifin-Rajka / AAD Criteria)

Essential features: **pruritus** plus **eczema** (acute, subacute, or chronic morphology with typical age-specific patterns).

Important features include: early age of onset; chronic or relapsing history; personal or family history of atopy (asthma, allergic rhinitis, allergic conjunctivitis); xerosis; facial or extensor involvement in infants; flexural involvement in adults; and serum IgE elevation.

| Severity | Clinical Characteristics | Impact on Quality of Life |
|----------|-------------------------|--------------------------|
| **Mild** | Limited body surface area (BSA <5%); infrequent flares; manageable with topical therapy alone | Minimal interference with sleep, daily activities, psychosocial functioning |
| **Moderate** | BSA 5-20%; frequent flares; requires maintenance therapy; moderate pruritus | Moderate sleep disturbance; some impact on daily activities and psychosocial functioning |
| **Severe** | BSA >20%; constant or near-constant disease; refractory to topical therapy; severe pruritus | Significant sleep loss; substantial impact on daily activities, work/school performance, and mental health |

---

## 1.2 Red Flags / Emergency Features

> **ECZEMA HERPETICUM (KAPOSI VARICELLIFORM ERUPTION)**
> HSV superinfection in AD patients presents with monomorphic vesicles, punched-out erosions, fever, and malaise. This is a **DERMATOLOGIC EMERGENCY** requiring immediate IV acyclovir to prevent disseminated infection, keratoconjunctivitis, and systemic sepsis. [^14^]

> **BACTERIAL SUPERINFECTION / CELLULITIS**
> Rapidly spreading erythema, warmth, pain, and fever in an AD patient suggest secondary bacterial infection requiring prompt systemic antibiotics. Community-acquired MRSA should be considered in regions with high prevalence.

> **Erythroderma (generalized exfoliative dermatitis)**
> Generalized erythema covering >90% BSA represents a dermatologic emergency with risks of thermoregulatory compromise, fluid/electrolyte imbalance, and cardiac stress. Requires hospitalization.

## 1.3 Topical Corticosteroids — Potency Classification

Topical corticosteroids (TCS) remain the cornerstone of AD therapy. The 2024 AAD Guidelines emphasize using the lowest effective potency for the shortest duration, with proactive maintenance strategies to reduce flare frequency. [^2^] [^3^]

| Class | Potency | Representative Agents | Appropriate Body Sites |
|-------|---------|----------------------|----------------------|
| **I (Super-high)** | Most potent | Clobetasol propionate 0.05%, augmented betamethasone dipropionate 0.05% | Thick plaques on palms, soles (short-term only) |
| **II (High)** | Potent | Fluocinonide 0.05%, betamethasone dipropionate 0.05%, desoximetasone 0.25% | Trunk, extremities (limited duration) |
| **III (High-Medium)** | Potent | Triamcinolone acetonide 0.5%, fluticasone propionate 0.005% | Trunk, extremities |
| **IV (Medium)** | Mid-strength | Triamcinolone acetonide 0.1%, fluocinolone acetonide 0.025%, mometasone furoate 0.1% | Trunk, extremities; short-term on face/folds in adults |
| **V (Low-Medium)** | Lower-mid | Fluocinolone acetonide 0.01%, hydrocortisone valerate 0.2%, fluticasone propionate 0.05% | Face, intertriginous areas (short-term) |
| **VI (Low)** | Mild | Desonide 0.05%, alclometasone dipropionate 0.05% | Face, eyelids, neck, skin folds, groin |
| **VII (Lowest)** | Least potent | Hydrocortisone 1%, 2.5% | Face, eyelids, diaper area, maintenance therapy |

### TCS Application Guidelines

- **Frequency:** Apply to active lesions 1-2 times daily; once daily is often sufficient for maintenance
- **Amount:** Use the fingertip unit (FTU) — the amount squeezed from a 5mm nozzle from fingertip to first crease (~0.5 g) covers approximately two adult palm-sized areas
- **Duration:** Acute flares typically require 7-14 days of daily therapy; taper to alternate-day or weekend therapy before discontinuing
- **Proactive therapy:** Apply low- to medium-potency TCS to previously affected areas 2 days per week to prevent flares — strongly supported by evidence
- **Safety considerations:** Avoid prolonged (>2-3 weeks continuous) use of Class I-III agents on face, groin, or skin folds; monitor for skin atrophy, striae, and telangiectasia
- **TCS phobia:** Common among patients and caregivers; education on safe use improves adherence and outcomes

## 1.4 Topical Calcineurin Inhibitors

Tacrolimus ointment and pimecrolimus cream represent non-steroidal anti-inflammatory alternatives for AD, particularly valuable for sensitive areas and long-term maintenance. [^4^]

| Agent | Formulation | Age Approval | Key Advantages |
|-------|------------|--------------|---------------|
| **Tacrolimus** | Ointment 0.03%, 0.1% | 0.03%: >=2 years; 0.1%: >=16 years | No skin atrophy; safe for face, eyelids, neck, skin folds; effective for moderate-severe AD |
| **Pimecrolimus** | Cream 1% | >=2 years | Lower burning sensation than tacrolimus; good for mild-moderate AD and maintenance |

**Mechanism:** Inhibit calcineurin, blocking T-cell activation and inflammatory cytokine (IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IFN-gamma) release.

**Key considerations:**
- **Black box warning:** Theoretical concern for malignancy (skin cancer, lymphoma) based on animal data and systemic immunosuppression theory; large-scale human studies and post-marketing surveillance have not confirmed this risk
- **Common adverse effects:** Transient burning, stinging, or warmth at application site (typically resolves within 1 week of continued use)
- **Application:** Can be used as steroid-sparing agents for maintenance (2-3x weekly on previously affected areas)
- **Photoprotection:** Apply at nighttime for areas with sun exposure; use sunscreen during the day

## 1.5 Biologics for Atopic Dermatitis

The 2024 AAD Guidelines strongly recommend biologics for moderate-to-severe AD. [^2^] [^3^] [^5^]

### Dupilumab (Dupixent)

| Parameter | Details |
|-----------|---------|
| **Target** | IL-4 receptor alpha (blocks both IL-4 and IL-13) |
| **Dosing (adults/adolescents >=12 years)** | 600 mg SC loading, then 300 mg every 2 weeks |
| **Dosing (children 6-11 years)** | 300 mg every 4 weeks (weight-based for some subgroups) |
| **FDA approval** | >=6 months for AD |
| **Efficacy** | SOLO1, SOLO2, CHRONOS trials: 48% EASI-75 at week 16 vs. 13% placebo; long-term data show 90.9% EASI-75 at 4 years |

**Key adverse effects:**
- Conjunctivitis/ocular surface disease (10-20% — most common; typically mild, responds to ophthalmic lubricants)
- Injection site reactions
- Upper respiratory infections
- Paradoxical head and neck erythema (distinct from AD; responds to topical TCS)

**Monitoring:** No laboratory monitoring required — a significant practical advantage.

### Tralokinumab (Adbry)

| Parameter | Details |
|-----------|---------|
| **Target** | IL-13 |
| **Dosing** | 600 mg SC loading, then 300 mg Q2W (may extend to Q4W in responders) |
| **FDA approval** | >=12 years for AD |
| **Efficacy** | ECZTRA trials: EASI-75 in 25-33% (monotherapy) vs. 11-13% placebo; 56% with concomitant TCS |

**Key adverse effects:** Upper respiratory infections, conjunctivitis (less frequent than dupilumab), injection site reactions.

### Lebrikizumab

| Parameter | Details |
|-----------|---------|
| **Target** | IL-13 (high affinity, small molecular size) |
| **Dosing** | 500 mg SC loading (two 250 mg injections), then 250 mg Q2W or Q4W maintenance |
| **FDA approval** | Under review |
| **Efficacy** | Demonstrated efficacy with both Q2W and Q4W dosing after 16-week induction |

**Comparative note:** Dupilumab blocks both IL-4 and IL-13; tralokinumab and lebrikizumab selectively target IL-13 alone. Head-to-head trials between these agents are limited.

## 1.6 JAK Inhibitors for Atopic Dermatitis

JAK inhibitors represent the most rapidly effective systemic therapy for AD but carry significant safety considerations. All oral JAK inhibitors carry a **black box warning** for serious heart-related events, cancer, blood clots, and death based on the tofacitinib ORAL Surveillance study in rheumatoid arthritis patients. They are **not first-line systemic therapy** and should be used with caution in patients with cardiovascular risk factors or malignancy history. [^6^]

| Agent | Route | Selectivity | Dosing (Adults) | Key Adverse Effects |
|-------|-------|-------------|-----------------|-------------------|
| **Upadacitinib** (Rinvoq) | Oral | JAK1 selective | 15 mg daily (may increase to 30 mg) | Acne, URI, herpes zoster, CPK elevation |
| **Abrocitinib** (Cibinqo) | Oral | JAK1 selective | 100-200 mg daily | Nausea, headache, acne, herpes infections |
| **Baricitinib** (Olumiant) | Oral | JAK1/JAK2 | 2-4 mg daily | Increased LDL, URI, headache |
| **Ruxolitinib** (Opzelura) | Topical | JAK1/JAK2 | 1.5% cream BID | Application site reactions, headache |

### Monitoring Requirements

| Timepoint | Assessments |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Baseline** | CBC with differential, liver enzymes, hepatitis B/C screening, TB screening (IGRA preferred), lipid panel, pregnancy test |
| **Follow-up** | Lipids 4-8 weeks after initiation; ongoing CBC, liver function every 3 months; clinical monitoring for infection, thrombosis symptoms |
| **Vaccination** | Shingles vaccine (Shingrix) recommended before starting, especially in older patients; all live vaccines should be completed prior to initiation |

### Efficacy Comparison

Upadacitinib 15 mg and abrocitinib 200 mg showed numerically higher response rates than dupilumab in head-to-head trials. In the **Heads Up study**, abrocitinib 200 mg demonstrated faster itch relief (PP-NRS4 at week 2: 48.2% vs. 25.5%) and higher EASI-90 at week 4 (28.5% vs. 14.6%) compared to dupilumab. Baricitinib showed slightly less EASI reduction compared with dupilumab at 16 weeks. [^5^]

## 1.7 General Management Principles

### Skin Barrier Maintenance (Foundation of Therapy)

- **Daily liberal emollient use:** Apply moisturizer within 3 minutes of bathing to trap moisture; ointments (petroleum jelly, Aquaphor) most effective; creams preferred over lotions
- **Bathing:** Lukewarm baths or showers (avoid hot water); colloidal oatmeal baths may soothe acute flares; bleach baths (1/4-1/2 cup household bleach in full bathtub) 2x weekly for patients with recurrent *S. aureus* infection
- **Product selection:** Fragrance-free, hypoallergenic products; avoid sodium lauryl sulfate; pH-balanced cleansers preferred
- **Wet wrap therapy:** Apply moisturizer and/or TCS, then cover with damp gauze/cotton and dry layer — highly effective for acute flares

### Trigger Avoidance

| Category | Common Triggers | Management |
|----------|----------------|------------|
| **Irritants** | Harsh soaps, detergents, wool, fragrances, chemicals | Switch to mild, fragrance-free products; avoid wool clothing |
| **Allergens** | Dust mites, pollen, pet dander, certain foods | Consider environmental controls; patch testing for persistent/recalcitrant AD |
| **Environmental** | Temperature extremes, low humidity, sweating | Humidifier in dry climates; manage sweating; gradual temperature changes |
| **Psychological** | Stress, anxiety, poor sleep | Stress management; sleep hygiene; consider mental health referral |
| **Infections** | *S. aureus*, *S. pyogenes*, HSV, molluscum | Treat secondary infections promptly; consider decolonization |

### Stepwise Treatment Approach

| Severity | Initial Treatment | Escalation |
|----------|------------------|------------|
| **Mild AD** | Topical emollients + low-medium potency TCS or TCI as needed for flares; proactive maintenance | Add crisaborole 2% ointment or topical ruxolitinib 1.5% cream |
| **Moderate AD** | Proactive TCS/TCI maintenance + phototherapy (NB-UVB); consider topical ruxolitinib | Add biologic (dupilumab or tralokinumab) |
| **Moderate-Severe AD** | Biologics (dupilumab, tralokinumab) or oral JAK inhibitors | Switch biologic class; add TCS; consider cyclosporine |
| **Severe/Refractory** | Oral JAK inhibitors; cyclosporine (short-term bridge); methotrexate; azathioprine; mycophenolate | Combination therapy; referral to specialty center |

## 1.8 Special Populations

### Pediatrics
- Mild-moderate disease: Low- to mid-potency TCS as first-line; tacrolimus 0.03% approved for >=2 years
- Dupilumab approved for >=6 months (youngest biologic indication in dermatology)
- Upadacitinib approved for >=12 years (>=40 kg)
- Oral JAK inhibitors: Generally avoid in very young children when possible; use only in severe, refractory cases
- Bleach baths may reduce *S. aureus* colonization and infection frequency

### Pregnancy
- Topical TCS (low-mid potency) generally safe; avoid super-high potency and prolonged use
- Tacrolimus/pimecrolimus: Generally considered safe (Category C; minimal systemic absorption)
- Systemic therapies: Avoid methotrexate, mycophenolate; hydroxychloroquine safe; limited data on biologics (case series suggest dupilumab safety)
- Emollient use is safe and important throughout pregnancy

### Geriatrics
- Skin atrophy risk increases with age — use lower potency TCS for shorter durations
- Increased risk of systemic absorption from barrier dysfunction
- Consider calcineurin inhibitors as steroid-sparing agents
- Monitor for medication interactions with systemic therapies

---

## 1.9 When to Seek Emergency Care

- Sudden onset of painful vesicles or punched-out erosions with fever (possible eczema herpeticum)
- Rapidly spreading redness with warmth, pain, and fever (possible cellulitis)
- Widespread skin involvement with fever, chills, or feeling ill
- Eye involvement with pain, redness, or vision changes
- Severe flare unresponsive to prescribed treatment

# Chapter 2: Psoriasis

## 2.1 Overview

Psoriasis is a chronic, immune-mediated inflammatory skin disease affecting approximately 2-3% of the global population. It is driven by T-cell-mediated inflammation with key cytokines including TNF-alpha, IL-17, IL-23, and IL-12. Beyond skin manifestations, psoriasis carries a substantial comorbidity burden including psoriatic arthritis (PsA), metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular disease, depression, and inflammatory bowel disease. Recognition and management of these comorbidities are integral to comprehensive psoriasis care. [^7^]

### Clinical Subtypes

| Subtype | Frequency | Key Features |
|---------|-----------|-------------|
| **Plaque psoriasis** | 80-90% of cases | Well-demarcated erythematous plaques with silvery scale; extensor surfaces (elbows, knees), scalp, trunk, lumbosacral area |
| **Guttate psoriasis** | ~10% | Small, drop-like papules; often post-streptococcal infection; trunk and proximal extremities; often self-limited |
| **Pustular psoriasis** | Uncommon | Sterile pustules on erythematous base; generalized pustular psoriasis (GPP) is a **dermatologic emergency** with fever, leukocytosis, and potential organ failure |
| **Erythrodermic psoriasis** | Rare | Generalized erythema covering >90% BSA; thermoregulatory compromise; high-output cardiac failure risk; **emergency** |
| **Inverse psoriasis** | Common | Smooth, shiny, well-demarcated erythema in intertriginous areas (axillae, groin, inframammary); minimal scale due to moisture |

### Disease Severity Assessment

The **Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI)** is the standard outcome measure, combining erythema, induration, scale, and BSA involvement. Treatment targets typically aim for PASI <=1 or BSA <=1%. Key benchmarks include PASI 75 (75% improvement), PASI 90, and PASI 100 (complete clearance).

Disease severity classification: Mild = BSA <3%; Moderate = BSA 3-10%; Severe = BSA >10%. Quality of life impact (DLQI score) also guides treatment decisions regardless of BSA. [^8^]

---

## 2.2 Red Flags / Emergency Features

> **ERYTHRODERMIC PSORIASIS**
> Generalized erythema with exfoliation, fever, and systemic toxicity. Can cause hypothermia, high-output cardiac failure, and electrolyte imbalances. Requires **hospitalization** for supportive care and systemic treatment.

> **GENERALIZED PUSTULAR PSORIASIS (GPP)**
> Widespread sterile pustules on erythematous skin with fever, leukocytosis, and potential multi-organ involvement. Associated with IL-36RN mutations in some patients. Requires **urgent hospitalization** and systemic therapy (cyclosporine, infliximab, or acitretin).

> **Psoriatic Arthritis Flare**
> Rapidly progressive joint symptoms, dactylitis, or enthesitis warrant urgent rheumatology referral.

## 2.3 Topical Therapy

First-line for mild disease and as adjunct to systemic therapy for moderate-severe disease. [^8^]

| Agent | Mechanism | Key Points |
|-------|-----------|------------|
| **Vitamin D analogs** (calcipotriene 0.005%, calcitriol) | Inhibit keratinocyte proliferation, modulate immune response | First-line monotherapy or in combination with TCS; excellent safety profile for long-term use |
| **Calcipotriene + betamethasone dipropionate** (Taclonex) | Combined anti-proliferative and anti-inflammatory | Highly effective fixed-dose combination; once-daily application; limited to 4 weeks continuous use |
| **Topical corticosteroids** | Anti-inflammatory | By potency class; limited by skin atrophy on long-term use; face/folds use Class VI-VII only |
| **Tazarotene** (Tazorac 0.05%, 0.1%) | Topical retinoid; modulates keratinocyte proliferation | May cause irritation; apply in evening; use in combination with TCS to reduce irritation |
| **Roflumilast cream 0.3%** (Zoryve) | PDE4 inhibitor | FDA-approved for plaque psoriasis including intertriginous areas; steroid-sparing; once daily |
| **Tapinarof cream 1%** (Vtama) | Aryl hydrocarbon receptor agonist | Steroid-free; once daily; remittive potential (durable response after discontinuation) |

### Topical Treatment Tips

- Combination therapy (vitamin D analog + TCS) is more effective than either alone
- Apply TCS in the morning and vitamin D analog in the evening to optimize efficacy
- Roflumilast and tapinarof offer steroid-free options for sensitive areas and maintenance
- Scalp psoriasis: Medicated shampoos (coal tar, salicylic acid) plus topical solutions/foams (clobetasol propionate 0.05% shampoo/solution, calcipotriene solution)

| Agent | Dosing | Monitoring | Key Considerations |
|-------|--------|------------|-------------------|
| **Methotrexate** | 10-25 mg weekly (oral, SC, IM) | CBC, LFTs, creatinine every 2-4 weeks initially, then q8-12 weeks | First-line systemic; folic acid 1-5 mg daily (except MTX day); teratogenic; avoid in pregnancy; caution with liver disease, alcohol use |

## 2.6 Biologic Therapy

Biologics have transformed psoriasis management. All require TB screening before initiation, hepatitis B screening, and ongoing infection surveillance. Live vaccines are contraindicated during therapy. [^9^]

### TNF-alpha Inhibitors

| Agent | Dosing | Administration | Efficacy (PASI 75) |
|-------|--------|---------------|-------------------|
| **Adalimumab** (Humira) | 80 mg loading, then 40 mg Q2W | SC injection | 70-80% |
| **Etanercept** (Enbrel) | 50 mg QW or 25 mg BIW | SC injection | 50-60% |
| **Infliximab** (Remicade) | 5 mg/kg at weeks 0, 2, 6, then Q8W | IV infusion | 75-80% |
| **Certolizumab pegol** (Cimzia) | 400 mg loading (weeks 0, 2, 4), then 200 mg Q2W or 400 mg Q4W | SC injection | 70-80% |

**Key adverse effects:** Increased infection risk (including TB reactivation), injection/infusion reactions, new/worsening heart failure (avoid in NYHA III-IV), demyelinating disease (contraindicated in MS), lymphoma (small theoretical increased risk). TNF inhibitors are preferred for patients with concomitant PsA or IBD.

### IL-17 Inhibitors

| Agent | Dosing | Administration | Efficacy (PASI 90) |
|-------|--------|---------------|-------------------|
| **Secukinumab** (Cosentyx) | 300 mg at weeks 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, then Q4W | SC injection | 70-80% |
| **Ixekizumab** (Taltz) | 160 mg loading, then 80 mg Q2W (may extend to Q4W) | SC injection | 70-80% |
| **Brodalumab** (Siliq) | 210 mg at weeks 0, 1, 2, then Q2W | SC injection | 70-80% |

**Key adverse effects:** IBD exacerbation (contraindicated in active Crohn's disease or ulcerative colitis); candida infections (especially mucocutaneous); neutropenia. **IL-17 inhibitors have the highest rates of skin clearance among all biologics**, achieving PASI 100 in 40-60% of patients. [^9^]

### IL-23 Inhibitors

| Agent | Dosing | Administration | Efficacy (PASI 90) |
|-------|--------|---------------|-------------------|
| **Guselkumab** (Tremfya) | 100 mg at weeks 0, 4, then Q8W | SC injection | 70-80% |
| **Risankizumab** (Skyrizi) | 150 mg at weeks 0, 4, then Q12W | SC injection | 85%+ |
| **Tildrakizumab** (Ilumya) | 100 mg at weeks 0, 4, then Q12W | SC injection | 60-70% |

**Key advantages:** Low immunogenicity; excellent long-term safety data; convenient dosing intervals; high efficacy. **IL-23 inhibitors are increasingly preferred as first-line biologics** due to excellent efficacy and favorable safety profile. [^9^]

### IL-12/23 Inhibitor

| Agent | Dosing | Administration | Efficacy (PASI 75) |
|-------|--------|---------------|-------------------|
| **Ustekinumab** (Stelara) | 45 mg (<=100 kg) or 90 mg (>100 kg) at weeks 0, 4, then Q12W | SC injection | 67-76% |

**Key advantages:** Generally well tolerated; fewer infections than TNF inhibitors; favorable pregnancy data. Good choice for patients with comorbid IBD.

### Treatment Selection Based on Comorbidities

| Comorbidity | Preferred Biologics | Avoid |
|------------|---------------------|-------|
| **Psoriatic arthritis** | TNF inhibitors, IL-17 inhibitors, IL-23 inhibitors, ustekinumab | — |
| **IBD (Crohn's/UC)** | TNF inhibitors, ustekinumab | IL-17 inhibitors |
| **Cardiovascular disease** | All appear relatively safe; some data suggest cardioprotective effects [^10^] | — |
| **Demyelinating disease** | IL-17, IL-23, ustekinumab | TNF inhibitors |
| **Pregnancy** | Limited data; certolizumab (minimal placental transfer) | Live vaccines during therapy |
| **Prior malignancy** | IL-23, IL-17 (lower immunosuppression) | TNF inhibitors (theoretical higher risk) |

## 2.7 Treatment Algorithm

| Severity | Treatment Approach |
|----------|-------------------|
| **Mild (BSA <3%)** | Topical therapy (vitamin D analogs, TCS, roflumilast, tapinarof); phototherapy for refractory cases |
| **Moderate (BSA 3-10%)** | Phototherapy (NB-UVB); systemic therapy if phototherapy inadequate or impractical; biologics if quality of life significantly impacted |
| **Severe (BSA >10%)** | Systemic therapy or biologics; preferred first-line biologics: IL-23 inhibitors or IL-17 inhibitors (highest efficacy); TNF inhibitors for patients with concomitant PsA or IBD |

## 2.8 Comorbidities

### Psoriatic Arthritis (PsA)
- Affects 20-30% of psoriasis patients; may precede, coincide with, or follow skin disease
- Screen all psoriasis patients annually for joint symptoms (PASE questionnaire): joint pain, swelling, morning stiffness, dactylitis, enthesitis, back pain
- Treatment with biologics that address both skin and joints (TNF inhibitors, IL-17 inhibitors, IL-23 inhibitors)
- Early rheumatology referral is essential to prevent joint damage

### Metabolic Syndrome and Cardiovascular Disease
- Psoriasis is an independent cardiovascular risk factor
- Increased risk of myocardial infarction, stroke, and cardiovascular mortality
- Screen for metabolic syndrome components (BMI, blood pressure, fasting glucose, lipids)
- Aggressive management of cardiovascular risk factors; weight loss for overweight/obese patients

### Mental Health
- Depression and anxiety prevalence 2-3x higher than general population
- Significant impact on quality of life; screen routinely
- Suicide risk may be elevated; assess mental health at each visit

### Other Comorbidities
- Inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis) risk increased
- Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease
- Type 2 diabetes mellitus
- Ocular disease (uveitis — especially with PsA)
- Small increased lymphoma risk

# Chapter 3: Acne Vulgaris

## 3.1 Overview

Acne vulgaris is the most common skin condition in the United States, affecting nearly 50 million Americans annually. It affects 85% of adolescents ages 12-24 and can persist into adulthood, particularly in women (adult female acne). The psychosocial burden is significant, with associations to depression, anxiety, and reduced quality of life comparable to chronic diseases like asthma or epilepsy.

### Pathophysiology

Four key factors contribute to acne pathogenesis: [^11^] [^12^]

1. **Follicular hyperkeratinization:** Abnormal desquamation of follicular keratinocytes leads to microcomedone formation — the precursor lesion of all acne
2. **Increased sebum production:** Androgen-driven sebaceous gland hyperplasia; sebum provides substrate for bacterial growth
3. ***Cutibacterium acnes* colonization:** Formerly *Propionibacterium acnes*; triggers inflammatory responses through innate immune activation
4. **Inflammation:** Cytokine-mediated inflammation converts microcomedones to inflammatory lesions

### Classification by Severity

| Severity | Description | Lesion Types |
|----------|-------------|-------------|
| **Mild** | Limited involvement | Comedones (open/closed) with few inflammatory papules/pustules |
| **Moderate** | More widespread | Multiple inflammatory papules/pustules with comedones; may include a few nodules |
| **Severe (Nodulocystic)** | Extensive involvement | Nodules, cysts, significant scarring risk; substantial psychosocial impact |

---

## 3.2 Topical Therapy

The **2024 AAD Guidelines** provide 18 evidence-based recommendations and 5 good practice statements for acne management. [^11^] [^13^]

### Strongly Recommended Agents

| Agent | Mechanism | Key Points |
|-------|-----------|------------|
| **Benzoyl peroxide (BP)** 2.5-10% | Antimicrobial, comedolytic, anti-inflammatory | OTC; prevents antibiotic resistance; fabric bleaching; start with lower concentrations to minimize irritation |
| **Topical retinoids** — adapalene, tretinoin, tazarotene, trifarotene | Normalize follicular keratinization, anti-inflammatory | **Cornerstone of acne treatment**; first-line for comedonal acne; **maintenance therapy is essential**; adapalene 0.1% now OTC in some regions |
| **Topical antibiotics** — clindamycin, erythromycin | Antibacterial, anti-inflammatory | **NEVER as monotherapy** — must combine with BP to prevent resistance; efficacy declining due to resistance |
| **Fixed-dose combinations** (BP + retinoid, BP + antibiotic, retinoid + antibiotic) | Multimodal | Recommended for improved adherence, convenience, and efficacy |

### Conditionally Recommended Agents

| Agent | Mechanism | Notes |
|-------|-----------|-------|
| **Clascoterone 1% cream** (Winlevi) | Androgen receptor inhibitor | First new mechanism for acne in decades; blocks androgen receptors in sebaceous glands; BID application; safe in females and males |
| **Azelaic acid 15-20%** | Antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, anti-keratinizing | Safe in pregnancy; beneficial for post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation; particularly useful in darker skin tones |
| **Salicylic acid** 0.5-2% | Keratolytic, comedolytic | OTC; limited efficacy as monotherapy; good for maintenance |
| **Dapsone 5-7.5% gel** | Anti-inflammatory | More effective in female patients; may cause temporary orange-brown discoloration when combined with BP |

### Topical Treatment Strategy

1. **Initial therapy:** Topical retinoid + BP (with or without topical antibiotic)
2. **Maintenance:** Continue topical retinoid after clearance — essential for preventing recurrence
3. **Retinoid tolerability:** Start with lower concentration, apply to dry skin, use every other night initially, moisturizer can be applied before or after

## 3.3 Systemic Therapy

### Systemic Antibiotics

| Agent | Dosing | Duration | Notes |
|-------|--------|----------|-------|
| **Doxycycline** | 50-100 mg daily-BID | Shortest possible; typically <=3-4 months | Strongly recommended; photosensitivity; esophagitis risk — take with full glass of water; avoid in pregnancy and children <8 years |
| **Minocycline** | 50-100 mg BID | <=3-4 months | Conditionally recommended; vestibular side effects; rare DRESS syndrome; skin discoloration |
| **Sarecycline** | Weight-based: 33-54 kg: 60 mg; 55-85 kg: 100 mg; >85 kg: 150 mg daily | <=3-4 months | Narrow-spectrum tetracycline; approved >=9 years; fewer GI side effects; lower resistance potential |

**Critical principles:**
- **Always combine with BP and topical retinoid** — never use antibiotics as monotherapy
- **Limit duration to the shortest possible**, typically no more than 3-4 months
- Antibiotic resistance in *C. acnes* is a growing global concern
- After discontinuation, continue topical retinoid for maintenance

### Hormonal Therapy (for Appropriate Female Patients)

| Agent | Dosing | Efficacy | Key Considerations |
|-------|--------|----------|-------------------|
| **Combined oral contraceptives (COCs)** | Various formulations | Conditionally recommended | Beneficial for women with hormonal pattern acne (jawline, premenstrual flares); requires >=3-6 months for full effect |
| **Spironolactone** | 50-200 mg daily | Conditionally recommended | Anti-androgen; highly effective for adult female acne; potassium monitoring generally not needed in healthy patients; consider checking if on ACE inhibitors, ARBs, or with renal disease; avoid in pregnancy |

## 3.4 Isotretinoin (13-cis Retinoic Acid)

### Indications (Strongly Recommended by AAD 2024)

- Severe acne (nodulocystic, conglobate)
- Acne causing or at risk of causing scarring
- Acne causing significant psychosocial burden
- Acne failing standard treatment with oral antibiotics and topical therapy
- Relapsing acne after multiple courses of oral antibiotics

### Dosing

| Regimen | Dose | Duration | Cumulative Target |
|---------|------|----------|-------------------|
| **Standard** | 0.5-1 mg/kg/day | 15-20 weeks | 120-150 mg/kg |
| **Low-dose** | 0.25-0.5 mg/kg/day | Longer | 120-150 mg/kg |

Low-dose regimens may have fewer side effects but may be less effective for severe disease. Daily dosing is preferred over intermittent regimens.

### iPLEDGE Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy (REMS)

iPLEDGE is a **mandatory** FDA risk management program for all isotretinoin prescribers, pharmacies, and patients: [^13^]

- **All prescribers** must be registered and certified
- **Female patients of childbearing potential:** Two forms of contraception required; monthly pregnancy tests required before each prescription; 7-day prescription window
- **All patients:** Monthly visits required; must complete comprehension questions
- **Pharmacies:** Must be registered; cannot dispense without iPLEDGE authorization
- **Pregnancy prevention:** Isotretinoin is **absolutely contraindicated in pregnancy** — causes severe teratogenicity (craniofacial, cardiac, CNS defects)

### Monitoring

| Parameter | Timing |
|-----------|--------|
| **Baseline** | LFTs, fasting lipid panel, CBC (optional in healthy patients), two negative pregnancy tests (females of childbearing potential) |
| **Follow-up** | LFTs and lipids at each monthly visit; pregnancy test monthly (females of childbearing potential) |
| **Psychiatric** | Screen for depression, anxiety, suicidal ideation at each visit — population-based studies have not identified increased risk, but individual monitoring is prudent |

### Key Adverse Effects

| System | Effect | Frequency | Management |
|--------|--------|-----------|------------|
| **Mucocutaneous** | Dry lips, dry skin, epistaxis | >90% | Lip balm, moisturizer, nasal saline spray |
| **Ocular** | Dry eyes, blepharitis | Common | Artificial tears, ophthalmic evaluation if severe |
| **Musculoskeletal** | Myalgias, arthralgias | 10-20% | Reduce dose; rare skeletal effects in children |
| **Hepatic** | Elevated transaminases | 5-15% | Usually mild; dose reduction if >3x ULN |
| **Metabolic** | Hypertriglyceridemia | 20-40% | Diet modification; consider fenofibrate if severe |
| **Other** | Photosensitivity, headache, pseudotumor cerebri (rare) | Variable | Sun protection; discontinue if severe headache with vision changes |

### Important Precautions

- **Avoid full-face dermabrasion, ablative laser treatments, and elective surgery within 6 months of isotretinoin use** due to impaired wound healing and keloid/scar risk [^13^]
- Do not donate blood during therapy and for 1 month after (teratogenic risk to pregnant recipients)
- Avoid vitamin A supplements (additive toxicity)
- Isotretinoin and IBD association remains controversial; large studies show no consistent causal link

## 3.5 Acne Scars

**Prevention:** Early, effective treatment of inflammatory acne is the best scar prevention strategy. Every inflammatory papule or pustule has potential to scar.

### Post-Inflammatory Changes

| Type | Description | Treatment |
|------|-------------|-----------|
| **Post-inflammatory erythema (PIE)** | Red/pink discoloration after acne lesion | Time, topical retinoids, azelaic acid, vascular lasers (PDL) |
| **Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH)** | Brown/dark discoloration, more common in darker skin tones | Time, topical retinoids, azelaic acid, vitamin C, hydroquinone, strict photoprotection |

### Atrophic Scars

| Scar Type | Description | Treatment Options |
|-----------|-------------|-------------------|
| **Ice pick** | Deep, narrow, sharply demarcated | Punch excision, TCA CROSS (trichloroacetic acid chemical reconstruction of skin scars) |
| **Boxcar** | Round/oval depressions with sharply demarcated edges | Microneedling, fractional laser, subcision, dermal fillers |
| **Rolling** | Broad, sloping edges, caused by tethering | Subcision, microneedling, fractional laser |

**Hypertrophic/keloid scars:** Intralesional corticosteroids (triamcinolone 10-20 mg/mL), silicone gel sheets, pressure therapy, laser.

**Important note:** Microneedling and laser procedures should be performed >6 months after isotretinoin completion to minimize scarring risk.

## 3.6 Special Populations

### Pregnancy
- **Safe:** Azelaic acid, BP (limited areas), erythromycin (topical), clindamycin (topical), glycolic acid
- **Contraindicated:** Tazarotene, topical retinoids (Category C; though systemic absorption minimal), oral tetracyclines, isotretinoin
- Use topical therapies other than retinoids as first-line; azelaic acid is particularly useful
- Acne often improves in the second trimester due to estrogen effects

### Pediatrics (Ages 9-12)
- Topical retinoids + BP are first-line
- Systemic antibiotics: Limited use; sarecycline approved for >=9 years; doxycycline avoid <8 years
- Isotretinoin for severe/recalcitrant cases with proper counseling
- Early effective treatment prevents long-term psychosocial impact

### Adult Female Acne
- Often hormonal pattern (jawline, chin, premenstrual flares)
- Consider spironolactone and/or combined oral contraceptives early
- Check for endocrine disorders (PCOS, late-onset congenital adrenal hyperplasia) if irregular menses, hirsutism present

---

## 3.7 When to Seek Medical Care

- Severe, painful nodules or cysts
- Signs of scarring
- Significant psychosocial distress
- Acne failing OTC treatment after 2-3 months
- Signs of androgen excess in females (irregular periods, hirsutism)
- Signs of infection (rapidly spreading redness, warmth, fever)

# Chapter 4: Skin Infections

## 4.1 Bacterial Skin Infections

### Impetigo

Impetigo is a highly contagious superficial bacterial skin infection, most common in children. [^14^]

| Feature | Non-Bullous Impetigo | Bullous Impetigo |
|---------|---------------------|------------------|
| **Pathogen** | *S. aureus*, Group A *Streptococcus* | *S. aureus* (exfoliative toxin-producing) |
| **Appearance** | Honey-colored crusts on erythematous base | Flaccid bullae that rupture leaving thin collarette of scale |
| **Distribution** | Face (around nose/mouth), extremities | Face, trunk, extremities, diaper area |
| **Treatment (localized)** | Mupirocin 2% ointment or retapamulin 1% ointment BID x 5 days | Same |
| **Treatment (extensive)** | Oral cephalexin, dicloxacillin x 7 days | Same |
| **MRSA suspected** | Doxycycline, clindamycin, or TMP-SMX x 7 days | Same |

**Key considerations:** Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis can follow impetigo caused by nephritogenic strains; good hygiene and contact precautions essential; treat household contacts if recurrent.

### Folliculitis

| Type | Pathogen/Trigger | Clinical Features | Treatment |
|------|-----------------|-------------------|-----------|
| **Bacterial** | *S. aureus* | Pustules at hair follicle openings | Topical mupirocin or clindamycin; oral antibiotics for extensive |
| **Hot tub** | *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* | Pruritic papulopustules on trunk after hot tub exposure; self-limited | Usually self-limited; ciprofloxacin for severe/prolonged |
| **Malassezia (Pityrosporum)** | *Malassezia* yeast | Itchy papules/pustules on trunk, upper arms; may worsen with antibiotics | Topical ketoconazole, selenium sulfide; oral itraconazole for extensive |
| **Candidal** | *Candida* | Pustules in intertriginous areas | Topical nystatin, azole antifungals |

### Cellulitis and Erysipelas

| Feature | Erysipelas | Cellulitis |
|---------|-----------|------------|
| **Level** | Superficial (upper dermis) | Deep dermis and subcutaneous tissue |
| **Appearance** | Well-demarcated, raised, bright red, warm | Poorly demarcated, flat, erythematous, warm |
| **Pathogen** | Group A *Streptococcus* | *S. aureus*, Group A *Streptococcus* |
| **Mild treatment** | Oral cephalexin, dicloxacillin, amoxicillin-clavulanate | Same |
| **Severe/inpatient** | IV cefazolin, ceftriaxone; add vancomycin if MRSA concern | Same |

**Always examine for tinea pedis as portal of entry** — treating interdigital dermatophyte infection reduces cellulitis recurrence. Recurrent cellulitis: Treat predisposing factors (tinea pedis, lymphedema, venous insufficiency); consider prophylactic penicillin VK or erythromycin.

### MRSA Skin and Soft Tissue Infections

Community-acquired MRSA (CA-MRSA) commonly presents as purulent skin infections, abscesses, and furuncles, often with the USA300 clone.

| Clinical Scenario | Treatment |
|-------------------|-----------|
| **Abscess** | Incision and drainage (primary treatment); antibiotics if associated cellulitis, systemic symptoms, or high-risk location |
| **Purulent cellulitis** | Oral TMP-SMX, doxycycline, or clindamycin (check local resistance patterns) |
| **Severe infection** | Vancomycin, daptomycin, or linezolid IV |
| **Recurrent infections** | Decolonization: Mupirocin to nares BID x 5 days, chlorhexidine washes; consider dilute bleach baths; treat household contacts |

## 4.2 Fungal Infections

### Dermatophyte Infections (Tinea)

| Type | Pathogen | Clinical Features | Treatment |
|------|----------|-------------------|-----------|
| **Tinea corporis** (ringworm) | *T. rubrum* (most common) | Annular, scaly, pruritic plaques with central clearing and raised borders | Topical terbinafine 1%, clotrimazole 1%, miconazole 2% BID x 2-4 weeks |
| **Tinea capitis** | *T. tonsurans* (US most common) | Scaly patches with alopecia, broken hairs, kerion | **Systemic antifungals required** (see below) |
| **Tinea pedis** (athlete's foot) | *T. rubrum*, *T. mentagrophytes* | Interdigital (maceration), moccasin (dry scaling), vesicular | Topical terbinafine 1%, butenafine 1% BID x 1-4 weeks; oral terbinafine for moccasin-type |
| **Tinea unguium** (onychomycosis) | *T. rubrum* | Thickened, discolored, brittle nails | Oral terbinafine (see below) |
| **Tinea cruris** (jock itch) | *T. rubrum*, *E. floccosum* | Erythematous plaques with sharp borders in groin, sparing scrotum | Topical antifungals x 2-4 weeks |

### Tinea Capitis — Systemic Treatment

| Agent | Pediatric Dosing | Duration | Notes |
|-------|-----------------|----------|-------|
| **Griseofulvin** (microsize) | 20-25 mg/kg/day | 6-8 weeks | Still preferred first-line in many pediatric guidelines; take with fatty food |
| **Griseofulvin** (ultramicrosize) | 10-15 mg/kg/day | 6-8 weeks | Better absorption |
| **Terbinafine** | <25 kg: 125 mg; 25-35 kg: 187.5 mg; >35 kg: 250 mg daily | 6 weeks | Higher cure rates for *T. tonsurans*; weight-based dosing |
| **Itraconazole** | 5 mg/kg/day | 2-4 weeks | Alternative; check drug interactions |

**Adjunctive therapy:** Selenium sulfide or ketoconazole shampoo 2-3x weekly reduces spore shedding and transmission. Treat household contacts with shampoo prophylaxis.

**Kerion:** Boggy, purulent, tender swelling; may add oral corticosteroids short-term; obtain culture before treatment if possible.

### Onychomycosis — Systemic Treatment

| Agent | Dosing | Duration | Cure Rates |
|-------|--------|----------|------------|
| **Oral terbinafine** | 250 mg daily | 12 weeks (toenails), 6 weeks (fingernails) | 70-80% (highest) |
| **Oral itraconazole** (pulse) | 200 mg BID x 1 week/month | 3-4 months (toenails) | 50-60% |
| **Efinaconazole 10% topical** | Daily application | 48 weeks | 15-20% complete cure |
| **Tavaborole 5% topical** | Daily application | 48 weeks | 6-9% complete cure |

**Pre-treatment:** Check baseline LFTs; monitor for drug interactions (terbinafine is a CYP2D6 inhibitor; itraconazole has extensive CYP3A4 interactions).

### Candidiasis

| Type | Clinical Features | Treatment |
|------|-------------------|-----------|
| **Cutaneous candidiasis** | Beefy red erythema in intertriginous areas with satellite pustules | Topical nystatin, clotrimazole, miconazole; keep area dry; absorbent powder |
| **Oral candidiasis (thrush)** | White plaques on oral mucosa that scrape off leaving erythematous base | Nystatin suspension, clotrimazole troches, oral fluconazole |
| **Chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis** | Persistent, recurrent candidal infections | Consider immunodeficiency workup (especially T-cell defects) |

## 4.3 Viral Infections

### Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV)

| Type | Presentation | Treatment |
|------|-------------|-----------|
| **HSV-1** | Oral herpes (cold sores); herpetic gingivostomatitis; facial/ocular infections | Acyclovir 400 mg TID x 7-10 days; valacyclovir 1 g BID x 7-10 days |
| **HSV-2** | Genital herpes; recurrent painful vesicles/ulcers | Same as above |
| **Recurrent (episodic)** | Prodrome + recurrent lesions | Valacyclovir 500 mg BID x 3 days or 2 g BID x 1 day |
| **Suppressive therapy** | Frequent recurrences | Valacyclovir 500-1000 mg daily; acyclovir 400 mg BID |

> **ECZEMA HERPETICUM — EMERGENCY**
> HSV superinfection in patients with atopic dermatitis. Presents with monomorphic vesicles, punched-out erosions, fever, and malaise. Risk of keratoconjunctivitis and disseminated infection. **Requires immediate IV acyclovir.**

### Varicella Zoster Virus (VZV)

| Presentation | Key Features | Treatment |
|-------------|-------------|-----------|
| **Varicella (chickenpox)** | Primary infection; vesicles in various stages; highly contagious | Supportive; acyclovir for high-risk patients (>12 years, immunocompromised, chronic skin/lung disease) |
| **Herpes zoster (shingles)** | Dermatomal vesicular eruption; pain may precede rash | Valacyclovir 1 g TID x 7 days; famciclovir 500 mg TID x 7 days; acyclovir 800 mg 5x daily x 7-10 days |

**Shingles treatment principles:**
- Start antiviral therapy **within 72 hours of rash onset** for maximum benefit
- **Ophthalmic involvement (V1):** Urgent ophthalmology referral — risk of vision loss
- **Ramsay Hunt syndrome:** Facial palsy + auricular vesicles — urgent ENT/neurology referral
- **Post-herpetic neuralgia prevention:** Early antiviral treatment; gabapentin/pregabalin for established PHN
- **Shingrix vaccine:** Recombinant zoster vaccine recommended for all adults >=50 years (two-dose series, 2-6 months apart) and immunocompromised adults >=19 years

### Molluscum Contagiosum

- **Pathogen:** Molluscum contagiosum virus (poxvirus)
- **Clinical:** 2-5 mm flesh-colored, dome-shaped, pearly papules with central umbilication
- **Natural history:** Usually self-limited (6-12 months); may persist up to 4 years
- **Treatment (for cosmetic reasons or to prevent spread):**
  - Physical: Curettage, cryotherapy, cantharidin ("blistering beetle" extract)
  - Topical: Imiquimod 5% cream (off-label), tretinoin cream
  - In immunocompromised: May be extensive and persistent; treat aggressively

## 4.4 Infestations

### Scabies

Caused by *Sarcoptes scabiei var. hominis* mite. Transmission occurs through prolonged skin-to-skin contact. [^15^] [^16^]

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Symptoms** | Intense nocturnal pruritus (hallmark) |
| **Distribution** | Web spaces of fingers, wrists, axillae, genitalia, periumbilical area, nipples |
| **Primary lesions** | Burrows (linear, grayish-white thread-like lesions); papules; vesicles |
| **Secondary lesions** | Excoriations, crusting, secondary bacterial infection |

**Classic scabies treatment:**

| Agent | Application | Repeat | Notes |
|-------|------------|--------|-------|
| **Permethrin 5% cream** | Neck down (include under nails, interdigital spaces), leave 8-14 hours | Repeat in 7 days | First-line; safe in pregnancy (>2nd trimester), children >2 months |
| **Oral ivermectin** | 200 mcg/kg PO | Repeat in 7-14 days | Alternative; not first-line in pregnancy; highly effective |
| **Benzyl benzoate 25%** | Topical | Repeat in 7 days | Alternative; more irritating |

**Critical management points:**
- **All close contacts must be treated simultaneously** — even if asymptomatic
- **Bedding and clothing:** Wash in hot water (>=50 degrees C) and dry on high heat for >=10 minutes, OR seal in plastic bag for >=72 hours
- Itching may persist for 2-4 weeks after successful treatment (post-scabietic pruritus) — this is not treatment failure

> **CRUSTED (NORWEGIAN) SCABIES — HIGHLY CONTAGIOUS**
> Occurs in immunocompromised, elderly, and institutionalized patients. Presents with thick crusts containing millions of mites. Highly contagious with minimal contact. Requires combination therapy: **topical permethrin daily + oral ivermectin** on days 1, 2, 8, 9, 15 (and possibly 22, 29). Contact precautions and outbreak management essential.

### Pediculosis (Lice)

#### Head Lice (*Pediculus humanus capitis*)

| Treatment | Application | Notes |
|-----------|------------|-------|
| **Permethrin 1% lotion** | 10 minutes, then rinse; repeat in 7-10 days | First-line; resistance widespread in some regions |
| **Pyrethrins with piperonyl butoxide** | 10 minutes; repeat in 7-10 days | OTC; resistance increasing |
| **Topical ivermectin 0.5% lotion** | Single application to dry hair, rinse after 10 minutes | Single-dose efficacy; prescription |
| **Oral ivermectin** | 200 mcg/kg; repeat in 7-10 days | Alternative; off-label |
| **Spinosad 0.9% suspension** | 10 minutes; repeat in 7 days if live lice | Effective against permethrin-resistant lice |
| **Malathion 0.5% lotion** | 8-12 hours; repeat in 7 days | Flammable — avoid hair dryers; highly effective |

**Non-pharmacologic:** Wet combing with fine-tooth nit comb every 3-4 days for 2 weeks. Essential adjunct to all chemical treatments.

#### Pubic Lice (*Phthirus pubis*)
- Permethrin 1% cream rinse x 10 minutes; repeat in 7-10 days
- **Screen for other STIs:** HIV, syphilis, hepatitis B/C, gonorrhea, chlamydia
- Treat sexual partners from prior 30 days

#### Body Lice (*Pediculus humanus corporis*)
- Improved hygiene; wash clothing and bedding in hot water
- Topical permethrin if needed
- **Can transmit epidemic typhus, trench fever, and relapsing fever** — important in homeless populations and humanitarian crises

## 4.5 When to Seek Emergency Care

- Eczema herpeticum (widespread painful vesicles in AD patient with fever)
- Rapidly spreading skin infection with fever or systemic symptoms
- Facial cellulitis, especially periorbital
- Necrotizing fasciitis signs (severe pain disproportionate to appearance, rapidly spreading erythema, crepitus, bullae, systemic toxicity)
- Shingles involving the eye (pain/lesions on nose tip — Hutchinson sign)

# Chapter 5: Skin Cancer

## 5.1 Melanoma

### Overview

Melanoma is the most serious form of skin cancer, with increasing incidence globally. Early detection is the single most important factor determining prognosis — 5-year survival for localized melanoma is >99%, but drops to approximately 27% for distant metastatic disease. [^18^] Risk factors include fair skin, UV exposure (intermittent intense exposure > chronic), history of blistering sunburns, multiple atypical nevi, family history of melanoma, and genetic predisposition (CDKN2A mutations).

### ABCDE Criteria for Early Detection

| Letter | Feature | Description |
|--------|---------|-------------|
| **A** | **A**symmetry | One half of the lesion does not match the other |
| **B** | **B**order irregularity | Ragged, notched, blurred, or poorly defined edges |
| **C** | **C**olor variation | Multiple colors (brown, black, tan, red, white, blue) or uneven distribution |
| **D** | **D**iameter | >6 mm (pencil eraser) — though melanomas can be smaller |
| **E** | **E**volving | Changing in size, shape, color, elevation; new symptoms (itching, bleeding) |

**Additional warning signs:**
- **"Ugly duckling" sign:** A lesion that looks different from the patient's other moles
- **EFG for nodular melanoma:** **E**levation, **F**irmness, **G**rowing (nodular melanomas often lack classic ABCDE features)
- New lesion in an adult; non-healing lesion; spontaneous bleeding

### Diagnosis

- **Full-thickness biopsy** with 1-3 mm clinical margins is preferred (punch or excisional); shave biopsy is acceptable only if full-thickness
- **Dermoscopy** improves diagnostic accuracy and should be used by trained clinicians
- **Sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB):** Indicated for melanomas >=0.8 mm Breslow thickness (or <0.8 mm with ulceration or other high-risk features)
- **Molecular testing:** BRAF V600E mutation testing for Stage III+ (guides targeted therapy); NRAS, c-KIT testing

### Staging (AJCC 8th Edition, 2018) [^18^]

#### T Classification (Tumor Thickness)

| Stage | Breslow Thickness | Ulceration Status |
|-------|-------------------|-------------------|
| T1 | <=0.8 mm | Without ulceration (T1a) |
| T1 | <=0.8 mm | With ulceration (T1b) |
| T2 | 0.8-1.0 mm | Without (T2a) / With (T2b) ulceration |
| T3 | 1.01-2.0 mm | Without (T3a) / With (T3b) ulceration |
| T4 | 2.01-4.0 mm | Without (T4a) / With (T4b) ulceration |
| T5 | >4.0 mm | Without (T5a) / With (T5b) ulceration |

#### Prognostic Stage Groups and Survival

| Stage | Features | 5-Year Survival |
|-------|----------|-----------------|
| **0** | Melanoma in situ | ~100% |
| **IA** | T1a N0 M0 | ~97% |
| **IB-IIA** | T1b-T2b N0 M0 | 92-95% |
| **IIB-IIC** | T3a-T4b N0 M0 | 77-87% |
| **III** | Node-positive (N1-N3) | 40-78% |
| **IV** | Distant metastasis (M1) | 15-27% |

**Ulceration** of the primary tumor is a major adverse prognostic factor that upstages the T category. **Mitotic rate** and **tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes** also provide prognostic information.

### Treatment by Stage

| Stage | Treatment |
|-------|-----------|
| **0 (in situ)** | Wide local excision with 0.5-1 cm margins; sentinel node biopsy not indicated |
| **I-II** | Wide local excision (1-2 cm margins depending on thickness); SLNB for >=T1b/T2; adjuvant pembrolizumab or nivolumab for high-risk Stage IIB-IIC |
| **III (node-positive)** | Lymphadenectomy if clinically positive nodes; adjuvant immunotherapy (pembrolizumab, nivolumab, dabrafenib+trametinib for BRAF V600E+); consider radiation for high-risk nodal disease |
| **IV (metastatic)** | Systemic therapy: checkpoint inhibitors first-line (pembrolizumab, nivolumab, nivolumab+ipilimumab); targeted therapy (dabrafenib+trametinib, encorafenib+binimetinib for BRAF-mutant); clinical trials |

**Adjuvant immunotherapy:** Pembrolizumab and nivolumab significantly improve recurrence-free survival and overall survival in Stage IIB-III melanoma and are now standard of care.

## 5.2 Basal Cell Carcinoma (BCC)

- **Most common skin cancer** — rarely metastasizes but can be locally destructive
- **Risk factors:** UV exposure (cumulative), fair skin, ionizing radiation, immunosuppression, Gorlin syndrome (nevoid BCC syndrome)
- **Subtypes:** Nodular (most common, pearly papule with telangiectasias and rolled border), superficial (erythematous scaly patch), morpheaform/sclerosing (more aggressive, scar-like), micronodular, infiltrative

### Treatment (NCCN Guidelines 2024) [^19^]

| Risk Category | Treatment Options |
|--------------|-------------------|
| **Low-risk** (small, well-defined, primary, non-aggressive histology) | Curettage and electrodesiccation (non-hair-bearing areas); standard excision (4 mm margins); Mohs micrographic surgery; topical therapies (imiquimod 5%, 5-FU, photodynamic therapy) for superficial BCC |
| **High-risk** (H-zone of face, recurrent, aggressive histology, >1 cm trunk/extremities) | **Mohs micrographic surgery** (tissue-sparing, highest cure rates); standard excision with wider margins; radiation for non-surgical candidates |
| **Locally advanced/metastatic** | Hedgehog pathway inhibitors (vismodegib, sonidegib); cemiplimab (PD-1 inhibitor) |

**Follow-up:** Skin examination every 6-12 months for life; sun protection counseling; 30-50% risk of developing another BCC within 5 years.

---

## 5.3 Squamous Cell Carcinoma (SCC)

- **Second most common skin cancer** — metastatic potential higher than BCC
- **Risk factors:** UV exposure (cumulative), fair skin, HPV (anogenital), immunosuppression (organ transplant recipients have 65-100x increased risk), chronic wounds, arsenic exposure, radiation
- **Subtypes:** Well-differentiated, moderately differentiated, poorly differentiated, desmoplastic (high risk), adenosquamous, spindle cell

### Treatment

| Risk Category | Treatment |
|--------------|-----------|
| **Low-risk** (small, well-differentiated, primary) | Standard excision (4-6 mm margins); ED&C; Mohs for appropriate sites |
| **High-risk** (recurrent, poorly differentiated, perineural invasion, >2 cm, immunosuppressed) | **Mohs micrographic surgery** (preferred); adjuvant radiation for perineural invasion, large tumors, incomplete excision |
| **Metastatic** | Cemiplimab (PD-1 inhibitor); multidisciplinary care |

**Follow-up:** Every 3-12 months initially; every 6-12 months long-term; higher recurrence and metastasis risk than BCC.

---

## 5.4 Actinic Keratosis (AK)

- **Premalignant lesions** with potential to progress to invasive SCC (estimated 0.025-16% per lesion per year)
- **Field cancerization concept:** Multiple AKs indicate photodamaged skin at increased risk for SCC
- **Clinical:** Rough, scaly patches on sun-exposed skin (face, scalp, dorsal hands, forearms, ears)

### Treatment Options

| Approach | Methods | Best For |
|----------|---------|----------|
| **Lesion-directed** | Cryotherapy, curettage, shave excision | Individual, thicker, suspicious lesions |
| **Field-directed** | 5-fluorouracil cream, imiquimod cream, diclofenac 3% gel, photodynamic therapy, ingenol mebutate gel | Multiple AKs in a photodamaged area |

| Field-Directed Agent | Application | Duration | Notes |
|---------------------|-------------|----------|-------|
| **5-fluorouracil 5% cream** | BID | 2-4 weeks | Highly effective; significant inflammation during treatment |
| **Imiquimod 5% cream** | 2-3x weekly | 16 weeks | Immune response modifier; inflammatory reaction expected |
| **Diclofenac 3% gel** | BID | 60-90 days | Less irritating; slower response; good tolerability |
| **Ingenol mebutate 0.015-0.05% gel** | Once daily x 2-3 days | Short course | Rapid; localized inflammatory response |
| **Photodynamic therapy** | Single session | One-time | Excellent cosmetic outcome; pain during treatment |

## 5.5 Skin Cancer Prevention

### Photoprotection (AAD Guidelines) [^20^]

| Strategy | Recommendation |
|----------|---------------|
| **Broad-spectrum sunscreen SPF 30+** | Apply liberally (2 mg/cm2 = ~1 oz for full body); reapply every 2 hours and after swimming/sweating |
| **Physical blockers** | Zinc oxide, titanium dioxide — preferred for sensitive skin, children, post-procedure |
| **Chemical blockers** | Avobenzone, octinoxate, octisalate, homosalate, oxybenzone |
| **Protective clothing** | UPF-rated clothing, wide-brimmed hats, UV-blocking sunglasses |
| **Behavioral** | Seek shade, especially 10 AM - 4 PM; avoid tanning beds |

**Tanning bed warning:** Indoor tanning increases melanoma risk by 75% when used before age 30. The WHO classifies UV tanning devices as Group 1 carcinogens.

### Screening

- **Self-examination:** Monthly; examine all skin surfaces including scalp, soles, between toes, genitalia
- **Professional skin examination:** Annually for average-risk adults; every 3-6 months for high-risk patients (personal history of melanoma, multiple atypical nevi, strong family history, immunosuppression)

## 5.6 When to Seek Urgent Evaluation

- New or changing mole (ABCDE features)
- Non-healing sore or ulcer
- Rapidly growing skin lesion
- Bleeding, crusting, or painful lesion
- Dark streak under a nail (possible subungual melanoma)
- Pigmented lesion with irregular features in a high-risk patient

# Chapter 6: Other Common Dermatoses

## 6.1 Contact Dermatitis

Contact dermatitis is divided into two main types with distinct pathophysiology:

| Feature | Irritant Contact Dermatitis (ICD) | Allergic Contact Dermatitis (ACD) |
|---------|----------------------------------|----------------------------------|
| **Mechanism** | Direct chemical/physical damage to skin barrier | Type IV delayed hypersensitivity (T-cell mediated) |
| **Immunology** | Non-immunologic; anyone can develop | Requires prior sensitization |
| **Onset** | Minutes to hours after exposure | 24-72 hours after re-exposure |
| **Distribution** | Exposed areas; hands most common | Exposed areas; may spread beyond contact site |
| **Appearance** | Erythema, dryness, fissuring, burning | Pruritic, eczematous, vesicular; sharp demarcation |
| **Prevalence** | 80% of occupational contact dermatitis | 20% of occupational contact dermatitis |

### Common Allergens

| Allergen | Source |
|----------|--------|
| **Nickel** | Jewelry, snaps, zippers, cell phones, eyeglass frames — **most common contact allergen globally** |
| **Fragrances** | Perfumes, cosmetics, personal care products |
| **Preservatives** | Formaldehyde, methylisothiazolinone (MI/MCI), quaternium-15 |
| **Topical antibiotics** | Neomycin, bacitracin |
| **Rubber accelerators** | Thiurams, carbamates, mercaptobenzothiazole |
| **Poison ivy/oak** | Urushiol (plants: *Toxicodendron* species) |
| **PPD (p-phenylenediamine)** | Hair dyes, temporary tattoos, leather |
| **Cocamidopropyl betaine** | Shampoos, body washes |
| **Balsam of Peru** | Fragrances, flavorings |

### Diagnosis and Treatment

**Diagnosis:**
- **Clinical:** History of exposure, distribution matching contact pattern, sharp demarcation
- **Patch testing:** Gold standard for ACD; performed by experienced clinician; apply panels of allergens, read at 48 hours and 72-96 hours [^21^]

**Treatment:**
- **First-line:** Strict avoidance of trigger; topical corticosteroids (medium-high potency for acute; low potency for maintenance)
- **Severe ACD (e.g., poison ivy):** Oral corticosteroids — **must taper over 2-3 weeks** (shorter tapers cause rebound dermatitis)
- **Chronic/refractory:** Phototherapy, systemic immunosuppressants (short-term)
- **Barrier repair:** Emollients, protective creams (barrier creams for occupational exposures)

---

## 6.2 Urticaria (Hives)

### Classification

| Type | Duration | Common Triggers |
|------|----------|----------------|
| **Acute urticaria** | <6 weeks | Infections (most common), medications (NSAIDs, antibiotics), foods, insect stings |
| **Chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU)** | >=6 weeks, most days | Autoimmune (anti-FceRI, anti-IgE antibodies), idiopathic (50-70%) |
| **Chronic inducible urticaria (CIndU)** | >=6 weeks, triggered | Physical stimuli: cold, heat, pressure, vibration, sunlight, water, exercise |

### Pathophysiology

Mast cell degranulation leads to histamine release, causing increased vascular permeability, dermal edema (wheals), and sensory nerve activation (pruritus). In CSU, approximately 30-50% of patients have functional autoantibodies against IgE or its receptor (FceRI).

### Diagnosis

- Primarily clinical; limited testing needed
- For chronic urticaria: CBC, ESR/CRP, thyroid function tests (TSH, anti-TPO); thyroid autoimmunity association present
- Physical challenge tests for CIndU (ice cube test for cold urticaria, pressure test for delayed pressure urticaria)
- Extensive allergen testing is **not** routinely recommended for CSU

### Treatment (EAACI/GA2LEN/EDF/WAO 2022 Update) [^22^] [^23^]

| Step | Treatment | Notes |
|------|-----------|-------|
| **1 (First-line)** | Second-generation H1-antihistamines (cetirizine, loratadine, fexofenadine, levocetirizine, desloratadine, bilastine) at standard doses daily | Non-sedating; once daily; excellent safety |
| **2** | Up-titrate H1-antihistamine up to 4x standard dose | Strongest evidence for levocetirizine, desloratadine, bilastine; generally well tolerated |
| **3** | Add **omalizumab (Xolair)** 300 mg SC every 4 weeks | FDA-approved for CSU >=12 years; anti-IgE monoclonal antibody; 70-90% response rate |
| **4 (Refractory)** | Add cyclosporine 3-5 mg/kg/day | Reserved for refractory cases due to nephrotoxicity, hypertension risk |

**Systemic corticosteroids:** Short courses only (up to 10 days for acute exacerbations); **never for long-term chronic urticaria management** due to cumulative toxicity.

### Special Considerations

- **Angioedema:** Occurs in ~40% of CSU patients; affects deeper dermis/subcutis — lips, periorbital area, genitals, tongue, extremities; omalizumab is highly effective for prevention
- **Anaphylaxis risk:** If urticaria is associated with systemic symptoms (hypotension, bronchospasm, laryngeal edema), patient requires epinephrine auto-injector and allergy evaluation
- **Thyroid autoimmunity:** Check TSH in all chronic urticaria patients; treat thyroid dysfunction

## 6.3 Rosacea

Rosacea is a chronic inflammatory condition affecting the central face, characterized by flushing, persistent erythema, papules, pustules, telangiectasias, and in some cases, phymatous changes.

### Subtypes

| Subtype | Features | Treatment Focus |
|---------|----------|----------------|
| **Erythematotelangiectatic** | Flushing, persistent central facial erythema, telangiectasias | Trigger avoidance, vascular-targeted therapies |
| **Papulopustular** | Erythema with papules and pustules ("acne rosacea") | Anti-inflammatory therapy |
| **Phymatous** | Skin thickening, irregular surface, rhinophyma (nose) | Isotretinoin, surgical/laser debulking |
| **Ocular** | Blepharitis, conjunctivitis, dryness, grittiness, recurrent chalazia | Lid hygiene, oral antibiotics, ophthalmology referral |

### Triggers

Common triggers include sun exposure, heat, alcohol (especially red wine), spicy foods, hot beverages, emotional stress, strenuous exercise, certain skincare products, and temperature extremes. Individual trigger identification and avoidance are essential.

### Treatment

| Presentation | Treatment Options |
|-------------|-------------------|
| **Erythema/telangiectasia** | Topical brimonidine 0.33% gel or oxymetazoline 1% cream (vasoconstrictors — temporary effect); pulsed dye laser (PDL) or Nd:YAG for telangiectasia |
| **Papulopustular** | Topical metronidazole 0.75-1%, azelaic acid 15%, ivermectin 1% cream (anti-inflammatory and anti-Demodex); oral doxycycline 40 mg (sub-antimicrobial dose) or minocycline |
| **Phymatous** | Oral isotretinoin; surgical or laser debulking; CO2 or erbium laser |
| **Ocular** | Warm compresses, lid hygiene, artificial tears; oral doxycycline; urgent ophthalmology if severe |

**Important:** **Avoid topical corticosteroids on the face** — can cause steroid-induced rosacea, perioral dermatitis, and skin atrophy. Use gentle, non-irritating skincare; daily sun protection is essential.

---

## 6.4 Vitiligo

Vitiligo is an autoimmune condition characterized by destruction of melanocytes causing depigmented (milky-white) patches. It affects 0.5-2% of the global population, with onset typically before age 30.

### Types

| Type | Features | Prognosis |
|------|----------|-----------|
| **Non-segmental vitiligo** (most common) | Generalized, bilateral, often symmetric; acrofacial, vulgaris, universal subtypes | Variable; may be progressive, stable, or repigment with treatment |
| **Segmental vitiligo** | Unilateral, dermatomal or linear distribution; earlier onset | Rapidly progressive initially, then typically stable; less responsive to medical therapy |

### Associated Autoimmune Conditions

- Thyroid disease (hyper- and hypothyroidism) — most common
- Pernicious anemia, type 1 diabetes mellitus, Addison disease, alopecia areata, rheumatoid arthritis
- **Recommendation:** Screen for thyroid dysfunction (TSH) at diagnosis and periodically

### Treatment

| Extent | Treatment Options |
|--------|-------------------|
| **Limited (<10% BSA)** | Potent topical corticosteroids (short-term, 2-3 months); topical calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus, pimecrolimus — especially for face); **topical ruxolitinib 1.5% cream (Opzelura)** — FDA-approved for non-segmental vitiligo |
| **Moderate-severe** | NB-UVB phototherapy (2-3x weekly; most effective); oral mini-pulse corticosteroids for rapidly progressive disease |
| **Stable/refractory** | Autologous melanocyte transplantation (epidermal grafting, blister grafting, cellular grafting) |
| **Cosmetic** | Cover-up makeup (Dermablend, Covermark), self-tanners, monobenzone depigmentation (for extensive/universal vitiligo) |

**Key points:**
- Face and neck respond best to treatment; hands, feet, and bony prominences respond poorly
- Repigmentation typically begins around hair follicles (perifollicular pigment dots)
- Treatment requires 3-6 months minimum before assessing response
- Photoprotection of depigmented areas is essential (no melanin = no UV protection; high sunburn and skin cancer risk)
- **Significant psychosocial impact:** Provide counseling, support group referrals, address mental health

---

## 6.5 Alopecia Areata

Alopecia areata is an autoimmune T-cell-mediated attack on hair follicles causing non-scarring hair loss. Lifetime prevalence is approximately 2%.

### Clinical Presentation

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Classic** | Well-circumscribed, smooth patches of complete hair loss; exclamation mark hairs at periphery |
| **Nail changes** | Pitting, trachyonychia, onychomadesis |
| **Severity spectrum** | Patchy alopecia -> alopecia totalis (entire scalp) -> alopecia universalis (entire body) |

### Severity Classification

| Grade | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| **SALT score** (Severity of Alopecia Tool) | Percentage of scalp hair loss: S0 (none), S1 (<25%), S2 (25-49%), S3 (50-74%), S4 (75-94%), S5 (95-100%) |

### Treatment [^24^]

| Extent | Treatment Options |
|--------|-------------------|
| **Limited (<50% scalp)** | Intralesional corticosteroids (triamcinolone acetonide 2.5-10 mg/mL) — first-line; topical corticosteroids; topical minoxidil; topical immunotherapy (diphenylcyclopropenone — DPCP, squaric acid) |
| **Extensive (>=50% scalp)** | Oral JAK inhibitors: **baricitinib 2-4 mg daily** (FDA-approved for severe AA); tofacitinib, ruxolitinib (off-label); systemic corticosteroids (pulse therapy — not for long-term) |
| **Support** | Wigs, hairpieces, camouflage products, psychological support, support groups |

**Prognostic factors:** Worse prognosis with early onset, extensive involvement, atopy, nail changes, positive family history, and alopecia totalis/universalis. Spontaneous regrowth occurs in 30-50% of limited disease within 1 year.

## 6.6 Seborrheic Dermatitis

Seborrheic dermatitis is a chronic, relapsing inflammatory condition affecting sebaceous gland-rich areas (scalp, face, chest). It is associated with *Malassezia* yeast overgrowth, altered sebum composition, and immune response dysregulation.

### Associations

HIV/AIDS (may be severe), Parkinson's disease, stroke, heart failure, mood disorders, and recovery from stressful physiologic states.

### Treatment by Body Site

| Site | Treatment Options |
|------|-------------------|
| **Scalp** | Ketoconazole 2% shampoo, selenium sulfide 2.5%, zinc pyrithione shampoo, coal tar shampoo, salicylic acid shampoo; topical corticosteroid solutions/foams (betamethasone valerate 0.12%, clobetasol propionate 0.05% solution) for flares |
| **Face** | Ketoconazole 2% cream, mild topical corticosteroids (hydrocortisone 1-2.5%), topical calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus, pimecrolimus — steroid-sparing) |
| **Chest/back** | Same as face; ketoconazole shampoo as body wash |
| **Refractory** | Oral fluconazole or itraconazole pulse therapy; consider underlying conditions |
| **Infantile (cradle cap)** | Mineral oil application, gentle shampooing; usually resolves spontaneously within months |

## 6.7 Hidradenitis Suppurativa (HS)

Hidradenitis suppurativa (acne inversa) is a chronic, recurrent inflammatory disease of follicular occlusion in apocrine gland-bearing areas, causing painful nodules, abscesses, draining sinus tracts, and progressive scarring.

### Clinical Features

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Location** | Axillae, groin, perianal, inframammary, inner thighs |
| **Hurley stages** | Stage I (abscesses, no sinus tracts/scarring); Stage II (recurrent abscesses with sinus tracts/scarring, separated); Stage III (diffuse involvement, interconnected sinus tracts) |
| **Associated conditions** | Obesity, smoking, metabolic syndrome, depression, Crohn's disease, acne conglobata, pilonidal disease ("follicular occlusion tetrad") |

### Treatment (AAD/European S2k 2024 Guidelines) [^25^] [^26^]

| Stage | Treatment |
|-------|-----------|
| **General measures (all stages)** | Smoking cessation, weight loss, loose clothing, wound care, pain management |
| **Mild (Hurley I)** | Topical clindamycin 1%; oral tetracyclines (doxycycline 100 mg BID) for >=12 weeks; intralesional corticosteroids for acute nodules |
| **Moderate (Hurley II)** | Oral tetracyclines; clindamycin 300 mg BID + rifampicin 600 mg daily x 10-12 weeks (highly effective combination); if refractory -> adalimumab |
| **Severe (Hurley III)** | **Adalimumab** (first-line biologic); infliximab; secukinumab; bimekizumab; surgical excision of affected areas |

### Adalimumab Dosing for HS

| Phase | Dosing |
|-------|--------|
| **Induction** | 160 mg SC (week 0), 80 mg SC (week 2) |
| **Maintenance** | 40 mg SC weekly |
| **Non-responders** | May increase to 80 mg weekly |

**Key management points:**
- Pain management is an essential component — consider multimodal analgesia
- Screen for and treat depression and anxiety (high prevalence)
- Screen for metabolic syndrome, diabetes, cardiovascular risk factors
- Surgical options (deroofing, wide local excision) for refractory Hurley II-III disease
- Wound care expertise is critical for managing draining tracts

---

## 6.8 When to Seek Medical Care

- Sudden onset of widespread hives with breathing difficulty or facial swelling (possible anaphylaxis)
- Any new or changing skin lesion with concern for skin cancer
- Widespread blistering or skin detachment
- Painful, draining skin nodules (possible HS)
- Rapidly progressive hair loss
- Widespread depigmentation causing significant distress
- Facial rash unresponsive to gentle skincare measures

# Chapter 7: Osteoarthritis

## 7.1 Overview

Osteoarthritis (OA) is the most common form of arthritis, affecting an estimated 32.5 million adults in the United States and 300 million people globally. It is a degenerative joint disease characterized by progressive loss of articular cartilage, subchondral bone remodeling, osteophyte formation, synovial inflammation, and progressive joint dysfunction. The simplistic "wear and tear" model has evolved to recognize OA as a disease of the whole joint organ, with contributions from biomechanical, inflammatory, metabolic, and genetic factors. [^27^]

### Commonly Affected Joints

Knee (most common symptomatic site), hip, hand (distal interphalangeal joints, proximal interphalangeal joints, first carpometacarpal joint), spine (cervical and lumbar), foot (first metatarsophalangeal joint).

### Risk Factors

| Category | Factors |
|----------|---------|
| **Non-modifiable** | Advanced age, female sex (especially hand/knee OA), genetic predisposition (e.g., GDF5 variants), joint shape/alignment (hip dysplasia, varus/valgus knee) |
| **Modifiable** | **Obesity** (strongest modifiable risk factor for knee OA), prior joint injury (ACL tear, meniscal tear), repetitive joint loading (occupational), muscle weakness, sedentary lifestyle |

### Pathophysiology

Progressive cartilage matrix degradation (mediated by MMPs, ADAMTS enzymes), chondrocyte apoptosis, subchondral bone sclerosis and cyst formation, osteophyte development at joint margins, synovial inflammation (low-grade), and periarticular muscle atrophy contribute to the disease process. Pain arises from subchondral bone, periosteum, synovium, ligaments, and capsule — cartilage itself is aneural.

---

## 7.2 Red Flags / When to Suspect Alternative Diagnosis

| Feature | Possible Alternative Diagnosis | Action |
|---------|-------------------------------|--------|
| Hot, swollen joint with systemic symptoms | Septic arthritis, gout, pseudogout, inflammatory arthritis | Joint aspiration for crystal analysis and culture; urgent evaluation |
| Rapidly progressive symptoms over weeks | Inflammatory arthritis, avascular necrosis, malignancy | Comprehensive workup including imaging, labs |
| Predominant morning stiffness >30 minutes | Rheumatoid arthritis, spondyloarthritis | RF, anti-CCP, inflammatory markers, rheumatology referral |
| Neurologic symptoms (weakness, numbness) | Spinal stenosis, radiculopathy, cord compression | MRI, neurology/neurosurgery referral |
| Unexplained weight loss, night sweats | Malignancy, infection | Systemic workup |
| Young patient (<40 years) with OA | Secondary OA (post-traumatic, congenital, metabolic) | Investigate underlying cause |

---

## 7.3 Guidelines & Evidence

The **2019 ACR/Arthritis Foundation Guidelines** and **2024 ACR Updates** provide comprehensive, joint-specific recommendations based on systematic review and expert consensus. [^27^] [^28^]

**Strongly recommended interventions:**
- Exercise (aerobic, resistance, neuromuscular)
- Weight loss for overweight/obese patients
- Self-efficacy and self-management programs
- Oral NSAIDs (shortest duration, lowest effective dose)
- Total joint replacement for end-stage disease with intractable pain

**Strongly recommended AGAINST:**
- Glucosamine and chondroitin sulfate (no consistent benefit over placebo)
- Hyaluronic acid injections (for knee OA — mixed evidence, not recommended by ACR)
- Biologic disease-modifying agents (TNF inhibitors, IL-1 antagonists) — no efficacy in OA
- Platelet-rich plasma (PRP), stem cell injections — insufficient evidence
- Opioids (non-tramadol) — unfavorable risk-benefit ratio
- Bisphosphonates — no proven disease-modifying effect in OA

---

## 7.4 Non-Pharmacologic Management

### Exercise — Cornerstone of Treatment

Exercise has the strongest evidence base of any OA intervention and should be recommended to all patients regardless of disease severity. [^27^]

| Type | Examples | Benefits |
|------|----------|----------|
| **Aerobic** | Walking, cycling, swimming, elliptical | Cardiovascular fitness, weight management, pain reduction |
| **Resistance/strengthening** | Quadriceps exercises (knee OA), hip abductors (hip OA), grip strengthening (hand OA) | Improved joint stability, shock absorption, functional performance |
| **Neuromuscular/balance** | Tai chi, balance exercises, proprioception training | Reduced fall risk, improved joint position sense |
| **Range of motion/flexibility** | Gentle stretching, yoga | Maintained joint mobility, reduced stiffness |
| **Aquatic therapy** | Water-based exercise programs | Reduced joint loading; excellent for patients with significant pain |

**Implementation:** Referral to physical therapy for individualized, supervised program; gradual progression; "start low, go slow" for deconditioned patients. Land-based exercise is as effective as aquatic therapy for most patients.

### Weight Management

- **5-10% weight loss** significantly improves pain and function in knee and hip OA
- Greater weight loss is associated with greater clinical benefit
- Combined diet and exercise produces optimal outcomes
- For every 1 kg of weight lost, there is a 4 kg reduction in knee joint load during ambulation

### Assistive Devices

| Device | Indication | Benefit |
|--------|-----------|---------|
| **Cane** | Unilateral lower extremity OA | Reduces joint load by 20-30%; use contralateral hand |
| **Walker** | Bilateral lower extremity OA, balance issues | Greater stability than cane |
| **Braces** | Unicompartmental knee OA (unloader brace) | Reduces load on affected compartment |
| **Orthotics/insoles** | Foot OA, knee malalignment | Improved alignment, shock absorption |
| **Patellar taping** | Patellofemoral OA | Immediate pain reduction in some patients |
| **Thumb splint (CMC)** | First CMC OA | Pain relief, improved pinch function |

### Self-Management

- Patient education about OA and its management
- Cognitive behavioral therapy for pain coping strategies
- Heat/cold therapy (heat for stiffness, cold for acute pain/swelling)
- Acupuncture (conditionally recommended — modest benefit for some patients)
- Activity modification while maintaining function

---

## 7.5 Pharmacologic Management

| Agent | Recommendation | Dosing/Notes |
|-------|----------------|-------------|
| **Topical NSAIDs** (diclofenac sodium 1% gel, diclofenac epolamine 1.3% patch) | First-line for hand and knee OA; conditionally recommended for hip | Apply to affected joint up to QID; lower systemic exposure; preferred over oral NSAIDs when feasible; minimal GI/renal/cardiovascular risk |
| **Oral NSAIDs** | Strongly recommended (shortest duration, lowest dose) | Naproxen 250-500 mg BID, ibuprofen 400-800 mg TID; COX-2 selective (celecoxib 100-200 mg daily) for lower GI risk; **avoid in CKD, PUD, anticoagulation, significant CVD** |
| **Acetaminophen** | Conditionally recommended | Up to 3 g/day total (previously 4 g — reduced due to liver toxicity); modest efficacy; safest pharmacologic option for many patients |
| **Duloxetine** (Cymbalta) | Conditionally recommended | 60 mg daily (start 30 mg x 1-2 weeks); especially useful for patients with concomitant widespread pain, depression; may take 2-4 weeks for benefit |
| **Intra-articular corticosteroids** | Conditionally recommended | Triamcinolone acetonide or methylprednisolone acetate; rapid short-term relief (1-4 weeks); repeat no more frequently than every 3-4 months; avoid excessive injections due to cartilage damage risk |
| **Tramadol** | Conditionally recommended | 50-100 mg up to QID (max 400 mg/day); weakest opioid; reserve for refractory cases; monitor for sedation, constipation, dependence |
| **Intra-articular hyaluronic acid** | Not recommended (ACR strongly against) | Mixed evidence; expensive; not recommended for knee OA; may have role in specific circumstances (contraindications to other therapies) |
| **Topical capsaicin** | Conditionally recommended AGAINST | Burning sensation limits adherence; modest benefit for hand OA |
| **Non-tramadol opioids** | Strongly recommended AGAINST | Unfavorable risk-benefit ratio; avoid in chronic OA management |

## 7.6 Surgical Management

### Total Joint Replacement (Arthroplasty)

| Joint | Procedure | Outcomes |
|-------|-----------|----------|
| **Knee** | Total knee arthroplasty (TKA) | 90-95% patient satisfaction; durable 15-20+ years; prehabilitation improves outcomes |
| **Hip** | Total hip arthroplasty (THA) | Highly successful; anterior and posterior approaches; rapid mobilization |
| **Partial knee** | Unicompartmental knee replacement | Select patients with unicompartmental disease; faster recovery; preserves cruciate ligaments |

**Indications:** End-stage OA with intractable pain and functional limitation despite comprehensive conservative treatment; radiographic evidence of joint space loss.

**Prehabilitation:** Exercise, weight loss, smoking cessation, diabetes optimization, nutrition — all improve surgical outcomes and reduce complications.

### Other Surgical Options

| Procedure | Indication | Notes |
|-----------|-----------|-------|
| **Osteotomy** | Younger patients with unicompartmental disease and malalignment | Redistributes load; delays need for arthroplasty |
| **Arthroscopy** | Mechanical symptoms (true locking from loose body or meniscal tear) | **Not recommended for OA without mechanical symptoms** |
| **Joint fusion (arthrodesis)** | Severe ankle, wrist, thumb CMC OA | Eliminates pain at cost of motion; salvage procedure |

---

## 7.7 Follow-up and Monitoring

- Reassess pain and function every 3-6 months
- Monitor NSAID use for GI, renal, and cardiovascular adverse effects
- Reassess exercise adherence and effectiveness
- Consider escalation to surgical evaluation if progressive functional decline despite optimal conservative management
- No specific laboratory monitoring required for non-pharmacologic therapy

# Chapter 8: Rheumatoid Arthritis

## 8.1 Overview

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic, systemic autoimmune disease characterized by symmetric, inflammatory polyarthritis. It affects approximately 1% of the adult population worldwide, with a female predominance of 3:1 and peak onset between ages 40-60. Untreated RA causes progressive joint destruction, disability, and increased mortality — primarily from cardiovascular disease. Early diagnosis and aggressive treatment are critical: most joint damage occurs within the first 6-12 months of disease onset. [^29^] [^30^]

### Diagnostic Features

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Symmetric small joint arthritis** | MCP, PIP, MTP joints — hallmark presentation |
| **Morning stiffness** | >60 minutes (distinct from OA's brief stiffness) |
| **Inflammatory markers** | Elevated ESR and/or CRP |
| **Autoantibodies** | Positive rheumatoid factor (RF) and/or anti-CCP (anti-cyclic citrullinated peptide) |
| **Radiographic findings** | Erosions at MCP, MTP, wrist — may be absent early |
| **Extra-articular manifestations** | Rheumatoid nodules, interstitial lung disease, vasculitis, Sjogren's syndrome, Felty syndrome, pericarditis, scleritis |

### 2010 ACR/EULAR Classification Criteria

Score-based algorithm for early diagnosis in patients with definite clinical synovitis in at least one joint:

| Domain | Criteria | Score |
|--------|----------|-------|
| **Joint involvement** | 1 large joint | 0 |
| | 2-10 large joints | 1 |
| | 1-3 small joints (with or without large) | 2 |
| | 4-10 small joints | 3 |
| | >10 joints (including >=1 small joint) | 5 |
| **Serology** | Negative RF and anti-CCP | 0 |
| | Low-positive RF or anti-CCP | 2 |
| | High-positive RF or anti-CCP | 3 |
| **Acute-phase reactants** | Normal CRP and ESR | 0 |
| | Abnormal CRP or ESR | 1 |
| **Duration** | <6 weeks | 0 |
| | >=6 weeks | 1 |

**Score >=6/10 = definite RA.** These criteria were designed for early diagnosis and classification for clinical trials, not as strict diagnostic criteria.

---

## 8.2 Red Flags / Emergency Features

| Feature | Concern | Action |
|---------|---------|--------|
| Monoarthritis with fever and hot, swollen joint | Septic arthritis | **EMERGENCY** — joint aspiration, cultures, urgent empiric antibiotics |
| Rapidly progressive vision changes, red eye | Scleritis, peripheral ulcerative keratitis | **URGENT** — ophthalmology same day |
| New neurologic deficits, stroke-like symptoms | Cerebral vasculitis, APS-related thrombosis | **URGENT** — neuroimaging, neurology referral |
| Acute dyspnea, hypoxia | Interstitial lung disease, pleuritis, PE | **URGENT** — chest imaging, pulmonary evaluation |
| Felty syndrome (RA + neutropenia + splenomegaly) | High infection risk | Hematology/rheumatology co-management |
| Atlantoaxial subluxation symptoms | Cord compression risk | Cervical spine flexion/extension X-rays before intubation |

---

## 8.3 Principles of Management: Treat-to-Target

The **ACR 2024 Guidelines** emphasize a treat-to-target strategy: [^30^] [^31^]

1. **Early DMARD initiation** within 3 months of symptom onset
2. **Treat-to-target:** Adjust therapy every 1-3 months to achieve remission or low disease activity
3. **Methotrexate as first-line** for moderate-to-high disease activity
4. **Avoid long-term glucocorticoids** (>3 months) whenever possible
5. **Monitor disease activity** with validated tools at every visit
6. **Address comorbidities:** Cardiovascular risk reduction, bone protection, vaccination

### Disease Activity Assessment Tools

| Tool | Components | Use |
|------|-----------|-----|
| **DAS28-CRP** | 28 joint count, CRP, patient global assessment | Most commonly used in practice and trials |
| **CDAI** | 28 joint count, patient/physician global assessment | No labs required; practical for clinic |
| **SDAI** | CDAI + CRP | Slightly more objective than CDAI |
| **ACR20/50/70** | Percentage improvement criteria | Clinical trial endpoints |

---

## 8.4 Conventional Synthetic DMARDs (csDMARDs)

| Agent | Dosing | Monitoring | Key Considerations |
|-------|--------|------------|-------------------|
| **Methotrexate (MTX)** | 7.5-25 mg weekly (oral, SC, IM); start 7.5-15 mg, titrate up | CBC, LFTs, creatinine every 2-4 weeks initially, then q8-12 weeks | **First-line for RA**; folic acid 1-5 mg daily (except MTX day); teratogenic; avoid alcohol; caution with renal impairment |
| **Hydroxychloroquine (HCQ)** | 200-400 mg daily | Baseline eye exam; annual screening after 5 years | Safest DMARD; slow onset (3-6 months); safe in pregnancy; low toxicity |
| **Sulfasalazine (SSZ)** | 500 mg daily, titrate by 500 mg weekly to 2-3 g/day in divided doses | CBC, LFTs at baseline and periodically | Sulfonamide component; caution with sulfa allergy; GI upset common; compatible with pregnancy |
| **Leflunomide** | 10-20 mg daily | CBC, LFTs at baseline and periodically | Teratogenic; cholestyramine washout required before conception (for both sexes); hepatotoxicity risk |

**ACR 2024 Strong Recommendations for DMARD-naive patients:** [^31^]
- MTX monotherapy over biologic or JAK inhibitor monotherapy for moderate-high disease activity
- MTX over hydroxychloroquine or sulfasalazine for moderate-high disease activity
- csDMARD initiation without longer-term glucocorticoids over initiation with glucocorticoids
- Treat-to-target approach over usual care

---

## 8.5 Biologic DMARDs (bDMARDs)

All biologics require **TB screening** (IGRA or TST) before initiation, **hepatitis B screening**, and ongoing infection surveillance. Live vaccines are contraindicated during therapy — administer all indicated live vaccines prior to starting.

### TNF-alpha Inhibitors

| Agent | Dosing | Administration |
|-------|--------|---------------|
| **Adalimumab** (Humira) | 40 mg SC every 2 weeks | SC injection; biosimilars available |
| **Etanercept** (Enbrel) | 25 mg SC twice weekly OR 50 mg weekly | SC injection |
| **Infliximab** (Remicade) | 3 mg/kg IV at weeks 0, 2, 6, then every 8 weeks | IV infusion; need for premedication |
| **Golimumab** (Simponi) | 50 mg SC monthly | SC injection |
| **Certolizumab pegol** (Cimzia) | 200 mg SC every 2 weeks OR 400 mg every 4 weeks | SC injection; minimal placental transfer |

**Key adverse effects:** Increased infection risk (including TB reactivation), injection site/infusion reactions, new/worsening heart failure (avoid in NYHA III-IV), demyelinating disease (contraindicated in MS), lymphoma (small theoretical increased risk), lupus-like syndrome.

### Non-TNF Biologics

| Agent | Target | Dosing | Key Notes |
|-------|--------|--------|-----------|
| **Abatacept** (Orencia) | CTLA-4-Ig (T-cell costimulation inhibitor) | IV monthly or SC weekly | Less immunogenic; may have lower infection risk; suitable for elderly |
| **Rituximab** (Rituxan) | CD20 (B-cell depletion) | 1000 mg IV x 2 doses, 2 weeks apart; repeat as needed (typically 6-12 months) | First-dose reactions; hepatitis B reactivation risk; monitor IgG levels; preferred for seropositive RA with poor response to TNF inhibitors |
| **Tocilizumab** (Actemra) | IL-6 receptor | IV monthly or SC every 2 weeks | Elevation in LFTs, lipids; neutropenia; diverticular perforation risk; injection site reactions |
| **Sarilumab** (Kevzara) | IL-6 receptor | 200 mg SC every 2 weeks | Similar to tocilizumab; higher injection site reactions |

### Treatment Selection

| Clinical Scenario | Preferred Agent |
|-------------------|----------------|
| DMARD-naive, moderate-high activity | Methotrexate first-line |
| MTX inadequate response | Add TNF inhibitor, abatacept, or rituximab; or switch to triple therapy (MTX + SSZ + HCQ) |
| First TNF inhibitor failure | Switch to second TNF inhibitor or non-TNF biologic |
| Seropositive, erosive disease | TNF inhibitor or rituximab |
| Concomitant IBD | Avoid TNF inhibitors (except infliximab) |
| Pregnancy planning | Certolizumab (minimal placental transfer); avoid others |
| Elderly (>65) | Abatacept (lower infection risk) |

---

## 8.6 Targeted Synthetic DMARDs (JAK Inhibitors)

| Agent | Selectivity | Dosing | Notes |
|-------|-------------|--------|-------|
| **Tofacitinib** (Xeljanz) | JAK1/JAK3 | 5 mg BID or 11 mg XR daily | First approved JAK for RA; most safety data |
| **Baricitinib** (Olumiant) | JAK1/JAK2 | 2 mg daily (may increase to 4 mg) | Once-daily dosing |
| **Upadacitinib** (Rinvoq) | JAK1 selective | 15 mg daily | Higher efficacy in some trials |

**Black Box Warning:** Based on the tofacitinib ORAL Surveillance study, all JAK inhibitors carry an FDA boxed warning for increased risk of serious cardiovascular events, malignancy, thrombosis (VTE, PE), and death compared to TNF inhibitors in RA patients >=50 years with >=1 cardiovascular risk factor. [^29^]

**ACR/EULAR Position:** Reserve JAK inhibitors for patients with inadequate response or intolerance to at least one bDMARD, particularly those at increased cardiovascular risk or with malignancy risk factors. [^30^]

**Monitoring:**
- Baseline: CBC with differential, LFTs, lipid panel, hepatitis B/C, TB screening
- Follow-up: CBC, LFTs every 4 weeks initially then every 12 weeks; lipids at 12 weeks
- Shingles vaccination (Shingrix) before starting; all live vaccines prior to initiation

---

## 8.7 Glucocorticoids in RA

| Use | Approach | Duration |
|-----|----------|----------|
| **Bridge therapy** | Low-dose prednisone 5-10 mg daily while waiting for DMARD onset | <=3 months; taper as disease control achieved |
| **Intra-articular** | Triamcinolone injection for single or few active joints | As needed; monitor total dose |
| **Flare management** | Short-term dose increase or intra-articular injection | Brief |
| **Severe systemic involvement** | High-dose IV methylprednisolone (pulse therapy) | Supervised taper |

**Principles:**
- **Avoid long-term systemic glucocorticoid use** when possible due to cumulative toxicity (osteoporosis, diabetes, cardiovascular, infection, cataracts)
- Taper to <5 mg/day as soon as possible
- If prolonged low-dose steroids needed, ensure bone protection (bisphosphonate, calcium, vitamin D)

## 8.8 Monitoring and Complications

### Routine Monitoring

| Parameter | Frequency |
|-----------|-----------|
| Disease activity (DAS28/CDAI) | Every 1-3 months until target achieved, then every 3-6 months |
| Labs on DMARDs (CBC, LFTs, creatinine) | Every 2-4 weeks initially, then every 8-12 weeks |
| TB screening | Annually if on biologics |
| Eye exam (HCQ) | Annually after 5 years of use |
| Lipids (JAK inhibitors) | At 12 weeks, then periodically |

### Key Complications

| Complication | Management |
|-------------|------------|
| **Cardiovascular disease** | Leading cause of death in RA; aggressive risk factor modification; statins as indicated; blood pressure control |
| **Osteoporosis** | DEXA screening; bisphosphonate prophylaxis if on glucocorticoids; calcium and vitamin D |
| **Infection** | Increased risk from disease and immunosuppression; keep vaccinations current (influenza annually, pneumococcal, Shingrix before biologics, COVID-19) |
| **Interstitial lung disease** | Screen with HRCT and PFTs if respiratory symptoms; present in 10-30% of RA patients |
| **Cervical spine instability** | Flexion/extension cervical spine X-rays before surgery requiring intubation; periodic assessment in long-standing RA |

---

## 8.9 When to Refer to Rheumatology

- Suspected inflammatory arthritis (morning stiffness, synovitis, elevated inflammatory markers)
- Confirmed RA requiring DMARD management
- Inadequate response to initial DMARD therapy
- Extra-articular manifestations (lung disease, vasculitis, scleritis)
- Pregnancy planning in RA patients
- Complex medication management (biologics, JAK inhibitors)

# Chapter 9: Gout and Crystal Arthropathies

## 9.1 Overview

**Gout** is an inflammatory arthritis caused by deposition of monosodium urate (MSU) crystals in joints and periarticular tissues, resulting from chronic hyperuricemia (serum urate >6.8 mg/dL). It affects 3.9% of US adults, making it the most common inflammatory arthritis in men. The prevalence is increasing due to rising rates of obesity, metabolic syndrome, chronic kidney disease, and diuretic use. [^32^]

### Risk Factors

| Category | Factors |
|----------|---------|
| **Demographic** | Male sex, postmenopausal women, advanced age |
| **Metabolic** | Obesity, metabolic syndrome, hypertension, CKD, type 2 diabetes |
| **Dietary** | Alcohol (especially beer and spirits), purine-rich diet (red meat, organ meats, seafood), fructose-sweetened beverages |
| **Medications** | Thiazide and loop diuretics, low-dose aspirin, cyclosporine, niacin, pyrazinamide, ethambutol |
| **Other** | Lead exposure, psoriasis, myeloproliferative disorders, tumor lysis syndrome |

### Pathophysiology

Chronic hyperuricemia -> MSU crystal supersaturation in joint fluid -> crystal formation and deposition in cartilage, synovium, and soft tissues -> phagocytosis by neutrophils -> NLRP3 inflammasome activation -> IL-1beta release -> acute inflammatory cascade -> intense pain, erythema, swelling, warmth.

---

## 9.2 Diagnosis

### Definitive Diagnosis

**Synovial fluid analysis** demonstrating **negatively birefringent, needle-shaped MSU crystals** under compensated polarized light microscopy remains the gold standard. MSU crystals are needle-shaped and show **negative birefringence** (yellow when parallel to compensator axis, blue when perpendicular).

### Clinical Diagnosis (when aspiration not feasible)

| Feature | Typical for Gout |
|---------|-----------------|
| Serum urate | >=6 mg/dL during intercritical period (may be normal during acute attack) |
| Onset | Acute, severe pain developing over hours, often at night |
| First MTP involvement | "Podagra" — first metatarsophalangeal joint (50% of first attacks) |
| Appearance | Exquisitely tender, erythematous, hot, swollen joint |
| Course | Rapid peak (<24 hours), self-limited (3-14 days), complete resolution between attacks |
| Chronic signs | Tophi (chronic crystal deposits in joints, helix, olecranon bursae, fingers) |

### ACR/EULAR 2015 Classification Criteria

Score-based classification system incorporating clinical, laboratory, and imaging features. A score >=8 is sufficient for classification.

### Imaging

| Modality | Findings |
|----------|----------|
| **X-ray** | "Punched-out" erosions with "overhanging edge" (rat bite erosions) in chronic gout; preserved joint space early |
| **Ultrasound** | "Double contour sign" (urate deposition on cartilage surface); tophaceous deposits |
| **Dual-energy CT (DECT)** | Color-coded urate detection; can identify tophi not visible on X-ray; limited availability |

---

## 9.3 Acute Gout Management

**Goal:** Rapid pain relief and inflammation reduction. Start anti-inflammatory therapy as soon as possible.

| Agent | Dosing | Key Considerations |
|-------|--------|-------------------|
| **NSAIDs** | Naproxen 500 mg BID, indomethacin 50 mg TID; full anti-inflammatory dose until attack resolves, then taper | Highly effective; **avoid in CKD, PUD, anticoagulation, significant CVD**; use with PPI for GI protection |
| **Colchicine** | 1.2 mg at first sign, then 0.6 mg 1 hour later (2-tablet regimen); OR 0.6 mg 2-3x daily until resolved | Most effective within 36 hours of onset; dose adjust for renal impairment; **major CYP3A4 and P-gp interactions** (avoid with clarithromycin, diltiazem, verapamil, cyclosporine, grapefruit); GI side effects common |
| **Corticosteroids** | Prednisone 30-35 mg daily x 5 days (or 3-day burst); intra-articular triamcinolone for monoarticular gout | Preferred if NSAIDs and colchicine contraindicated; may cause transient rebound flare after stopping; **avoid if septic arthritis cannot be excluded without aspiration** |
| **IL-1 inhibitors** | Anakinra 100 mg SC daily; canakinumab 150 mg SC single dose | For refractory gout; canakinumab highly effective but expensive; generally off-label for acute gout |

### General Principles for Acute Gout

1. **Continue urate-lowering therapy (ULT) during acute attacks** — do not stop existing ULT
2. **Rest, ice, and elevate** the affected limb
3. **Monarticular gout:** Consider joint aspiration to exclude septic arthritis (especially first episode, atypical joint, systemic symptoms)
4. **Do NOT start ULT during an acute attack** unless the patient is already receiving prophylaxis
5. Combination therapy (e.g., colchicine + NSAID, or colchicine + intra-articular steroid) may be used for severe attacks

---

## 9.4 Urate-Lowering Therapy (ULT)

### Indications for ULT [^32^]

| Indication | Rationale |
|-----------|-----------|
| Recurrent flares (>=2/year) | Recurrent disease warrants prevention |
| Tophi or chronic gouty arthritis | Need to dissolve existing crystal deposits |
| Stage 3+ CKD | Increased morbidity; ULT slows progression |
| History of urolithiasis | Prevent recurrent kidney stones |
| Serum urate >9 mg/dL | High risk of complications |

### Treat-to-Target

**Goal serum urate: <6 mg/dL** (<5 mg/dL if tophaceous gout). Regular monitoring is essential — titrate ULT dose to achieve target.

### ULT Agents

| Agent | Dosing | Notes |
|-------|--------|-------|
| **Allopurinol** | Start 100 mg daily (50 mg if CKD); titrate every 2-5 weeks by 100 mg to target; maximum 800 mg/day | **First-line ULT**; safe and effective; HLA-B*5801 testing before starting in high-risk ethnicities (Han Chinese, Thai, Koreans, African Americans) due to risk of severe cutaneous adverse reactions (SJS/TEN); safe to start during acute attack WITH anti-inflammatory prophylaxis |
| **Febuxostat** (Uloric) | 40-80 mg daily | Second-line if allopurinol intolerance or contraindication; **black box warning** for increased cardiovascular death — avoid in patients with established CVD; no need for dose adjustment in mild-moderate renal impairment |
| **Probenecid** | 500 mg BID, titrate to 2 g/day | Uricosuric — promotes renal excretion; avoid if history of urolithiasis or CKD (eGFR <30); requires high fluid intake; less effective as monotherapy; generally add-on to xanthine oxidase inhibitors |
| **Pegloticase** (Krystexxa) | 8 mg IV every 2 weeks | Recombinant uricase (converts uric acid to allantoin); for refractory, tophaceous gout failing conventional ULT; risk of infusion reactions — pre-treat with antihistamine and corticosteroid; check serum urate before each infusion; **discontinue if pre-infusion urate >6 mg/dL** (indicates development of anti-drug antibodies) |

### Flare Prophylaxis When Initiating ULT

**Essential** — flares are common during the first 3-6 months of ULT initiation as urate shifts:

| Prophylaxis | Dosing | Duration |
|-------------|--------|----------|
| **Colchicine** (preferred) | 0.6 mg daily or BID | 3-6 months |
| **Low-dose NSAID** | Naproxen 250 mg BID + PPI | 3-6 months |
| **Low-dose prednisone** | <=10 mg daily | 3-6 months (if colchicine/NSAIDs contraindicated) |

---

## 9.5 Chronic Gout Management

- **Patient education:** Dietary modification (limit purines, alcohol, fructose), weight loss, hydration (goal urine output >2 L/day)
- **Comorbidity management:** Screen and treat hypertension, CKD, metabolic syndrome, CVD
- **Cardiovascular risk management:** Statins, blood pressure control, diabetes management
- **Medication review:** Minimize thiazide diuretics when possible; consider losartan (has mild uricosuric effect)
- **Lifestyle:** Low-fat dairy, coffee, and vitamin C may have protective effects; cherries may reduce flare risk

---

## 9.6 Pseudogout (Calcium Pyrophosphate Deposition Disease — CPPD)

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Pathophysiology** | Calcium pyrophosphate dihydrate (CPPD) crystal deposition in articular cartilage |
| **Crystal identification** | **Weakly positively birefringent, rhomboid-shaped crystals** (contrast with MSU: negatively birefringent, needle-shaped) |
| **Clinical presentations** | Acute attacks (pseudogout), chronic degenerative arthritis, asymptomatic chondrocalcinosis |
| **Commonly affected joints** | Knee (most common), wrist, MCP joints, shoulder, ankle |
| **Associations** | Advanced age, hemochromatosis, hyperparathyroidism, hypothyroidism, hypomagnesemia, hypophosphatasia, familial (ANKH mutations) |
| **Imaging** | Chondrocalcinosis (linear calcification of articular cartilage on X-ray) |

### Treatment

| Scenario | Treatment |
|----------|-----------|
| **Acute pseudogout** | Similar to gout — NSAIDs, colchicine, intra-articular corticosteroids (preferred for monoarticular) |
| **Chronic/prevention** | Low-dose colchicine 0.6 mg daily for recurrent attacks |
| **Underlying conditions** | Screen for and treat metabolic disorders (iron studies, calcium, PTH, magnesium, TSH) |

### Gout vs. Pseudogout — Key Distinguishing Features

| Feature | Gout | Pseudogout |
|---------|------|------------|
| **Crystal** | MSU | CPPD |
| **Shape** | Needle | Rhomboid |
| **Birefringence** | Negative | Weak positive |
| **Color (parallel)** | Yellow | Blue |
| **First MTP** | Common | Rare |
| **Wrist/MCP** | Unusual | Common |
| **Age** | Younger (middle-aged men) | Older (>60 years) |
| **X-ray** | Punched-out erosions | Chondrocalcinosis |

---

## 9.7 When to Seek Emergency Care

- First episode of acute monoarthritis with fever (exclude septic arthritis)
- Severe, unremitting joint pain unresponsive to home measures
- Suspected septic arthritis (hot, swollen joint with fever and systemic toxicity)
- Tophi with overlying skin breakdown and drainage (risk of secondary infection)
- Recurrent kidney stones

# Chapter 10: Low Back Pain

## 10.1 Overview

Low back pain (LBP) is the leading cause of disability worldwide and one of the most common reasons for medical visits. Most episodes are acute (<6 weeks) and resolve spontaneously regardless of treatment. The primary goals of evaluation are: identifying serious underlying pathology (red flags), providing symptomatic relief, preventing transition to chronicity, and maintaining functional activity. [^33^] [^34^]

### Classification

| Category | Description | Frequency |
|----------|-------------|-----------|
| **Non-specific low back pain** | No identifiable structural or systemic cause | ~85% of cases |
| **Radicular pain (sciatica)** | Nerve root compression causing radiating leg pain | ~5-10% |
| **Specific causes** | Fracture, infection, malignancy, inflammatory spondyloarthropathy, cauda equina syndrome | ~5-10% |

---

## 10.2 Red Flags — "Can't Miss" Diagnoses

> **Absolute red flags require urgent or emergency evaluation and imaging (typically MRI).** [^33^] [^35^]

| Red Flag | Condition to Rule Out | Action |
|----------|----------------------|--------|
| **Cauda equina symptoms** (saddle anesthesia, urinary retention, fecal incontinence, bilateral leg weakness/numbness) | Cauda equina syndrome | **EMERGENCY MRI + urgent neurosurgical referral** |
| **Fever + severe back pain + risk factors** (IV drug use, immunocompromise, recent procedure) | Spinal infection (discitis, osteomyelitis, epidural abscess) | **Urgent MRI + infectious disease consultation + empiric antibiotics** |
| **History of cancer + new back pain** | Metastatic spinal disease | **Urgent MRI + oncology referral** |
| **Significant trauma + osteoporosis risk** | Vertebral compression fracture | X-ray/CT + pain management + osteoporosis workup |
| **Progressive motor deficit** (weakness, foot drop) | Cord compression, tumor, severe radiculopathy | **Urgent MRI + appropriate specialist referral** |
| **IV drug use + back pain + fever** | Spinal epidural abscess | **Emergency MRI + surgical evaluation + antibiotics** |

### Relative Red Flags

- Unexplained weight loss or night sweats
- Age >50 years with new-onset back pain
- Pain at rest or at night (unrelieved by position change)
- Failure to improve after 4-6 weeks of conservative treatment
- Long-term glucocorticoid use (risk of osteoporotic fracture)
- Inflammatory back pain features: morning stiffness >30 minutes, improves with exercise, night pain (suggestive of axial spondyloarthritis)

---

## 10.3 Cauda Equina Syndrome (CES) — EMERGENCY

Cauda equina syndrome is a **surgical emergency** requiring immediate recognition and intervention.

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Anatomy** | Compression of nerve roots below L2 (cauda equina — "horse's tail") |
| **Classic triad** | (1) Saddle anesthesia (S2-S5 dermatomes), (2) bladder/bowel dysfunction (urinary retention > incontinence), (3) decreased rectal tone |
| **Other features** | Bilateral leg pain, bilateral leg weakness, severe radiculopathy, loss of ankle/knee reflexes |
| **Common causes** | Large central disc herniation (L4-L5 most common), tumor, epidural abscess, hematoma, spinal stenosis |
| **Diagnostic test** | **Urgent MRI** is the gold standard |
| **Treatment** | **Surgical decompression within 48 hours** improves outcomes — time-sensitive emergency |
| **Prognosis** | Better recovery with earlier decompression; urinary retention (vs. incontinence) may indicate less severe compression |

**Refer immediately** if any suspicion of CES. Time to decompression is the most important factor determining functional recovery.

---

## 10.4 Imaging Guidelines

**Do NOT image in the first 6 weeks** for uncomplicated acute low back pain without red flags. Early imaging does not improve outcomes and may lead to unnecessary interventions. [^34^]

### When to Image

| Indication | Preferred Modality |
|-----------|-------------------|
| Red flags present | MRI (gold standard for most) |
| Progressive neurological deficit | MRI |
| Suspected malignancy, infection, CES | MRI with contrast |
| Vertebral fracture (trauma) | X-ray or CT |
| Failure to improve after 4-6 weeks of conservative treatment | MRI |
| Before invasive interventions (epidural injection, surgery) | MRI |
| Contraindication to MRI | CT |

**X-ray:** Limited utility for non-traumatic LBP; useful for suspected fracture, spondylolisthesis, or severe degenerative changes.

**MRI:** Gold standard for disc herniation, spinal stenosis, infection, malignancy, CES. High false-positive rate — many asymptomatic adults have disc bulges/herniations.

**CT:** Alternative when MRI contraindicated (pacemaker, severe claustrophobia); superior for bony detail; involves radiation exposure.

---

## 10.5 Management of Acute Low Back Pain (<6 weeks)

### Core Principles

1. **Stay active** — avoid prolonged bed rest; encourage gentle activity and walking
2. **Reassurance** — most acute LBP resolves spontaneously; prognosis is favorable
3. **Heat therapy** — more effective than cold for acute LBP
4. **Patient education** — self-management strategies, expected timeline

### Pharmacologic Therapy

| Agent | Recommendation | Notes |
|-------|---------------|-------|
| **Acetaminophen** | First-line option | Modest efficacy; safer for many patients |
| **NSAIDs** | First-line for most (shortest duration) | Ibuprofen, naproxen; avoid with renal disease, PUD, anticoagulation |
| **Muscle relaxants** | Short-term for severe spasms | Cyclobenzaprine, methocarbamol; sedation risk — avoid driving; limited benefit beyond 1-2 weeks |
| **Opioids** | **NOT first-line** | Short-term only if severe, refractory pain and alternatives inadequate; lowest effective dose; immediate-release preferred |
| **Gabapentinoids** | Not recommended for acute LBP | Limited evidence |
| **Systemic corticosteroids** | Not recommended | No benefit demonstrated |

### Non-Pharmacologic Therapy

| Modality | Evidence | Notes |
|----------|----------|-------|
| **Heat/cold therapy** | Strong for heat | Heat wraps effective; cold for acute injury |
| **Physical therapy** | Moderate | Consider if not improving after 2-3 weeks; exercise-based PT most effective |
| **Spinal manipulation** | Moderate | May provide short-term relief; appropriate for acute uncomplicated LBP |
| **Massage** | Limited | Short-term benefit |
| **Acupuncture** | Mixed | Some evidence for short-term relief |

---

## 10.6 Subacute and Chronic Low Back Pain (>12 weeks)

### Multidisciplinary Approach

| Component | Details |
|-----------|---------|
| **Exercise therapy** | Core strengthening, aerobic exercise, flexibility — **strongest evidence** for chronic LBP |
| **Yoga, tai chi** | Moderate evidence for benefit |
| **Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)** | Strong evidence for pain coping, fear-avoidance behaviors |
| **Return-to-work programs** | Coordinated occupational intervention |

### Pharmacologic Therapy for Chronic LBP

| Agent | Evidence | Notes |
|-------|----------|-------|
| **NSAIDs** | Moderate | If not contraindicated; lowest effective dose |
| **Duloxetine** (Cymbalta) | Strong | 60 mg daily; **best evidence** for chronic LBP of any pharmacologic agent |
| **Tramadol** | Limited | Short-term use only |
| **Gabapentin/pregabalin** | Limited for non-radicular chronic LBP | May help selected patients with neuropathic features |
| **Opioids** | Weak | **Avoid for chronic non-radicular LBP**; poor long-term risk-benefit ratio |

### Interventional Therapies

| Procedure | Indication | Evidence |
|-----------|-----------|----------|
| **Epidural steroid injection** | Radicular pain (sciatica) from confirmed disc herniation or stenosis | Short-term benefit (2-6 weeks); not for axial LBP |
| **Facet joint injections** | Suspected facet-mediated pain | Diagnostic and therapeutic |
| **Medial branch blocks** | Confirm facet joint pain source | Diagnostic |
| **Radiofrequency ablation** | Confirmed facet-mediated pain | Moderate evidence for 6-12 month relief |
| **Spinal cord stimulation** | Refractory chronic pain (failed back surgery syndrome) | For carefully selected patients |
| **Surgery** | Progressive deficit, CES, confirmed disc herniation with persistent radiculopathy, spinal stenosis with neurogenic claudication | See below |

### Surgical Indications

| Condition | Surgical Option | Timing |
|-----------|----------------|--------|
| **Cauda equina syndrome** | Emergency decompression | Immediate |
| **Progressive neurologic deficit** | Decompression | Urgent |
| **Confirmed disc herniation with persistent radiculopathy** | Microdiscectomy | After 6-12 weeks failed conservative management |
| **Spinal stenosis with neurogenic claudication** | Decompression (laminectomy) | When functionally limiting |
| **Spondylolisthesis with instability** | Fusion + decompression | When symptomatic |

**Radiculopathy natural history:** Approximately 90% of disc herniations improve spontaneously within 6-12 weeks. Surgery (microdiscectomy) provides faster relief but similar long-term outcomes to conservative management.

---

## 10.7 When to Seek Emergency Care

- Saddle anesthesia or numbness in perineal area
- New urinary retention or fecal incontinence
- Progressive leg weakness or foot drop
- Fever with severe back pain
- History of cancer with new or worsening back pain
- Back pain after significant trauma (fall, motor vehicle accident)

# Chapter 11: Osteoporosis

## 11.1 Overview

Osteoporosis is a skeletal disorder characterized by compromised bone strength (due to reduced bone mineral density and deteriorated bone microarchitecture) predisposing to increased fracture risk. It affects 10.2 million adults in the US, with another 43.4 million having low bone mass (osteopenia). One in two women and one in four men >50 years will experience an osteoporotic fracture in their lifetime. Hip and vertebral fractures are associated with significant morbidity and mortality — 20-30% of hip fracture patients die within 1 year. [^36^]

### Risk Factors

| Category | Factors |
|----------|---------|
| **Non-modifiable** | Female sex, advanced age, Caucasian/Asian race, family history of hip fracture, prior fragility fracture, early menopause, dementia |
| **Modifiable** | Low BMI (<20 kg/m2), smoking, excessive alcohol (>3 drinks/day), glucocorticoid use, low calcium/vitamin D intake, physical inactivity, recurrent falls |
| **Secondary causes** | Hyperthyroidism, hyperparathyroidism, Cushing syndrome, celiac disease, CKD, multiple myeloma, rheumatoid arthritis, organ transplantation |
| **Medications** | Glucocorticoids (>=2.5 mg prednisone equivalent for >=3 months), aromatase inhibitors, androgen deprivation therapy (ADT), PPIs, anticonvulsants, SSRIs, excess thyroid hormone, methotrexate, heparin |

---

## 11.2 Diagnosis

### FRAX Risk Assessment Tool

The **FRAX tool** (https://www.sheffield.ac.uk/FRAX/) calculates 10-year probability of major osteoporotic fracture (hip, spine, forearm, humerus) and hip fracture. It integrates clinical risk factors with or without femoral neck BMD. [^36^]

**Clinical risk factors in FRAX:** Age, sex, weight, height, prior fracture, parental hip fracture, current smoking, alcohol (>=3 units/day), rheumatoid arthritis, secondary osteoporosis, glucocorticoid use.

### DEXA (Dual-Energy X-ray Absorptiometry)

DEXA is the gold standard for bone mineral density measurement.

| T-Score | Classification |
|---------|---------------|
| >= -1.0 | Normal |
| -1.0 to -2.5 | Osteopenia (low bone mass) |
| <= -2.5 | Osteoporosis |
| <= -2.5 + fragility fracture | Severe osteoporosis |

**T-score** = standard deviations from young adult reference mean. **Z-score** compares to age-matched controls (used for premenopausal women and men <50 years).

### Indications for DEXA Screening [^36^]

| Population | Recommendation |
|-----------|---------------|
| **Women >=65 years** | Universal screening |
| **Men >=70 years** | Universal screening |
| **Younger postmenopausal women with risk factors** | Risk factor-based screening |
| **Adults with fragility fracture** | Diagnostic (not just screening) |
| **Adults with conditions/medications associated with bone loss** | Indicated |
| **Anyone being considered for pharmacologic therapy** | Required |
| **Monitoring response to therapy** | Every 1-2 years (minimum 1-2 year interval between tests) |

**Vertebral Fracture Assessment (VFA):** Recommended when T-score <= -1.0 with height loss, kyphosis, or glucocorticoid use.

## 11.3 Pharmacologic Therapy

### Indications for Treatment [^36^] [^37^]

| Indication | Details |
|-----------|---------|
| T-score <= -2.5 at spine, femoral neck, or total hip | Definite osteoporosis |
| T-score -1.0 to -2.5 with FRAX 10-year major osteoporotic fracture risk >=20% or hip fracture risk >=3% | Osteopenia with high risk |
| Prior hip or vertebral fracture (clinical or morphometric) | Very high risk |
| Glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis | See section below |

### Antiresorptive Agents (First-Line)

| Class | Agent | Dosing | Key Considerations |
|-------|-------|--------|-------------------|
| **Oral bisphosphonates** | Alendronate (Fosamax) | 70 mg weekly | First-line; take on empty stomach with full glass plain water; remain upright 30-60 minutes; GI side effects; esophagitis risk |
| | Risedronate (Actonel) | 35 mg weekly or 150 mg monthly | Similar to alendronate; potentially lower GI risk; available as immediate and delayed-release |
| | Ibandronate (Boniva) | 150 mg monthly | Less potent; IV form available |
| **IV bisphosphonates** | Zoledronic acid (Reclast) | 5 mg IV annually | Ensure vitamin D repletion before infusion; acute phase reaction (fever, myalgia, flu-like symptoms — first infusion); renal function required (CrCl >35); reassess after 3 years |
| **RANKL inhibitor** | Denosumab (Prolia) | 60 mg SC every 6 months | Alternative first-line; no GI or renal concerns; **must NOT stop without bisphosphonate transition** (risk of rebound vertebral fractures); check calcium/vitamin D before each dose |

### Anabolic Agents (For Very High Fracture Risk)

| Class | Agent | Dosing | Key Considerations |
|-------|-------|--------|-------------------|
| **PTH analog** | Teriparatide (Forteo) | 20 mcg SC daily x 2 years | For very high fracture risk (T-score < -3.5, multiple fractures, glucocorticoid-induced); **must follow with antiresorptive** after completion; osteosarcoma risk in rats — avoid with Paget disease, prior radiation, open epiphyses |
| | Abaloparatide (Tymlos) | 80 mcg SC daily x 2 years | Similar to teriparatide; possibly lower hypercalcemia risk |
| **Sclerostin inhibitor** | Romosozumab (Evenity) | 210 mg SC monthly x 12 months | Dual action (increases bone formation, decreases resorption); very high fracture risk; **must follow with antiresorptive**; potential cardiovascular risk — avoid in patients with recent MI or stroke |

### Treatment Duration and Drug Holidays

| Agent | Duration | Drug Holiday |
|-------|----------|-------------|
| **Oral bisphosphonates** | Reassess after 3-5 years | If fracture risk no longer high, consider 1-2 year holiday with monitoring; resume if BMD declines or fracture occurs |
| **IV zoledronic acid** | Reassess after 3 years | Same principle as oral bisphosphonates |
| **Denosumab** | Long-term or until risk changes | **Do NOT stop without transition** to bisphosphonate (rebound vertebral fractures within 7-18 months) |
| **Teriparatide/abaloparatide** | Maximum 2 years | Must follow with antiresorptive |
| **Romosozumab** | 12 months | Must follow with antiresorptive |

**Sequential therapy:** After anabolic therapy (teriparatide, romosozumab), always transition to antiresorptive therapy (bisphosphonate or denosumab) to maintain gains. Anabolic followed by antiresorptive produces greater BMD increases than either alone.

## 11.4 Glucocorticoid-Induced Osteoporosis (GIOP)

GIOP deserves special attention due to its high prevalence and rapid onset.

| Feature | Details |
|---------|---------|
| **Timing** | Significant bone loss occurs within the first 3-6 months of glucocorticoid use |
| **Dose threshold** | Fracture risk increases at doses >=2.5 mg prednisone equivalent/day |
| **When to start bone protection** | Planned glucocorticoids >=7.5 mg/day for >=3 months; or any dose if existing high fracture risk |
| **First-line agents** | Oral bisphosphonates or IV zoledronic acid |
| **Highest risk patients** | Teriparatide preferred over bisphosphonates |
| **All patients** | Ensure adequate calcium (1000-1200 mg/day) and vitamin D (800-1000 IU/day) |

---

## 11.5 Non-Pharmacologic Management

### Calcium and Vitamin D

| Nutrient | Daily Intake | Notes |
|----------|-------------|-------|
| **Calcium** | 1000-1200 mg/day total (diet + supplements) | 1200 mg/day for postmenopausal women, men >70; calcium carbonate with meals; calcium citrate without food (better with PPI use) |
| **Vitamin D** | 800-1000 IU/day | Goal 25(OH)D level >30 ng/mL; higher doses for deficiency replacement |

### Exercise

- Weight-bearing exercises (walking, dancing, stair climbing)
- Resistance training (weights, resistance bands)
- Balance training to reduce falls
- Avoid high-impact activities if high fracture risk

### Lifestyle

- **Smoking cessation:** Smoking accelerates bone loss
- **Alcohol moderation:** Maximum 2 drinks/day (men), 1 drink/day (women)
- **Fall prevention:** See comprehensive section below

## 11.6 Fall Prevention

Falls are the leading cause of fractures in older adults. Multifactorial fall risk assessment and intervention is essential. [^36^]

### Risk Assessment

| Domain | Assessment Tool |
|--------|----------------|
| **History** | Falls in past year, fear of falling |
| **Gait and balance** | Timed Up and Go (TUG) test, observation of gait |
| **Vision** | Visual acuity testing, cataract screening |
| **Medications** | Review psychotropics, sedatives, antihypertensives, opioids |
| **Home environment** | Loose rugs, poor lighting, lack of grab bars, stairs |
| **Orthostatic vital signs** | Blood pressure and heart rate supine and standing |
| **Footwear** | Proper fit, non-slip soles |
| **Neurologic** | Cognition, peripheral neuropathy, Parkinsonism |

### Interventions with Strong Evidence

| Intervention | Evidence Level |
|-------------|---------------|
| **Exercise programs** (strength and balance) | Strong |
| **Tai Chi** | Strong |
| **Home modification** (remove hazards, install grab bars, improve lighting) | Strong |
| **Vision correction** | Moderate |
| **Medication optimization** (reduce sedatives, psychotropics) | Moderate |
| **Management of orthostatic hypotension** | Moderate |
| **Vitamin D supplementation** (800 IU/day minimum) | Moderate |
| **Proper footwear** | Moderate |
| **Hip protectors** | Moderate (for very high-risk patients in care settings) |

| Parameter | Frequency |
|-----------|-----------|
| DEXA | Every 1-2 years while on therapy (minimum 1 year between tests) |
| Medication adherence | At every visit |
| Calcium/vitamin D intake | At every visit |
| FRAX recalculation | Annually if not on pharmacotherapy |
| Height measurement | Annually (height loss may indicate vertebral fracture) |
| Fall risk assessment | Annually |
| New fracture assessment | At every visit |

## 11.8 When to Refer

- Very high fracture risk requiring anabolic therapy
- Unusual fractures or atypical presentation (rule out secondary causes)
- Recurrent fractures despite adherence to therapy
- Younger patients with osteoporosis (workup for secondary causes)
- Complex medication management (GIOP, cancer treatment-related bone loss)

# Chapter 12: Common Sports Injuries

## 12.1 Sprains vs. Strains

| Feature | Sprain | Strain |
|---------|--------|--------|
| **Tissue injured** | Ligament | Muscle or tendon |
| **Mechanism** | Stretching or tearing of ligament | Stretching or tearing of muscle/tendon |
| **Grading** | Grade 1: mild stretch; Grade 2: partial tear; Grade 3: complete rupture | Grade 1: mild; Grade 2: moderate partial tear; Grade 3: severe/complete |
| **Common examples** | Ankle sprain (lateral), ACL tear, MCL tear | Hamstring strain, groin pull, calf strain |
| **Symptoms** | Pain, swelling, instability, bruising | Pain, weakness, cramping, spasm, bruising |

---

## 12.2 RICE Protocol and Acute Management

The RICE protocol is the foundation of acute soft tissue injury management: [^38^]

| Component | Action | Duration |
|-----------|--------|----------|
| **R — Rest** | Protect injured area; avoid activities causing pain; consider immobilization if severe | First 24-72 hours |
| **I — Ice** | Apply ice pack (wrapped in thin towel) for 15-20 minutes every 2-3 hours | First 48-72 hours |
| **C — Compression** | Elastic bandage or compression sleeve; firm but not tight (should not cause numbness, tingling, or increased pain) | First 48-72 hours |
| **E — Elevation** | Elevate above heart level to reduce swelling | As much as possible, first 48-72 hours |

### Modern Modifications

- **Protection and optimal loading** are increasingly emphasized over strict rest — early gentle range of motion prevents stiffness and promotes healing
- **Avoid HARM** in the first 48-72 hours: Heat, Alcohol, Running, Massage
- Gradual return to activity as symptoms allow
- Functional rehabilitation is preferred over prolonged immobilization for most injuries

---

## 12.3 Rotator Cuff Injuries

**Anatomy:** Four muscles (supraspinatus, infraspinatus, teres minor, subscapularis — "SITS") stabilize and move the shoulder.

| Injury Type | Mechanism | Clinical Features |
|-------------|-----------|-------------------|
| **Tendinopathy/impingement** | Overuse, degeneration, subacromial compression | Pain with overhead activity, positive Neer/Hawkins tests, painful arc |
| **Partial tear** | Trauma or progressive degeneration | More significant pain, weakness, night pain |
| **Full-thickness tear** | Acute trauma or chronic attrition | Significant weakness (especially external rotation, abduction), functional limitation, positive drop arm test |

### Treatment

| Approach | Details |
|----------|---------|
| **Conservative (most cases)** | Rest, activity modification, NSAIDs; physical therapy (rotator cuff strengthening, scapular stabilization, posterior capsule stretching); subacromial corticosteroid injection for persistent pain |
| **Surgical repair** | Indicated for: full-thickness tears in active patients, acute traumatic tears in younger patients, failed conservative management (3-6 months of PT) |
| **Technique** | Most repairs performed arthroscopically; open repair for massive tears |

---

## 12.4 Meniscus Tears

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Acute tears** | Twisting injury in younger patients (sports-related); often associated with ACL injury |
| **Degenerative tears** | Older patients with minimal trauma; age-related degeneration |
| **Symptoms** | Joint line pain, swelling, locking, catching, difficulty with deep flexion, giving way |
| **Physical exam** | Joint line tenderness, positive McMurray test, pain with hyperflexion |

### Treatment

| Approach | Details |
|----------|---------|
| **Conservative** | RICE, NSAIDs, physical therapy (quadriceps and hamstring strengthening, range of motion); many degenerative tears improve without surgery |
| **Surgical indications** | Mechanical symptoms (true locking), failure of conservative management (6-12 weeks), repairable tears in younger patients |
| **Meniscal repair** | Preferred for peripheral tears in younger patients (red-red zone — better blood supply); better long-term outcomes, lower OA risk |
| **Partial meniscectomy** | For irreparable tears; trim torn fragment; faster recovery but higher OA risk |
| **Avoid total meniscectomy** | Significantly increases risk of subsequent osteoarthritis |

---

## 12.5 ACL Injuries

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Mechanism** | Non-contact: pivoting, deceleration, awkward landing (common in soccer, basketball, skiing, football) |
| **Symptoms** | Audible "pop," immediate swelling (hemarthrosis within 2 hours), instability, inability to continue activity |
| **Physical exam** | Lachman test (most sensitive, 85-95%), anterior drawer test, pivot shift test |
| **Imaging** | MRI confirms diagnosis and evaluates associated injuries (meniscus, MCL, bone bruise) |

### Treatment

| Approach | Indications | Details |
|----------|------------|---------|
| **Non-surgical** | Older, less active patients; able to modify activities; partial tears | Physical therapy (quadriceps/hamstring strengthening, proprioception); activity modification; functional bracing |
| **Surgical reconstruction** | Younger, active patients; athletes; instability with daily activities | Arthroscopic ACL reconstruction (patellar tendon or hamstring autograft); PT-guided rehabilitation 6-12 months return to sport |
| **Prevention** | Female athletes (5-8x higher ACL injury rate) | Neuromuscular training programs reduce injury risk by 50-80% |

---

## 12.6 Tennis Elbow (Lateral Epicondylitis)

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Pathophysiology** | Degenerative tendinopathy of extensor carpi radialis brevis tendon origin (not primarily inflammatory) |
| **Risk factors** | Repetitive wrist extension and gripping (tennis, manual labor, computer use) |
| **Symptoms** | Lateral elbow pain with gripping, wrist extension, lifting; tenderness over lateral epicondyle; positive Cozen test, Mill test |

### Treatment

| Modality | Details |
|----------|---------|
| **Activity modification** | Avoid aggravating activities; ergonomic modifications |
| **Physical therapy** | Eccentric exercises, stretching, strengthening; evidence supports PT as first-line |
| **Counterforce brace** | Reduces strain on tendon origin |
| **NSAIDs** | Short-term pain relief |
| **Corticosteroid injection** | Short-term benefit (2-6 weeks) but may delay long-term healing; use with caution; maximum 2-3 injections |
| **Platelet-rich plasma** | Mixed evidence; some studies suggest benefit |
| **Surgery** | Rarely needed (<10%); for refractory cases >12 months; debridement of degenerative tissue |

**Golfer's elbow (medial epicondylitis):** Similar condition affecting flexor/pronator tendon origins; pain on medial elbow with wrist flexion and forearm pronation.

## 12.7 Plantar Fasciitis

Plantar fasciitis is the **most common cause of heel pain** in adults, affecting approximately 10% of the population at some point. [^39^]

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Pathophysiology** | Degenerative changes (fasciosis) at plantar fascia origin on medial calcaneal tubercle |
| **Symptoms** | Sharp, stabbing heel pain with first steps in morning or after prolonged sitting; improves with activity, worsens with prolonged standing |
| **Risk factors** | Obesity, prolonged standing, flat feet or high arches, tight Achilles tendon/calf muscles, running (especially sudden increase in mileage), inappropriate footwear |
| **Physical exam** | Point tenderness at medial calcaneal tubercle; pain with passive dorsiflexion of toes (windlass test) |

### Treatment (90% resolve within 6-12 months with conservative care)

| Modality | Details | Evidence |
|----------|---------|----------|
| **Plantar fascia stretching** | Specific stretching exercises targeting plantar fascia and Achilles | Strong — cornerstone of treatment |
| **Calf/Achilles stretching** | Gastrocnemius and soleus stretches | Strong |
| **Arch supports/orthotics** | Over-the-counter or custom orthotics | Moderate |
| **Heel cups/cushions** | Silicone or felt heel pads | Limited |
| **Night splints** | Maintain gentle stretch during sleep | Moderate |
| **NSAIDs** | Short-term pain relief | Limited for long-term |
| **Activity modification** | Reduce aggravating activities; weight loss if overweight | Moderate |
| **Physical therapy** | Stretching, strengthening, taping | Strong |

### Refractory Cases

| Modality | Details |
|----------|---------|
| **Corticosteroid injection** | Short-term benefit (weeks); risk of plantar fascia rupture and fat pad atrophy — use sparingly (maximum 2-3 injections) |
| **Extracorporeal shockwave therapy (ESWT)** | Moderate evidence; 3-5 sessions; considered when conservative therapy fails |
| **Platelet-rich plasma injections** | Emerging evidence; variable results |
| **Surgery (plantar fascia release)** | Last resort (<5% require surgery); partial release of fascia from calcaneus |

---

## 12.8 Achilles Tendinopathy

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Epidemiology** | Most common in runners and middle-aged "weekend warriors" |
| **Types** | Mid-substance (2-6 cm above insertion) or insertional |
| **Symptoms** | Morning stiffness, pain with activity (especially pushing off), swelling, tenderness |
| **Risk factors** | Sudden increase in training, tight calf muscles, flat feet (overpronation), fluoroquinolone antibiotics (e.g., ciprofloxacin), systemic corticosteroids |

### Treatment

| Modality | Details |
|----------|---------|
| **Relative rest** | Avoid aggravating activities (running, jumping); maintain gentle activity (walking, swimming, cycling) |
| **Eccentric strengthening** | **Alfredson protocol** — gold standard: 3 sets of 15 heel drops with knee straight and knee bent, twice daily, for 12 weeks |
| **Heel raises** | In shoes to reduce tendon strain |
| **Calf stretching** | Both gastrocnemius and soleus |
| **Night splints** | Maintain gentle stretch |
| **NSAIDs** | Pain relief (not for healing) |

### Refractory Cases

| Modality | Details |
|----------|---------|
| **Extracorporeal shockwave therapy** | Moderate evidence; good option for refractory cases |
| **Platelet-rich plasma** | Emerging evidence |
| **Corticosteroid injection** | **Generally avoided** — associated with tendon rupture risk, especially intratendinous injection |
| **Surgery** | For refractory cases >6 months; debridement of degenerative tissue |

### Achilles Tendon Rupture

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Mechanism** | Sudden push-off or forced dorsiflexion; patient may report hearing a "pop" |
| **Symptoms** | Immediate severe pain, inability to push off or stand on toes, palpable gap |
| **Physical exam** | **Thompson test:** Squeeze calf — absence of plantarflexion indicates rupture |
| **Imaging** | MRI or ultrasound if diagnosis uncertain |
| **Treatment** | Surgical repair (younger, active patients) vs. functional bracing with structured rehabilitation (older patients, sedentary); both approaches have similar functional outcomes in some populations |

---

## 12.9 Return-to-Play Considerations

| Principle | Application |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Pain-free range of motion** | Required before return to sport |
| **Near-normal strength** | >=90% compared to uninjured side |
| **Sport-specific drills** | Progressive return without pain or swelling |
| **Mental readiness** | Confidence in injured limb; address fear of re-injury |
| **Gradual progression** | Increase intensity, duration, and complexity gradually |
| **Protective equipment** | Bracing/taping as appropriate during initial return |

# Chapter 13: Fibromyalgia and Systemic Lupus Erythematosus

## 13.1 Fibromyalgia

### Overview

Fibromyalgia is a chronic pain syndrome characterized by widespread musculoskeletal pain, fatigue, sleep disturbances, cognitive difficulties ("fibro fog"), and various somatic symptoms. It affects 2-4% of the population, predominantly women (female:male ratio 2-3:1). The pathophysiology involves central sensitization — abnormal pain processing in the central nervous system with alterations in neurotransmitters (serotonin, norepinephrine, dopamine, substance P). Functional MRI studies demonstrate augmented pain processing in the brain. [^40^] [^41^]

### 2016 ACR Revised Diagnostic Criteria [^40^]

| Component | Assessment |
|-----------|-----------|
| **Widespread Pain Index (WPI)** | Count of painful areas from 19 specified body regions (0-19) |
| **Symptom Severity (SS) scale** | Fatigue, waking unrefreshed, cognitive symptoms (each scored 0-3); plus somatic symptoms (0-3) |
| **Diagnosis** | WPI >=7 AND SS >=5 OR WPI 4-6 AND SS >=9 |
| **Duration** | Symptoms present for >=3 months |
| **Exclusion** | No other disorder that would explain the pain (though fibromyalgia may coexist with other conditions) |

**Important:** Fibromyalgia commonly coexists with rheumatoid arthritis, SLE, osteoarthritis, and other conditions. A patient can have both fibromyalgia and another rheumatic disease — diagnosis of one does not exclude the other.

---

### Multimodal Treatment Approach

**No single treatment is effective alone; combination therapy addressing multiple domains is essential.** [^40^] [^41^] Treatment goals focus on improving function and quality of life rather than eliminating all pain.

---

### Non-Pharmacologic Therapy (Foundation of Treatment)

| Modality | Evidence | Details |
|----------|----------|---------|
| **Patient education** | Strong | Understanding fibromyalgia as a real, treatable condition; self-management programs |
| **Exercise** | Strong (Grade A) | Low-impact aerobic exercise (walking, swimming, cycling); gradual progression; "start low, go slow"; Tai chi (strong evidence); aquatic therapy |
| **Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)** | Strong (Grade A) | Addresses catastrophizing, fear-avoidance, pain coping; short and medium-term benefits [^41^] |
| **Sleep hygiene** | Moderate | Regular schedule, optimal sleep environment, treat sleep apnea/RLS |
| **Physical therapy** | Moderate | Graded exercise programs, gentle stretching |
| **Mindfulness-based stress reduction** | Moderate | Meditation, relaxation techniques |
| **Pacing** | Moderate | Avoid "push-crash" cycles; balance activity and rest |

---

### Pharmacologic Therapy

#### FDA-Approved Agents

| Agent | Class | Dosing | Key Adverse Effects | Notes |
|-------|-------|--------|---------------------|-------|
| **Duloxetine** (Cymbalta) | SNRI | 30-60 mg daily | Nausea, dry mouth, constipation, dizziness | Also treats comorbid depression/anxiety; start 30 mg x 1 week |
| **Milnacipran** (Savella) | SNRI | 12.5 mg day 1, titrate to 50-100 mg BID | Nausea, headache, constipation, sweating, tachycardia | Titrate slowly over 1 week to minimize GI side effects |
| **Pregabalin** (Lyrica) | Alpha-2-delta ligand | 150-450 mg/day in 2-3 divided doses | Dizziness, somnolence, weight gain, peripheral edema | Also improves sleep quality; Schedule V controlled substance |

#### Other Commonly Used Evidence-Based Agents

| Agent | Evidence | Dosing | Notes |
|-------|----------|--------|-------|
| **Amitriptyline** (TCA) | Strong (Grade A) | 10-50 mg at bedtime | Improves sleep and pain; anticholinergic side effects (dry mouth, constipation, sedation); often first-line in clinical practice |
| **Cyclobenzaprine** | Moderate | 5-10 mg at bedtime | Similar to amitriptyline; muscle relaxant properties |
| **Gabapentin** | Moderate | 300-2400 mg/day in divided doses | Similar mechanism to pregabalin; less studied but widely used |
| **Tramadol** | Limited | 50-100 mg up to QID | Short-term use for flares only; avoid long-term |

#### Not Recommended

| Agent | Rationale |
|-------|-----------|
| **Opioids** | Limited evidence; significant risks (dependence, hyperalgesia, constipation, sedation); not recommended for fibromyalgia |
| **NSAIDs** | Not effective for central pain mechanisms in fibromyalgia |
| **Corticosteroids** | No efficacy; significant toxicity |

### Treatment Principles

1. Start with lowest effective dose; titrate slowly
2. Combine non-pharmacologic with pharmacologic therapy
3. Address comorbid conditions (depression, anxiety, sleep disorders, IBS, migraine)
4. Set realistic goals (pain reduction, improved function — not cure)
5. Regular follow-up every 4-8 weeks initially; adjust treatment as needed
6. Consider referral to pain management or rheumatology for refractory cases

## 13.2 Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE)

### Overview

Systemic lupus erythematosus is a chronic, multisystem autoimmune disease characterized by immune dysregulation, autoantibody production, and immune complex deposition causing inflammation and tissue damage. It predominantly affects women (female:male ratio 9:1), especially during reproductive years (peak onset 15-45 years). Disease course is characterized by flares and remissions, with the potential to affect virtually any organ system. [^42^] [^43^]

### Key Autoantibodies

| Antibody | Prevalence | Clinical Significance |
|----------|-----------|----------------------|
| **ANA** | >95% | Screening test; low specificity (present in other autoimmune diseases, infections, healthy individuals) |
| **Anti-dsDNA** | 70-80% | Relatively specific; correlates with disease activity and lupus nephritis |
| **Anti-Smith (Sm)** | 20-30% | Highly specific for SLE; does not correlate with disease activity |
| **Anti-Ro/SSA, Anti-La/SSB** | 30-40% | Associated with neonatal lupus, Sjogren's overlap, photosensitivity, subacute cutaneous lupus |
| **Antiphospholipid antibodies** | 30-40% | Lupus anticoagulant, anticardiolipin, anti-beta2-GPI; associated with thrombosis, pregnancy loss |
| **Low complement (C3, C4)** | During flares | Indicates active disease/consumption; useful for monitoring |

---

### 2019 EULAR/ACR Classification Criteria [^44^] [^45^]

**Entry Criterion:** Positive ANA (titer >=1:80 on HEp-2 cells) at least once.

**Weighted Criteria (7 clinical domains + 3 immunologic domains):**

| Domain | Criteria | Weight |
|--------|----------|--------|
| **Constitutional** | Fever | 2 |
| **Hematologic** | Leukopenia | 3 |
| | Thrombocytopenia | 4 |
| | Autoimmune hemolysis | 4 |
| **Neuropsychiatric** | Delirium | 2 |
| | Psychosis | 3 |
| | Seizure | 5 |
| **Mucocutaneous** | Non-scarring alopecia | 2 |
| | Oral ulcers | 2 |
| | Subacute cutaneous or discoid lupus | 4 |
| | Acute cutaneous lupus | 6 |
| **Serosal** | Pleural or pericardial effusion | 5 |
| | Acute pericarditis | 6 |
| **Musculoskeletal** | Joint involvement | 6 |
| **Renal** | Proteinuria >0.5 g/day | 4 |
| | Active urinary sediment | 4 |
| | Class II or V lupus nephritis | 8 |
| | Class III or IV lupus nephritis | 10 |
| **Antiphospholipid antibodies** | Positive | 2 |
| **Complement proteins** | Low C3 or C4 | 3 |
| | Low C3 AND C4 | 4 |
| **SLE-specific antibodies** | Anti-dsDNA | 6 |
| | Anti-Sm | 6 |

**Classification:** ANA positive + total weighted score >=10. Operating characteristics: sensitivity 96.1%, specificity 93.4% — superior to previous ACR 1997 and SLICC 2012 criteria. [^44^]

---

### Red Flags / Emergency Features in SLE

| Feature | Concern | Action |
|---------|---------|--------|
| **Acute nephritis** (rising creatinine, active urine sediment, heavy proteinuria) | Lupus nephritis flare | **URGENT** — nephrology referral, possible renal biopsy |
| **Seizures, psychosis, severe headache** | Neuropsychiatric SLE | **URGENT** — neuroimaging, neurology consultation |
| **Acute chest pain, dyspnea** | Pericarditis, pleuritis, PE | **URGENT** — cardiac workup, imaging |
| **Acute vision loss, severe red eye** | Retinal vasculitis, optic neuritis, scleritis | **EMERGENT** — ophthalmology same day |
| **Acute limb ischemia** | Vasculitis or antiphospholipid thrombosis | **EMERGENCY** — vascular surgery, anticoagulation |
| **Severe pancytopenia** | Bone marrow suppression, autoimmune cytopenias | Urgent hematology evaluation |

---

### Organ Involvement and Management

#### Lupus Nephritis (LN)

- Occurs in 50% of SLE patients; major cause of morbidity and mortality
- **Diagnosis:** Urinalysis, urine protein/creatinine ratio, serum creatinine, complement levels, anti-dsDNA; **renal biopsy for classification and treatment guidance**
- **ISN/RPS Classification:** Class I (minimal mesangial) through Class VI (advanced sclerosing)
- **2024 ACR Guidelines for LN:** [^42^] [^43^]

| Class | Treatment |
|-------|-----------|
| **Class I/II (mild)** | Hydroxychloroquine; low-dose steroids if proteinuric; ACE inhibitor |
| **Class III/IV (proliferative)** | High-dose glucocorticoids + mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) OR cyclophosphamide; consider belimumab or voclosporin addition |
| **Class V (membranous)** | MMF + glucocorticoids; calcineurin inhibitor if refractory |
| **Maintenance** | MMF or azathioprine + low-dose glucocorticoids; hydroxychloroquine |
| **Refractory** | Rituximab; consider repeat biopsy |
| **All LN** | Blood pressure goal <130/80; ACE inhibitor/ARB for proteinuria; SGLT2 inhibitors for proteinuria reduction; statins for dyslipidemia |

**Monitoring:** Proteinuria, renal function, complement, anti-dsDNA every 1-3 months initially.

#### Cutaneous Lupus

| Type | Features | Treatment |
|------|----------|-----------|
| **Acute cutaneous** | Malar rash (butterfly), photosensitive | Sun protection, topical TCS, HCQ |
| **Subacute cutaneous (SCLE)** | Annular or papulosquamous lesions; anti-Ro+ | Sun protection, topical TCS, HCQ, dapsone |
| **Discoid (DLE)** | Scarring, atrophy, dyspigmentation; scalp involvement causes permanent alopecia | Potent topical TCS, intralesional TCS, HCQ, methotrexate |

#### Neuropsychiatric SLE (NPSLE)

- Spectrum: headache, seizures, psychosis, cerebrovascular disease, cognitive dysfunction, mood disorders, myelitis, aseptic meningitis
- **Evaluation:** Exclude other causes (infection, medication, metabolic); MRI brain; CSF analysis as indicated
- **Treatment:** Corticosteroids, immunosuppressants (cyclophosphamide for severe cases); anticoagulation if antiphospholipid-associated

#### Hematologic Manifestations

- **Anemia:** Anemia of chronic disease most common; autoimmune hemolytic anemia (Coombs positive)
- **Leukopenia:** Lymphopenia common; neutropenia less common
- **Thrombocytopenia:** Immune-mediated; may require IVIG, rituximab, or thrombopoietin agonists

### General SLE Management

#### Hydroxychloroquine (HCQ) — Foundation of SLE Therapy [^42^] [^43^]

| Parameter | Recommendation |
|-----------|---------------|
| **Dosing** | 200-400 mg daily (<=5 mg/kg/day real body weight to minimize retinal toxicity) |
| **Indication** | **Recommended for ALL SLE patients unless contraindicated** |
| **Benefits** | Reduces flares, improves long-term survival, decreases organ damage, reduces thrombosis risk, improves lipid profile, treats cutaneous lupus |
| **Monitoring** | Baseline eye exam; annual screening after 5 years (earlier if high risk: kidney disease, tamoxifen use, high cumulative dose) |

HCQ is the **key drug for SLE** — virtually all patients should be on HCQ unless contraindicated. It is one of the safest immunomodulatory agents and improves virtually every outcome in SLE.

#### Glucocorticoids

| Use | Approach |
|-----|----------|
| **General principle** | Minimize dose and duration; aim for <5-7.5 mg/day prednisone equivalent |
| **Bridge therapy** | Low-dose while waiting for immunosuppressant onset |
| **Flares** | Dose escalation for mild-moderate flares |
| **Severe organ involvement** | High-dose IV methylprednisolone 500-1000 mg IV x 3 days (pulse therapy) |

#### Immunosuppressive and Biologic Agents

| Agent | Role in SLE | Key Notes |
|-------|------------|-----------|
| **Mycophenolate mofetil (MMF)** | First-line for moderate-severe disease, lupus nephritis | 1.5-3 g/day in divided doses; teratogenic |
| **Azathioprine** | Maintenance therapy, mild-moderate disease | Safer in pregnancy; monitor TPMT activity |
| **Cyclophosphamide** | Severe organ involvement (LN, CNS) | IV monthly pulses; gonadotoxic; hemorrhagic cystitis risk |
| **Methotrexate** | Arthritis, cutaneous lupus | Weekly dosing; folic acid supplementation |
| **Belimumab** (Benlysta) | FDA-approved for SLE and LN; anti-BLyS | Reduces flares; allows steroid sparing; IV or SC |
| **Anifrolumab** (Saphnelo) | FDA-approved for moderate-severe SLE; anti-type I IFN receptor | Effective for cutaneous and musculoskeletal manifestations; IV every 4 weeks |
| **Rituximab** | Refractory disease (off-label) | B-cell depletion; especially for hematologic and renal manifestations |
| **Voclosporin** | LN (in combination with MMF) | Calcineurin inhibitor; improved renal response rates |

#### Antiphospholipid Syndrome Management

| Clinical Scenario | Management |
|-------------------|-----------|
| **APS with thrombosis** | Warfarin (INR 2-3) or DOAC (controversial; warfarin preferred for arterial thrombosis) |
| **APS with pregnancy** | LMWH + low-dose aspirin |
| **aPL positive, no thrombosis** | HCQ (reduces thrombosis risk); low-dose aspirin if high-risk profile |
| **Catastrophic APS** | Heparin + high-dose steroids + plasma exchange/IVIG; mortality 30-50% |

### Disease Activity Monitoring

| Tool | Components | Use |
|------|-----------|-----|
| **SLEDAI-2K** | 24 items across organ systems; score 0-105 | Most widely used; captures active disease |
| **BILAG** | Separate assessment of 8 organ systems (A-E) | More granular; guides treatment decisions |
| **PGA** | Physician global assessment (0-3) | Simple; correlates with other indices |

**Laboratory monitoring:** Complement levels (C3, C4), anti-dsDNA titers, CBC, creatinine, urinalysis every 1-3 months during active disease, then every 3-6 months.

**Target:** Remission or low disease activity state; treat-to-target approach.

### Pregnancy in SLE [^42^] [^46^] [^47^]

SLE pregnancy requires multidisciplinary management involving rheumatology and high-risk obstetrics.

#### Preconception Planning

| Principle | Details |
|-----------|---------|
| **Timing** | Plan pregnancy during remission or low disease activity (>=6 months preferred) |
| **HCQ** | **Continue** — strongly recommended; reduces flares and improves pregnancy outcomes |
| **Stop teratogenic medications** | Methotrexate (stop 3 months before), mycophenolate (stop 6 weeks before), cyclophosphamide |
| **Transition to pregnancy-safe agents** | Azathioprine, tacrolimus, cyclosporine |
| **Prednisone** | Keep <10-20 mg/day if possible |

#### Medications in Pregnancy

| Medication | Recommendation |
|-----------|---------------|
| **Hydroxychloroquine** | Strongly recommended — continue |
| **Azathioprine** | Compatible — safe |
| **Tacrolimus / Cyclosporine** | Compatible — safe |
| **Prednisone** | Compatible — minimize dose |
| **Belimumab** | Limited data; may be used if needed |
| **Rituximab** | May be used for organ/life-threatening disease; avoid 2nd/3rd trimester if possible |
| **Methotrexate** | **Contraindicated** — stop before pregnancy |
| **Mycophenolate** | **Contraindicated** — stop 6 weeks before |
| **Cyclophosphamide** | **Contraindicated** — avoid |
| **Warfarin** | Contraindicated in 1st trimester; may be used in 2nd/3rd |
| **LMWH** | Safe — preferred for APS in pregnancy |
| **NSAIDs** | Avoid in 3rd trimester; may use earlier with caution |

#### Pregnancy Complications and Monitoring

| Complication | Risk / Management |
|-------------|-------------------|
| **Preeclampsia** | Higher risk; low-dose aspirin for preeclampsia prevention in high-risk patients (start preconception or by 16 weeks) |
| **Preterm birth, fetal loss, IUGR** | Risk stratification: aPL positivity, lupus nephritis, active disease increase risk |
| **Neonatal lupus** | Transient cutaneous rash, cytopenias, hepatitis; associated with anti-Ro/SSA |
| **Congenital heart block (CHB)** | Irreversible; risk 1-2% (higher if prior affected child); associated with anti-Ro/SSA and anti-La/SSB |
| **Fetal echocardiography** | At 18-24 weeks if anti-Ro/SSA positive (detect CHB early) |
| **HCQ benefit** | **HCQ reduces CHB risk** — continue throughout pregnancy |

#### Neonatal Lupus

| Feature | Details |
|---------|---------|
| **Cause** | Transplacental passage of maternal anti-Ro/SSA and/or anti-La/SSB antibodies |
| **Cutaneous** | Transient photosensitive rash (typically resolves by 6 months as maternal antibodies clear) |
| **Hematologic** | Transient cytopenias |
| **Hepatic** | Transient hepatitis |
| **Cardiac** | **Congenital heart block** — irreversible; may require pacemaker; risk 1-2% in anti-Ro+ mothers |
| **Recurrence risk** | 15-20% if prior affected child |

---

### Cardiovascular Risk Management

- SLE patients have **2-10x increased risk** of cardiovascular disease compared to the general population
- Traditional risk calculators underestimate risk in SLE
- **Aggressive CV risk factor modification:** Blood pressure control (<130/80), statins for hyperlipidemia, diabetes management, smoking cessation, HCQ (improves lipid profile), aspirin for high-risk patients
- Annual cardiovascular risk assessment recommended

## 13.3 When to Seek Emergency Care

### Fibromyalgia
- Fibromyalgia itself is not life-threatening
- Seek care if new, different, or worsening symptoms that may indicate another condition
- Suicidal ideation requires immediate evaluation

### Systemic Lupus Erythematosus
- New neurologic symptoms (seizure, confusion, severe headache, weakness)
- Chest pain, severe shortness of breath
- Decreased urine output, blood in urine
- Severe abdominal pain
- High fever with immunosuppression
- Signs of blood clot (leg swelling, chest pain, stroke symptoms)
- Vision changes or severe eye pain
- Pregnancy with lupus flare symptoms

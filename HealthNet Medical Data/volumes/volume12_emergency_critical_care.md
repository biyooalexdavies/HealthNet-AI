# Chapter 1: ACLS Protocols

## 1.1 Overview

Advanced Cardiac Life Support (ACLS) encompasses the systematic, evidence-based approach to adult cardiac arrest, peri-arrest arrhythmias, and post-cardiac arrest care. The American Heart Association (AHA) 2020 and 2025 Guidelines provide the current evidence-based recommendations for resuscitation [^567^][^568^][^569^]. The foundational principle of ACLS is the **Chain of Survival**, which now includes six links: (1) immediate recognition and activation, (2) early CPR, (3) rapid defibrillation, (4) effective advanced care, (5) post-cardiac arrest care, and (6) recovery [^571^].

Cardiac arrest in adults most commonly results from ventricular fibrillation (VF) or pulseless ventricular tachycardia (pVT) in the out-of-hospital setting, while in-hospital arrests more frequently present as non-shockable rhythms (asystole or pulseless electrical activity, PEA). The distinction between shockable and non-shockable rhythms drives the initial algorithmic approach, though all rhythms share common elements of high-quality CPR and pharmacologic support.

## 1.2 Cardiac Arrest Algorithms

### 1.2.1 Universal Adult Cardiac Arrest Algorithm

**Basic Life Support (BLS) Foundation:**

All ACLS interventions build upon high-quality BLS. The provider must:
- Verify scene safety before approaching
- Assess responsiveness (no breathing or only gasping, no definite pulse within 10 seconds)
- Activate emergency response and retrieve automated external defibrillator (AED)
- Begin immediate chest compressions: push hard (at least 2 inches/5 cm), push fast (100-120/minute), allow complete chest recoil
- Minimize interruptions (goal chest compression fraction >80%)
- Change compressor every 2 minutes to prevent fatigue-induced quality degradation
- Use 30:2 compression-ventilation ratio without an advanced airway
- Once an advanced airway is placed: deliver 1 breath every 6 seconds (10 breaths/minute) with continuous, uninterrupted compressions [^567^][^568^]

### 1.2.2 Shockable Rhythms: VF and Pulseless VT

**Step-by-Step Algorithm:**

1. **Confirm pulselessness** and begin CPR immediately; attach monitor/defibrillator
2. **Shock as soon as defibrillator available:** Biphasic 120-200J (or manufacturer recommendation); monophasic 360J
3. **Resume CPR immediately** for 2 minutes after shock (do not pause to assess rhythm)
4. **Establish IV/IO access** during CPR; give epinephrine 1 mg IV/IO every 3-5 minutes
5. **Consider advanced airway placement** plus quantitative waveform capnography
6. **After the second shock:** Administer amiodarone 300 mg IV/IO bolus; may repeat 150 mg once for persistent VF/pVT
7. **Lidocaine alternative:** 1-1.5 mg/kg first dose; 0.5-0.75 mg/kg second dose (maximum 3 mg/kg cumulative)
8. **Continue 2-minute cycles** of CPR, rhythm check, shock if indicated, and treat reversible causes

**Key principle:** Early defibrillation is the single most important intervention for shockable rhythms. Each minute of delay reduces survival by 7-10% [^567^].

### 1.2.3 Non-Shockable Rhythms: Asystole and PEA

**Step-by-Step Algorithm:**

1. **Start CPR immediately**; give high-flow oxygen; attach monitor
2. **Verify rhythm** in two leads; check for loose leads or equipment failure
3. **Continue CPR** for 2-minute cycles
4. **Give epinephrine as soon as feasible** (1 mg IV/IO every 3-5 minutes) -- early epinephrine is particularly critical for non-shockable rhythms [^571^]
5. **Consider advanced airway** once IV/IO access established
6. **Treat reversible causes aggressively** (H's and T's; see Section 1.6)
7. **Continue 2-minute cycles** with rhythm checks

**2025 AHA Key Updates for Non-Shockable Rhythms:**
- IV access preferred over IO; IO is reasonable if IV cannot be obtained quickly [^570^]
- Early epinephrine administration is specifically emphasized for non-shockable rhythms
- Routine sodium bicarbonate, calcium, and magnesium are NOT recommended unless specific indication exists (e.g., hyperkalemia, hypomagnesemia, toxicologic arrest)
- Mechanical CPR: routine use not recommended; may be considered when sustained high-quality manual CPR is not possible
- Corticosteroids are NOT routinely recommended in cardiac arrest [^570^]

### 1.2.4 High-Quality CPR Standards

| Parameter | Target | Rationale |
|-----------|--------|-----------|
| Rate | 100-120 compressions/minute | Rates <100 or >120 associated with worse outcomes |
| Depth | At least 2 inches (5 cm); avoid >2.4 inches (6 cm) | Insufficient depth reduces cardiac output; excessive depth causes injury |
| Recoil | Allow complete chest recoil; do not lean on chest | Incomplete recoil impedes venous return |
| Interruptions | Minimize to <10 seconds for rhythm checks, pulse checks, or airway management | Interruptions reduce coronary and cerebral perfusion |
| Compression fraction | Goal >80% | Higher fraction correlates with ROSC rates |
| Ventilation (no advanced airway) | 30:2 ratio | Balances perfusion and oxygenation |
| Ventilation (advanced airway) | 1 breath every 6 seconds; do not pause compressions | Continuous compressions maximize perfusion |
| Switch compressors | Every 2 minutes or sooner if fatigued | Fatigue significantly reduces compression quality |
| Quantitative waveform capnography | If ETCO2 <10 mmHg or decreasing, reassess CPR quality | ETCO2 is a real-time marker of cardiac output during CPR |

[^567^][^568^]

### 1.2.5 Defibrillation Principles

- **Biphasic waveform:** Use manufacturer-recommended energy (typically 120-200J for initial shock). If the manufacturer's recommendation is unknown, use the maximum available. For subsequent shocks, use an equivalent or higher energy level.
- **Monophasic waveform:** 360J for all shocks
- **Pediatric (8 years or <8):** 2-4 J/kg initial; 4 J/kg for subsequent shocks; higher doses (up to 10 J/kg) may be considered, though not to exceed adult maximum doses
- **Paddle/pad placement:** Anterolateral (sternum-apex) is standard; anteroposterior may be used when anterolateral is not feasible or for cardioversion
- **Self-adhesive defibrillation pads** should be used to facilitate rapid, repeated shocks and reduce provider exposure during CPR [^567^]

### 1.2.6 Medications in Cardiac Arrest

| Drug | Dose | Timing | Indication | Notes |
|------|------|--------|------------|-------|
| Epinephrine | 1 mg IV/IO | Every 3-5 minutes | All cardiac arrest rhythms | Alpha- and beta-adrenergic effects; vasoconstriction improves coronary/cerebral perfusion pressure |
| Amiodarone | 300 mg IV/IO bolus, then 150 mg | After 3rd shock/2nd cycle | Refractory VF/pVT only | Vasodilation risk; hypotension if given too rapidly |
| Lidocaine | 1-1.5 mg/kg IV/IO, then 0.5-0.75 mg/kg | After 3rd shock | Alternative to amiodarone | Maximum 3 mg/kg cumulative |
| Atropine | **NOT recommended in cardiac arrest** | N/A | Removed from cardiac arrest algorithm | No evidence of benefit in asystole or PEA |

[^567^][^568^][^570^]

## 1.3 Post-Cardiac Arrest Care

### 1.3.1 Immediate Priorities After Return of Spontaneous Circulation (ROSC)

1. **Obtain 12-lead ECG** immediately to assess for STEMI
2. **Manage airway** (early endotracheal tube placement with waveform capnography)
3. **Optimize oxygenation:** Target SpO2 92-98%; PaCO2 35-45 mmHg (avoid both hypoxia and hyperoxia)
4. **Achieve hemodynamic stability:** SBP >90 mmHg or MAP >65 mmHg
5. **Implement temperature control** (formerly Targeted Temperature Management, TTM) if the patient remains comatose (does not follow verbal commands) [^566^][^569^]

### 1.3.2 Temperature Control/TTM

- **Target temperature:** 32-37.5 degrees C for at least 36 hours
- **Common methods:** Endovascular cooling catheter, surface cooling devices (cooling blankets, ice packs)
- **Temperature monitoring:** Core temperature via esophageal, bladder, or rectal probe
- **Evidence:** The TTM2 Trial showed no survival benefit of 33 degrees C compared with normothermia at 36-37.5 degrees C with active fever prevention [^569^][^573^]
- **Fever prevention is essential:** Normothermia (36-37.5 degrees C) with aggressive prevention of fever is an acceptable alternative to active cooling
- **Controlled rewarming:** 0.25-0.5 degrees C/hour to prevent hemodynamic instability
- **Shivering management:** Shivering increases metabolic demand and must be prevented/treated (measures include sedation, analgesia, counter-warming of face/hands, buspirone, magnesium, and neuromuscular blockade if needed) [^569^]

### 1.3.3 H's and T's: Reversible Causes of Cardiac Arrest

| Category | Condition | Clues/Assessment |
|----------|-----------|-----------------|
| **H's** | Hypovolemia | History of hemorrhage, trauma, burns; flat JVP; poor response to fluids/pressors |
| | Hypoxia | Pre-arrest respiratory distress; airway obstruction; inadequate ventilation |
| | Hydrogen ion (acidosis) | Severe metabolic acidosis (DKA, sepsis, renal failure); hypercapnia |
| | Hypo-/Hyperkalemia | Renal failure; diuretic use; peaked T waves (hyperK); U waves (hypoK) |
| | Hypothermia | Exposure history; Osborn J waves on ECG; core temperature |
| **T's** | Tension pneumothorax | Unilateral absent breath sounds, tracheal deviation, JVD, resistant to CPR/fluids |
| | Tamponade (cardiac) | Beck's triad (hypotension, JVD, muffled heart sounds); pulsus paradoxus; history of cancer, pericarditis, or penetrating chest trauma |
| | Toxins | Medication history; drug paraphernalia; altered pupils; drug-specific toxidromes |
| | Thrombosis (pulmonary) | Risk factors for PE; prior DVT/PE; right heart strain on ECG |
| | Thrombosis (coronary) | Cardiac history; STEMI on ECG post-ROSC |

[^567^]

## 1.4 Bradycardia Algorithm (With Pulse)

### 1.4.1 Step-by-Step Management

1. **Assess and maintain patent airway;** assist breathing if needed; administer oxygen
2. **Establish IV access;** place patient on monitor; obtain 12-lead ECG
3. **Assess for signs of poor perfusion:** hypotension, altered mental status, signs of shock, chest pain, acute heart failure
4. **Atropine:** 1 mg IV; may repeat every 3-5 minutes (maximum 3 mg total) [^571^]
5. **If atropine ineffective:** Begin transcutaneous pacing immediately
6. **If pacing delayed or ineffective:** Consider dopamine 5-20 mcg/kg/min infusion OR epinephrine 2-10 mcg/min infusion
7. **Consider expert consultation** for transvenous pacing
8. **Prepare for transvenous pacing** if transcutaneous pacing is not effective or sustainable

### 1.4.2 Indications for Intervention

- Heart rate typically <50/minute **with symptoms**
- High-grade AV block (second-degree type II, third-degree) regardless of rate
- Any symptomatic bradycardia with signs of poor perfusion [^571^][^572^]

**2025 Update:** Atropine dose increased from 0.5 mg to 1 mg per bolus; dopamine dosing range updated to 5-20 mcg/kg/min [^571^].

## 1.5 Tachycardia Algorithm (With Pulse)

### 1.5.1 Step 1: Assess Stability

**Unstable patients** (hypotension, altered mental status, signs of shock, chest pain, acute heart failure):
- **Immediate synchronized cardioversion**
- Synchronized cardioversion energy: Follow device-specific recommendations; typical starting doses: 75J, then 120J, then 150J (escalating)

**Stable patients:** Proceed to Step 2

### 1.5.2 Step 2: Identify Rhythm and Treat

| Rhythm | QRS Width | Regularity | Management |
|--------|-----------|------------|------------|
| SVT/AVNRT | Narrow (<0.12s) | Regular | Vagal maneuvers first; adenosine 6 mg rapid IVP with flush; may repeat 12 mg x2 |
| Atrial fibrillation | Narrow (<0.12s) | Irregular | Rate control (diltiazem, beta-blockers); anticoagulation assessment |
| Atrial flutter | Narrow (<0.12s) | Regular | Rate control; consider adenosine for diagnosis (reveals flutter waves); cardioversion |
| VT (monomorphic) | Wide (>0.12s) | Regular | Consider adenosine 6 mg if uncertain of SVT with aberrancy; amiodarone 150 mg/100 mL over 10 min; procainamide; expert consultation |
| Polymorphic VT/Torsades | Wide (>0.12s) | Irregular | Defibrillation dose (NOT synchronized); magnesium 2 g IV; correct QT-prolonging drugs and electrolytes |

[^571^][^572^]

### 1.5.3 Adenosine Administration

- **Initial dose:** 6 mg rapid IV push followed immediately by rapid saline flush (20 mL)
- **Second/third doses:** 12 mg; may repeat 12 mg once more (maximum cumulative 30 mg)
- **Half-life:** <10 seconds (extremely rapid metabolism by red blood cells)
- **Patient counseling:** Warn patient about transient flushing, chest discomfort, dyspnea, and sense of impending doom -- all brief and self-limited
- **Contraindications/cautions:** Second- or third-degree AV block (without pacemaker), sick sinus syndrome, known hypersensitivity, decompensated heart failure, severe hypotension, asthma (may cause bronchospasm)

## 1.6 Special Considerations

### 1.6.1 Cardiac Arrest in Pregnancy

- **Perform manual left uterine displacement** (move uterus to the patient's left) to relieve aortocaval compression; this is preferred over left lateral tilt
- **Place IV access above the diaphragm** (upper extremities)
- **If receiving magnesium sulfate, stop immediately** and give calcium gluconate/chloride
- **If no ROSC within 5 minutes** of resuscitation, consider emergency perimortem cesarean delivery to improve maternal cardiac output and fetal survival
- **Additional considerations:** Higher oxygen requirements, increased risk of aspiration, potential for amniotic fluid embolism [^571^]

### 1.6.2 2025 AHA Guideline Summary of Key Changes

| Change | Previous | Updated 2025 |
|--------|----------|-------------|
| Chain of Survival | 5 links | 6 links (added "Recovery") |
| Vascular access | No preference | IV preferred over IO |
| Non-shockable rhythms | Give epinephrine | Emphasize early epinephrine |
| Post-arrest SpO2 target | 94-98% | 92-98% |
| Atropine dose | 0.5 mg | 1 mg per bolus |
| Dopamine dosing | 2-20 mcg/kg/min | 5-20 mcg/kg/min |

[^571^]

## 1.7 Red Flags

> **CRITICAL / IMMEDIATE INTERVENTION REQUIRED:**
- Pulseless patient = cardiac arrest; initiate CPR immediately
- Shockable rhythm (VF/pVT) = defibrillate immediately; every minute counts
- ROSC with STEMI on ECG = emergent percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI)
- Cardiac arrest in pregnancy >20 weeks gestation = manual left uterine displacement; perimortem C-section at 5 minutes if no ROSC
- Bradycardia with hypotension and altered mental status = prepare for pacing
- Wide-complex tachycardia with hemodynamic instability = immediate synchronized cardioversion

# Chapter 2: Acute Coronary Syndrome

## 2.1 Overview

Acute Coronary Syndrome (ACS) encompasses a spectrum of clinical presentations caused by acute myocardial ischemia, including ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), non-ST-elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), and unstable angina (UA). The central management goal is rapid risk stratification, prompt antithrombotic therapy, and timely reperfusion for STEMI. ACS affects approximately 800,000 Americans annually, with mortality highest in the first hours to days following presentation [^572^][^571^].

## 2.2 STEMI: ST-Elevation Myocardial Infarction

### 2.2.1 Definition and Diagnosis

STEMI is defined by ST-segment elevation in two or more contiguous leads (>=1 mm in all leads except V2-V3: >=2 mm in men >=40 years, >=2.5 mm in men <40 years, >=1.5 mm in women) or new/presumed new left bundle branch block (LBBB) with clinical evidence of acute myocardial ischemia [^571^].

### 2.2.2 Time-Critical Management Goals

| Metric | Target Time | Rationale |
|--------|-------------|-----------|
| Door-to-balloon (primary PCI) | <90 minutes | Myocardial salvage; mortality increases with delay |
| Door-to-needle (fibrinolysis) | <30 minutes | Only if PCI not available within 120 minutes |
| First medical contact-to-device | <120 minutes | Regional systems of care target |
| Best practice | Bypass ED to cath lab | When PCI team immediately available |

[^571^][^572^]

### 2.2.3 Reperfusion Strategy

- **Primary PCI is preferred** when available within 120 minutes of first medical contact
- **Fibrinolysis** should be administered if PCI cannot be performed within 120 minutes and there are no contraindications
- **Prehospital fibrinolysis** may be considered if transport delays are expected to exceed PCI availability
- **Rescue PCI** (urgent PCI after failed fibrinolysis) is indicated if <50% ST-resolution at 60-90 minutes post-lysis or ongoing/recurrent ischemia

### 2.2.4 Fibrinolytic Therapy: Tenecteplase

**Tenecteplase (TNK-tPA)** is the preferred fibrinolytic agent due to its single bolus administration and fibrin specificity [^571^]:

| Weight | Dose | Administration |
|--------|------|---------------|
| <60 kg | 30 mg | IV bolus over 5 seconds |
| 60-69 kg | 35 mg | IV bolus over 5 seconds |
| 70-79 kg | 40 mg | IV bolus over 5 seconds |
| 80-89 kg | 45 mg | IV bolus over 5 seconds |
| >=90 kg | 50 mg | IV bolus over 5 seconds |

**Absolute Contraindications to Fibrinolysis:**
- Prior hemorrhagic stroke at any time
- Ischemic stroke within 3 months
- Known intracranial vascular lesion (AVM, aneurysm) or malignancy
- Active bleeding or bleeding diathesis (excluding menses)
- Suspected aortic dissection
- Intracranial or intraspinal surgery within 2 months
- Severe closed head or facial trauma within 3 months

**Relative Contraindications:**
- Uncontrolled blood pressure >180/110 mmHg
- Current anticoagulation with elevated INR
- Pregnancy or within 1 week postpartum
- >10 minutes of CPR with suspected traumatic injuries
- Recent internal bleeding (2-4 weeks)
- Non-compressible vascular punctures [^571^]

### 2.2.5 Antithrombotic Therapy for STEMI

| Agent | Loading Dose | Maintenance | Notes |
|-------|-------------|-------------|-------|
| Aspirin | 325 mg (if not already given) | 81 mg daily | All ACS patients unless true aspirin allergy |
| Clopidogrel | 600 mg | 75 mg daily | Use if ticagrelor/prasugrel not available |
| Ticagrelor | 180 mg | 90 mg BID | Preferred; reversible P2Y12; dyspnea side effect |
| Prasugrel | 60 mg | 10 mg daily | Avoid if prior stroke/TIA; age >75 or weight <60 kg |

**Anticoagulation during PCI:** Unfractionated heparin (UFH) or bivalirudin per activated clotting time (ACT) monitoring. Glycoprotein IIb/IIIa inhibitors (eptifibatide, tirofiban) for selected cases with high thrombus burden or no-reflow phenomenon.

**Duration of dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT):** Minimum 12 months following ACS, balancing ischemic risk against bleeding risk [^572^].

## 2.3 NSTEMI and Unstable Angina

### 2.3.1 Risk Stratification

NSTEMI/UA management is guided by risk assessment:

| Risk Tool | Components | Application |
|-----------|-----------|-------------|
| TIMI Score | Age, risk factors, CAD history, aspirin use, severe angina, ST changes, elevated troponin | Prognostic; 14-day event rate ranges 5-41% |
| GRACE Score | Age, HR, SBP, creatinine, Killip class, ST deviation, cardiac arrest, troponin | Predicts in-hospital and 6-month mortality |

**High-risk features** mandating early invasive strategy:
- Elevated cardiac troponin
- Dynamic ST-segment changes (depression or transient elevation)
- Hemodynamic instability
- Recurrent ischemia despite medical therapy
- Heart failure or reduced LVEF
- Prior PCI within 6 months or prior CABG
- Sustained ventricular tachycardia

### 2.3.2 Management

1. **Aspirin** -- all patients unless contraindicated (chewable 325 mg load)
2. **P2Y12 inhibitor** -- ticagrelor or clopidogrel (addition to aspirin)
3. **Anticoagulation** -- enoxaparin, UFH, fondaparinux, or bivalirudin
4. **High-intensity statin** -- atorvastatin 40-80 mg or rosuvastatin 20-40 mg regardless of baseline LDL
5. **Beta-blockers** -- within 24 hours if no contraindications (active asthma, acute heart failure, shock, high-grade AV block)
6. **ACE inhibitors/ARBs** -- especially with anterior MI, LVEF <40%, or diabetes
7. **Early invasive strategy** for high-risk patients (cardiac catheterization within 24-72 hours)
8. **Conservative strategy** for low-risk patients after adequate risk stratification

### 2.3.3 Anticoagulation Regimens

| Agent | STEMI (Fibrinolysis) | STEMI (PCI) | NSTEMI |
|-------|---------------------|-------------|--------|
| UFH | 60 U/kg bolus (max 4000U) + 12 U/kg/hr infusion | Per ACT protocol | Weight-based infusion |
| Enoxaparin | 30 mg IV + 1 mg/kg SC q12h | 0.5-0.75 mg/kg IV bolus | 1 mg/kg SC q12h |
| Bivalirudin | Alternative | During PCI | During PCI |
| Fondaparinux | NOT for primary PCI | NOT recommended | 2.5 mg SC daily |

## 2.4 Red Flags / Emergency Features

> **CRITICAL:**
- **Cardiogenic shock:** SBP <90 mmHg, cool extremities, altered mental status, oliguria -- mortality 40-50% without intervention
- **Mechanical complications:** Papillary muscle rupture (new harsh holosystolic murmur with acute pulmonary edema), ventricular septal defect (step-up in O2 saturation from RA to RV), free wall rupture (electromechanical dissociation)
- **Malignant arrhythmias:** Sustained VT, VF -- immediate ACLS
- **Killip Class III-IV:** Acute pulmonary edema or cardiogenic shock
- **Recurrent ischemia** despite maximal medical therapy

## 2.5 Special Populations

**Elderly:** Higher bleeding risk; weight-based dosing of anticoagulants especially important; prasugrel generally avoided if >75 years; individualized approach to invasive strategy
**Women:** Higher complication rates with invasive management; careful attention to bleeding risk and contrast-induced nephropathy
**Diabetes:** Benefit from insulin-based glucose management (target 140-180 mg/dL); SGLT2 inhibitors should be held during acute illness
**Chronic kidney disease:** Dose-adjust anticoagulants; bivalirudin may be preferred; contrast risk-benefit assessment

# Chapter 3: Stroke Emergency

## 3.1 Overview

Stroke is the fifth leading cause of death and the leading cause of long-term disability in the United States. Acute stroke management is fundamentally time-dependent: an estimated 1.9 million neurons are lost each minute during a large vessel ischemic stroke [^190^][^191^]. The paradigm is "time is brain" -- rapid recognition, prehospital notification, streamlined ED evaluation, and timely reperfusion therapy are the cornerstones of modern stroke care.

## 3.2 Ischemic Stroke

### 3.2.1 Time-Critical Management

| Metric | Target | Rationale |
|--------|--------|-----------|
| Door-to-needle (IV thrombolysis) | <60 minutes | Neuronal loss accelerates with time |
| Door-to-imaging | <25 minutes | Imaging distinguishes ischemic vs hemorrhagic |
| Door-to-puncture (thrombectomy) | <90 minutes | Faster recanalization = better outcomes |

**Systems of care:** Prehospital stroke assessment tools (CPSS, RACE, LAMS) facilitate triage to stroke centers. Direct-to-CT protocols bypass the traditional ED bed assignment when possible [^571^].

### 3.2.2 IV Thrombolysis

**Tenecteplase (Preferred Agent):**
- **Dose:** 0.25 mg/kg IV bolus (maximum 25 mg) -- single bolus, no infusion
- **Window:** Within 4.5 hours of last known well (Class 1 recommendation) [^190^]
- **Advantages over alteplase:** Single bolus administration (no infusion pump needed), easier patient transfer, faster administration, non-inferior efficacy and safety demonstrated in multiple randomized trials

**Alteplase (tPA) -- Alternative:**
- **Dose:** 0.9 mg/kg IV (maximum 90 mg); 10% as bolus, remainder over 60 minutes
- **Same 4.5-hour window** [^190^]

**Extended Window (4.5-24 hours):**
- May be reasonable in selected patients with advanced imaging demonstrating salvageable penumbra tissue
- Useful for wake-up stroke when perfusion imaging shows mismatch between core infarct and penumbra
- Class 2a/2b recommendation depending on specific imaging criteria [^190^]

### 3.2.3 Blood Pressure Management Before and After Thrombolysis

- **Must be <185/110 mmHg before thrombolysis** can be administered
- **Maintain <180/105 mmHg** for 24 hours following thrombolysis
- **Agents:** Labetalol 10-20 mg IV, nicardipine infusion, or clevidipine infusion
- **Class III recommendation against** lowering SBP <140 mmHg post-tPA in mild-to-moderate stroke (may worsen outcomes) [^190^]

### 3.2.4 NIH Stroke Scale (NIHSS)

The NIHSS is a validated, reproducible neurological examination tool used for severity assessment and treatment decisions:

| Score Range | Severity | Clinical Implication |
|-------------|----------|---------------------|
| 0 | No stroke symptoms | May still require evaluation (covert stroke) |
| 1-4 | Minor stroke | May not be candidate for thrombolysis if improving |
| 5-15 | Moderate stroke | Standard candidate for IV thrombolysis |
| 16-20 | Moderate-severe stroke | Candidate for thrombolysis + thrombectomy |
| 21-42 | Severe stroke | Consider thrombectomy; guarded prognosis |

## 3.3 Mechanical Thrombectomy

### 3.3.1 Indications (Class 1 Recommendations)

**Anterior circulation large vessel occlusion (LVO):**
- **0-6 hours:** Definite recommendation for ICA or M1 MCA occlusion (multiple RCTs)
- **6-24 hours:** Selected patients based on DAWN/DEFUSE-3 criteria
  - Age <80 years, NIHSS >=10, infarct core <70 mL (measured by RAPID or similar automated software) with clinical-imaging mismatch
- **Large core infarcts (6-24 hours):** Class 1 for ASPECTS 3-5, NIHSS >=6, age <80 years (SELECT2, ANGEL-ASPECT trials) [^190^]

**Posterior circulation (basilar artery occlusion):**
- Class 1 within 24 hours for NIHSS >=10 with mild-to-moderate ischemic damage on imaging (ATTENTION, BAOCHE trials) [^190^]

### 3.3.2 Thrombectomy Criteria

- Pre-stroke modified Rankin Scale (mRS) 0-1 (functionally independent)
- Causative occlusion of internal carotid artery (ICA) or middle cerebral artery (M1 segment)
- Age >18 years
- NIHSS >=6
- ASPECTS >=6 (for early window patients)

## 3.4 Hemorrhagic Stroke

### 3.4.1 Intracerebral Hemorrhage (ICH)

**Immediate management priorities:**
- **Blood pressure management:** SBP target 140-160 mmHg (INTERACT-2 and ATACH-2 trial evidence); avoid excessive lowering that may compromise cerebral perfusion
- **Reverse anticoagulation immediately:** This is the highest priority in anticoagulant-associated ICH
  - Warfarin: Vitamin K 10 mg IV + 4-factor prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC) or fresh frozen plasma (FFP)
  - Dabigatran: Idarucizumab 5 g IV (two 2.5 g boluses)
  - Factor Xa inhibitors (apixaban, rivaroxaban): Andexanet alfa or 4-factor PCC if andexanet unavailable
- **Manage intracranial pressure:** Head of bed 30 degrees, osmotherapy (mannitol 0.25-1 g/kg or 3% hypertonic saline 250 mL bolus)
- **Neurosurgical consultation:** For hematoma evacuation if cerebellar hemorrhage >3 cm, lobar hemorrhage with mass effect, or clinical deterioration

### 3.4.2 Subarachnoid Hemorrhage (SAH)

**Diagnosis:**
- CT head: Sensitivity >95% within 6 hours of symptom onset
- Lumbar puncture if CT negative but high clinical suspicion (xanthochromia on spectrophotometry)
- CTA to identify aneurysm; digital subtraction angiography if CTA negative and high suspicion

**Management:**
- **Secure the aneurysm** (endovascular coiling preferred over surgical clipping for most ruptured aneurysms -- ISAT trial evidence)
- **Nimodipine 60 mg PO/NG every 4 hours** for 21 days (reduces vasospasm-related delayed cerebral ischemia; improves functional outcomes)
- **Monitor for complications:** Vasospasm (peak days 3-14), hyponatremia (cerebral salt wasting or SIADH), hydrocephalus, rebleeding

## 3.5 Post-tPA Monitoring

- **Frequent neurological checks** and blood pressure monitoring (q15 min x 2 hours, then q30 min x 6 hours, then q1h)
- **No antiplatelet or anticoagulation** for 24 hours following thrombolysis
- **Repeat head CT at 24 hours** before starting any antithrombotic therapy
- **Watch for complications:**
  - Symptomatic intracranial hemorrhage (sudden neurological decline, severe headache, nausea/vomiting, blood pressure increase)
  - Orolingual angioedema (incidence ~1.3-5.1%; more common with concurrent ACE inhibitor use; treat with methylprednisolone, diphenhydramine, ranitidine; airway management if progressive)
  - Extracranial bleeding

## 3.6 Differential Diagnosis of Acute Stroke

| Condition | Distinguishing Features |
|-----------|------------------------|
| Hypoglycemia | Check fingerstick glucose immediately; glucose <50 may mimic stroke |
| Seizure with Todd's paralysis | History of seizure, encephalopathy; deficits resolve over hours |
| Migraine with aura | Gradual onset, visual phenomena, prior history, younger patient |
| Bell's palsy | Isolated facial weakness only, no extremity involvement |
| Conversion disorder | Inconsistent examination findings, non-anatomic distribution, psychiatric history |
| Brain tumor | Subacute onset, progressive symptoms, headache, seizure |
| Wernicke encephalopathy | Ataxia, ophthalmoplegia, confusion; thiamine deficiency risk factors |
| Hypertensive encephalopathy | Diffuse symptoms, headache, visual changes; BP typically >180/120 |

## 3.7 Red Flags

> **CRITICAL / TIME-SENSITIVE:**
- **Every minute counts** in acute stroke; bypass non-essential steps when possible
- Rapidly deteriorating neurological examination = possible hemorrhagic transformation or mass effect
- Signs of herniation: Unequal pupils, decorticate/decerebrate posturing, Cushing's triad (hypertension, bradycardia, irregular respirations)
- ICH after thrombolysis: Immediate head CT, hematology consultation, blood pressure control, possible neurosurgical intervention
- Mass effect from large territory stroke: Hemicraniectomy consideration for malignant MCA infarction

# Chapter 4: Sepsis and Septic Shock

## 4.1 Overview

Sepsis is life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection. It affects over 1.7 million adults annually in the United States, with hospital mortality of 15-30% for sepsis and 30-50% for septic shock. The Surviving Sepsis Campaign (SSC) 2021 Guidelines provide the most current evidence-based management recommendations, emphasizing early recognition, prompt antibiotic administration, and protocolized hemodynamic support [^5^][^579^].

## 4.2 Sepsis-3 Definitions

### 4.2.1 Sepsis

Life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection, identified by an acute increase in total Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score of >=2 points [^579^].

**SOFA Score Components (0-4 points each):**

| System | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|--------|---|---|---|---|---|
| Respiratory (PaO2/FiO2) | >=400 | <400 | <300 | <200 | <100 |
| Coagulation (Platelets) | >=150 | <150 | <100 | <50 | <20 |
| Liver (Bilirubin) | <1.2 | 1.2-1.9 | 2.0-5.9 | 6.0-11.9 | >=12.0 |
| Cardiovascular (MAP) | >=70 | <70 | Dop <=5 | Dop 5.1-15 | Dop >15 |
| CNS (GCS) | 15 | 13-14 | 10-12 | 6-9 | <6 |
| Renal (Creatinine) | <1.2 | 1.2-1.9 | 2.0-3.4 | 3.5-4.9 | >=5.0 |

### 4.2.2 Quick SOFA (qSOFA) -- Bedside Screening Tool

qSOFA is a rapid bedside screening tool to identify adults outside the ICU with suspected infection who are at greater risk for poor outcome [^579^]:

| Criterion | Threshold |
|-----------|-----------|
| Respiratory rate | >=22/min |
| Altered mentation | Glasgow Coma Scale <15 |
| Systolic blood pressure | <=100 mmHg |

- **>=2 criteria** suggest high risk of poor outcome (prolonged ICU stay or in-hospital mortality)
- A positive qSOFA should prompt further evaluation for organ dysfunction and escalation of care

### 4.2.3 Septic Shock

Septic shock is a subset of sepsis with **profound circulatory, cellular, and metabolic abnormalities** that is associated with a greater risk of mortality than sepsis alone. It is defined as [^579^]:
- Sepsis **plus** persistent hypotension requiring vasopressors to maintain MAP >=65 mmHg
- **Plus** serum lactate >2 mmol/L (18 mg/dL) despite adequate volume resuscitation
- Mortality rate: Approximately 40-50%

## 4.3 Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2021: Hour-1 Bundle

All elements should be initiated **immediately upon recognition** of sepsis or septic shock, ideally within 1 hour [^579^]:

1. **Measure lactate level** (remeasure within 2-4 hours if initial >=2 mmol/L)
2. **Obtain blood cultures before antibiotics** -- but do not delay antibiotic administration >45 minutes for culture acquisition
3. **Administer broad-spectrum antibiotics** within 1 hour of recognition
4. **Rapidly administer 30 mL/kg crystalloid** for hypotension or lactate >=4 mmol/L
5. **Apply vasopressors** during or after fluid resuscitation to maintain MAP >=65 mmHg

## 4.4 Fluid Resuscitation

### 4.4.1 Crystalloid Selection

- **Balanced crystalloids preferred over normal saline** (2021 SSC recommendation) [^579^]
  - Lactated Ringer's or Plasma-Lyte reduce the risk of hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis and may improve renal outcomes (SALT-ED and SMART trial evidence)
- **Initial bolus:** 30 mL/kg within the first 3 hours (weak recommendation, low-quality evidence)
- **Dynamic assessments** should guide further fluid administration after the initial bolus:
  - Passive leg raise (PLR) test with stroke volume measurement
  - Pulse pressure variation (PPV; requires regular rhythm and mechanical ventilation)
  - Capillary refill time (<3 seconds as target; ANDROMEDA-SHOCK trial)
  - Bedside ultrasound (IVC assessment, lung ultrasound for B-lines)
- **Avoid excessive fluid administration:** Some evidence suggests a U-shaped mortality curve with both under- and over-resuscitation [^576^]

## 4.5 Vasopressors

### 4.5.1 First-Line Agent: Norepinephrine

| Agent | Dosing | Notes |
|-------|--------|-------|
| **Norepinephrine** | Start 0.01-3 mcg/kg/min; titrate to MAP target | **First-line preferred agent**; combined alpha-1 and beta-1 activity |
| Vasopressin | 0.03 units/min | Add if norepinephrine inadequate; may allow norepinephrine dose reduction |
| Epinephrine | 0.1-0.5 mcg/kg/min | Alternative or addition to norepinephrine; consider for cold shock with low cardiac output |
| Dopamine | 5-20 mcg/kg/min | **NOT recommended**; use only if norepinephrine unavailable |
| Phenylephrine | 40-180 mcg/min | **NOT recommended** (pure alpha-agonist reduces stroke volume) |

[^5^][^579^]

**Administration considerations:**
- Central venous access is preferred for all vasopressors but initiation through a peripheral IV is acceptable if central access is not immediately available (use a large vein in the upper extremity; monitor for extravasation)
- Target MAP >=65 mmHg (individualize for patients with chronic hypertension -- target 75-80 mmHg)
- Consider arterial line placement for continuous blood pressure monitoring

### 4.5.2 Vasopressor-Resistant Shock

If adequate MAP is not achieved with norepinephrine:
1. Add vasopressin 0.03 units/min (VAAST trial -- may reduce norepinephrine requirements)
2. Consider epinephrine addition
3. Initiate stress-dose corticosteroids (see Section 4.8)
4. Evaluate for uncorrected hypovolemia, ongoing bleeding, or uncontrolled infection source
5. Consider ECMO for refractory shock in experienced centers

## 4.6 Antibiotic Therapy

### 4.6.1 Core Principles

- **Broad-spectrum empiric coverage** based on suspected source and local resistance patterns
- **Within 1 hour** of sepsis recognition -- each hour of delay increases mortality
- **Combination therapy** for septic shock (beta-lactam + aminoglycoside or fluoroquinolone) -- de-escalate when cultures return
- **Source control** as soon as medically and logistically practical (drain abscesses, remove infected devices, debride necrotic tissue, definitive surgical management)
- **Daily reassessment** for de-escalation based on culture results and clinical response
- **Typical duration:** 7-10 days (shorter for uncomplicated infections; longer for immunocompromised, inadequate source control, or endocarditis/osteomyelitis)

### 4.6.2 Common Empiric Regimens by Source

| Suspected Source | Empiric Antibiotic Regimen |
|-----------------|---------------------------|
| Community-acquired pneumonia | Ceftriaxone + azithromycin OR respiratory fluoroquinolone (levofloxacin, moxifloxacin) |
| Healthcare-associated pneumonia | Anti-Pseudomonal beta-lactam (piperacillin-tazobactam or cefepime) + vancomycin |
| Intra-abdominal (community) | Piperacillin-tazobactam OR ceftriaxone + metronidazole |
| Intra-abdominal (post-operative/biliary) | Piperacillin-tazobactam + vancomycin |
| Urinary (complicated/HA) | Piperacillin-tazobactam OR ceftolozane-tazobactam |
| Skin/soft tissue (necrotizing fasciitis) | Vancomycin + piperacillin-tazobactam + clindamycin |
| Meningitis | Vancomycin + ceftriaxone + ampicillin (if age >50 or immunocompromised) + acyclovir |
| Unknown source (septic shock) | Vancomycin + piperacillin-tazobactam OR vancomycin + cefepime + metronidazole |

## 4.7 Corticosteroids

- **Indication:** Adults with septic shock who have an ongoing vasopressor requirement (norepinephrine >=0.25 mcg/kg/min for at least 4 hours despite adequate fluid resuscitation)
- **Regimen:** Hydrocortisone 200 mg/day IV (50 mg every 6 hours OR continuous infusion)
- **Evidence:** ADRENAL and APROCCHSS trials showed faster shock resolution with corticosteroids; mortality benefit observed in APROCCHSS (fludrocortisone + hydrocortisone) but not ADRENAL
- **2021 SSC:** Weak recommendation; moderate-quality evidence [^5^][^579^]

## 4.8 ARDS Management in Sepsis

Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARDS) develops in approximately 40% of patients with septic shock. See Chapter 9 for comprehensive ARDS management. Key principles in sepsis-related ARDS [^5^]:

### 4.8.1 Lung-Protective Ventilation

| Parameter | Target |
|-----------|--------|
| Tidal volume | 6 mL/kg predicted body weight (range 4-8 mL/kg) |
| Plateau pressure | <30 cmH2O |
| Driving pressure | <15 cmH2O (plateau minus PEEP) |
| PEEP | 5-15 cmH2O, titrated to oxygenation |
| SpO2 target | 88-95% (PaO2 55-80 mmHg) |

### 4.8.2 Adjunctive ARDS Therapies

- **Prone positioning:** For moderate-to-severe ARDS (PaO2/FiO2 <150); minimum 16 hours/session; demonstrated mortality benefit (PROSEVA trial) [^604^][^614^]
- **Neuromuscular blockade:** Cisatracurium infusion for 48 hours if PaO2/FiO2 <150 (ACURASYS trial); intermittent boluses preferred over continuous for longer use
- **Conservative fluid strategy:** After initial hemodynamic stabilization; diurese to achieve even or negative fluid balance
- **ECMO:** For refractory hypoxemia when conventional management fails; experienced centers only (EOLIA trial context)

## 4.9 Red Flags

> **CRITICAL:**
- MAP <65 mmHg despite adequate fluids and escalating vasopressors = refractory septic shock (mortality >50%)
- Lactate >4 mmol/L with hypotension = severe shock requiring aggressive intervention
- Acute respiratory failure requiring intubation in sepsis = high mortality
- Oliguria (<0.3 mL/kg/hour) or rising creatinine = septic AKI
- Altered mental status in sepsis = possible septic encephalopathy
- Uncontrolled infection source (necrotizing fasciitis, undrained abscess, infected hardware) = surgical emergency

## 4.10 References

- Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2021 Guidelines [^5^]
- Surviving Sepsis Campaign Educational Materials [^579^]
- SSC Fluid Resuscitation Evidence Review [^576^]
- ANDROMEDA-SHOCK Trial (Hernandez et al., 2019)
- PROSEVA Trial (Guerin et al., 2013)

# Chapter 5: Anaphylaxis

## 5.1 Overview

Anaphylaxis is a serious, rapid-onset, potentially fatal systemic allergic reaction. It is the most severe form of immediate hypersensitivity (Type I), mediated primarily by IgE-triggered mast cell and basophil degranulation. The estimated lifetime prevalence is 1.6-5.1%, with increasing incidence. Anaphylaxis causes approximately 1,500 deaths annually in the United States, most of which are preventable with prompt epinephrine administration [^605^].

## 5.2 Definition and Recognition Criteria

Anaphylaxis is **highly likely** when ANY ONE of the following three criteria is met:

1. **Acute onset** of illness (minutes to hours) with involvement of skin, mucosal tissue, or both (e.g., hives, pruritus, flushing, swollen lips/tongue/uvula) **AND AT LEAST ONE OF:**
   - Respiratory compromise (dyspnea, wheeze-bronchospasm, stridor, reduced PEF, hypoxemia)
   - Hypotension or end-organ dysfunction (collapse, syncope, incontinence)

2. **Two or more** of the following that occur **rapidly** after exposure to a **likely allergen** (minutes to hours):
   - Skin-mucosal tissue involvement
   - Respiratory compromise
   - Reduced blood pressure or associated symptoms (syncope, incontinence)
   - Persistent gastrointestinal symptoms (crampy abdominal pain, vomiting)

3. **Reduced blood pressure** after exposure to a **known allergen** for that patient:
   - Adults: SBP <90 mmHg or >30% decrease from baseline
   - Children: SBP <70 mmHg + (2 x age in years) mmHg

## 5.3 Epinephrine: First-Line Treatment

> **CRITICAL:** Epinephrine is the ONLY medication that prevents progression and reverses the life-threatening manifestations of anaphylaxis. Antihistamines, corticosteroids, and bronchodilators are adjuncts only and must NEVER delay epinephrine administration.

### 5.3.1 Intramuscular Epinephrine

| Parameter | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| **Dose (adults)** | 0.3-0.5 mg |
| **Concentration** | 1:1000 (1 mg/mL) |
| **Route** | Intramuscular into anterolateral thigh (vastus lateralis) |
| **Repeat dosing** | Every 5-15 minutes as needed for persistent or recurrent symptoms |
| **Onset (IM thigh)** | Peak plasma concentrations in ~8 minutes; faster than subcutaneous |
| **Why thigh (not arm)?** | Vastus lateralis has better blood supply; faster absorption; injection site does not affect outcomes in most cases |

### 5.3.2 Autoinjectors

| Product | Dose | Patient Weight |
|---------|------|---------------|
| EpiPen | 0.3 mg | >=30 kg (66 lbs) |
| EpiPen Jr | 0.15 mg | 15-30 kg (33-66 lbs) |
| Auvi-Q | 0.1 mg, 0.15 mg, 0.3 mg | Variable by device |

### 5.3.3 Intravenous Epinephrine

Reserved for patients with refractory anaphylaxis, shock, or cardiovascular collapse:
- Start 0.1-1 mcg/minute IV infusion; titrate to effect
- Requires continuous cardiac and hemodynamic monitoring
- High risk of arrhythmias and myocardial ischemia

### 5.3.4 Pediatric Dosing

- **0.01 mg/kg IM** (maximum 0.5 mg) of 1:1000 concentration into the anterolateral thigh
- Weight-based dosing is critical for optimal therapeutic effect

## 5.4 Adjunctive Therapy

| Medication | Role | Dosing | Notes |
|------------|------|--------|-------|
| H1 antihistamine (diphenhydramine) | Relieves urticaria, pruritus, flushing | 25-50 mg IV/IM/PO | **NOT life-saving**; adjunct only; does NOT treat airway or hemodynamic compromise |
| H2 antihistamine (famotidine) | Adjunct for skin symptoms | 20 mg IV | Evidence limited; may provide additional cutaneous symptom relief |
| Corticosteroids (methylprednisolone) | May prevent biphasic reaction | 125 mg IV | Slow onset (>4-6 hours); **NOT for acute rescue** |
| Inhaled beta-agonist (albuterol) | Bronchospasm refractory to epinephrine | 2.5-5 mg nebulized | Adjunct for wheezing; does NOT replace epinephrine |
| Glucagon | For patients on beta-blockers with refractory anaphylaxis | 1-5 mg IV over 5 minutes | May precipitate hyperglycemia; use with caution in diabetics |
| IV Fluids | Volume resuscitation for hypotension | 1-2 liters normal saline rapidly | Large volumes may be needed due to distributive shock |

## 5.5 Biphasic Reactions

Biphasic reactions are recurrences of anaphylactic symptoms after initial resolution, without re-exposure to the trigger. They occur in approximately 3-20% of patients (estimates vary widely across studies) [^605^].

| Parameter | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| Typical onset | 4-10 hours after initial reaction |
| Maximum reported delay | Up to 33 hours |
| Risk factors | Severe initial reaction, wide pulse pressure, repeated epinephrine doses, unknown trigger, delayed initial epinephrine administration |
| **Observation period** | **Minimum 4-6 hours** for mild-to-moderate reactions |
| **Extended observation** | **12-24 hours** for severe reactions, reactions requiring >1 epinephrine dose, unknown trigger, asthma, limited access to emergency care, or patient unable to self-treat recurrence |

## 5.6 Discharge Planning

All patients who experience anaphylaxis require comprehensive discharge planning:

1. **Epinephrine auto-injector prescription:** Two devices (one for immediate use, one backup)
2. **Allergy specialist referral:** For trigger identification, testing, and consideration of immunotherapy (especially for venom and medication allergies)
3. **Written anaphylaxis action plan:** Clear instructions on recognition, self-treatment, and when to call emergency services
4. **Avoidance education:** Specific strategies to avoid identified triggers (food labeling, insect precautions, medication alternatives)
5. **Medical alert identification:** Bracelet or necklace indicating allergy
6. **Education on proper autoinjector technique:** Include family members and caregivers
7. **Follow-up:** Within 1-2 weeks with primary care; allergy specialist within 2-4 weeks

## 5.7 Red Flags

> **CRITICAL:**
- Stridor, severe wheezing, or respiratory distress = impending airway compromise
- Hypotension (SBP <90 mmHg) or syncope = cardiovascular collapse
- Rapid progression of symptoms despite initial treatment = severe reaction
- History of asthma significantly increases risk of fatal anaphylaxis
- **Delayed epinephrine administration** is the single greatest risk factor for fatal anaphylaxis
- Beta-blocker use may blunt epinephrine response; higher doses and glucagon may be needed

- AHA Guidelines for Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation and Emergency Cardiovascular Care

# Chapter 6: Toxicology and Overdose

## 6.1 Overview and General Approach

The approach to the poisoned patient follows a systematic, prioritized framework. Over 2 million human poison exposures are reported annually in the United States, with analgesics, sedatives/hypnotics, and antidepressants among the most common substance classes involved in serious overdoses. The general approach prioritizes stabilization, identification, and targeted antidotal therapy [^574^][^575^].

### 6.1.1 ABCD Approach to the Poisoned Patient

1. **Airway protection:** Intubate if airway reflexes are compromised (GCS <8, absent gag)
2. **Breathing and ventilation:** Supplemental O2; assess for respiratory depression (opioids, benzodiazepines, alcohol)
3. **Circulation and hemodynamic support:** IV access, cardiac monitoring, blood pressure support
4. **Dextrose, Thiamine, Naloxone:** The universal antidotes given early
   - Dextrose: Rule out/treat hypoglycemia (fingerstick glucose first if available)
   - Thiamine 100 mg IV: Prevent Wernicke encephalopathy (before glucose in malnourished patients)
   - Naloxone: If opioid overdose suspected
5. **Decontamination:** When appropriate and within the therapeutic window (see Section 6.11)

### 6.1.2 Toxidrome Recognition

Rapid identification of the toxidrome guides empiric management when the specific agent is unknown:

| Toxidrome | Key Clinical Features | Pupils | Common Agents |
|-----------|----------------------|--------|---------------|
| **Sympathomimetic** | Tachycardia, hypertension, hyperthermia, agitation, diaphoresis, mydriasis | Dilated | Cocaine, amphetamines, methamphetamine, MDMA, synthetic cathinones ("bath salts") |
| **Anticholinergic** | "Hot as a hare, blind as a bat, dry as a bone, red as a beet, mad as a hatter" | Dilated | Diphenhydramine, TCA, atropine, scopolamine, jimson weed |
| **Cholinergic** | SLUDGE: Salivation, Lacrimation, Urination, Defecation, GI distress, Emesis; or DUMBELS | Miotic (constricted) | Organophosphates, carbamates, nerve agents, pilocarpine |
| **Opioid** | Respiratory depression, miosis (pinpoint pupils), decreased level of consciousness, hypotension | Pinpoint | Heroin, fentanyl, oxycodone, methadone, morphine |
| **Sedative-hypnotic** | Sedation, respiratory depression, ataxia, slurred speech | Normal or constricted | Benzodiazepines, barbiturates, alcohol, zolpidem |
| **Serotonin syndrome** | Altered mental status, hyperthermia, clonus, hyperreflexia, diaphoresis, tremor | Dilated | SSRIs, MAOIs, TCAs, tramadol, fentanyl, MDMA, lithium, linezolid |

## 6.2 Acetaminophen (Paracetamol) Overdose

### 6.2.1 Mechanism and Toxicity

Acetaminophen is the most common pharmaceutical exposure reported to poison centers. Toxicity results from the depletion of hepatic glutathione stores, leading to the accumulation of N-acetyl-p-benzoquinone imine (NAPQI), a highly reactive intermediate metabolite that causes centrilobular hepatic necrosis. The Rumack-Matthew nomogram guides treatment decisions for single acute ingestions [^574^][^585^].

### 6.2.2 Rumack-Matthew Nomogram

- **Plot serum acetaminophen concentration** at 4-24 hours post-ingestion
- **Treatment line ("possible hepatotoxicity"):** 150 mcg/mL at 4 hours, declining to 37 mcg/mL at 12 hours, and 18.75 mcg/mL at 24 hours
- **High-risk line (300 mcg/mL at 4 hours):** For patients with risk factors for enhanced toxicity including chronic alcohol use, malnutrition, concurrent enzyme-inducing medications (rifampin, phenytoin, carbamazepine)
- **Values above the treatment line at 4-24 hours = treat with NAC**
- **Values below the treatment line = NAC not indicated for acute ingestion**

### 6.2.3 N-Acetylcysteine (NAC) Protocol

NAC replenishes glutathione stores and directly binds NAPQI. It is the specific antidote for acetaminophen toxicity [^574^][^581^][^584^]:

**IV NAC (Acetadote):**

| Phase | Dose | Infusion Time | Total |
|-------|------|--------------|-------|
| Loading | 150 mg/kg in 200 mL D5W | 60 minutes | 150 mg/kg |
| Second | 50 mg/kg in 500 mL D5W | 4 hours | 50 mg/kg |
| Third | 100 mg/kg in 1000 mL D5W | 16 hours | 100 mg/kg |

**Total: 300 mg/kg over 21 hours**

**Two-bag protocol (preferred in many centers):** Simplified regimen with similar effectiveness, fewer adverse reactions, and reduced medication errors [^578^].

**Oral NAC (Mucomyst):**
- Loading dose: 140 mg/kg
- Then 70 mg/kg every 4 hours for 17 additional doses
- Unpleasant taste and smell; high rates of vomiting

### 6.2.4 Treatment Timing and Efficacy

- **Within 8 hours of ingestion:** NAC provides nearly 100% protection against hepatotoxicity
- **8-24 hours:** Still beneficial; treatment should not be withheld even if >8 hours post-ingestion
- **>24 hours:** Treatment should still be given if evidence of hepatotoxicity (elevated LFTs) or elevated acetaminophen level
- **Unknown time of ingestion:** Treat empirically if any clinical suspicion

### 6.2.5 NAC Discontinuation Criteria

All of the following must be met before stopping NAC [^578^]:
- Patient clinically well
- Acetaminophen level <10 mcg/mL
- INR <2.0
- AST normal or down-trending
- If peak AST >=1000 IU/L: stop when AST decreased >25% from peak and INR improving

### 6.2.6 High-Risk Ingestion Considerations

- **>30 grams ingested** or concentration above the high-risk line on the nomogram
- Consider activated charcoal even >4 hours post-ingestion (acetaminophen has prolonged absorption in overdose)
- Consult toxicology; may require extended NAC dosing or liver transplant evaluation

## 6.3 Opioid Overdose

### 6.3.1 Recognition

The classic triad of opioid toxicity:
- Respiratory depression (rate <12/minute or apnea)
- Miosis (pinpoint pupils) -- though may be normal with certain opioids (meperidine, propoxyphene) or hypoxic dilation
- Altered mental status (sedation to coma)

### 6.3.2 Naloxone Dosing

The goal of naloxone administration is to restore adequate spontaneous breathing, NOT to achieve full arousal [^575^][^582^]:

| Route | Dose | Notes |
|-------|------|-------|
| **Intranasal (preferred for layperson/first responder)** | 2-4 mg per nostril (total 4-8 mg); repeat q2-3 min | Most accessible; no needle required |
| **IV (preferred in clinical setting)** | Start 0.04-0.4 mg; titrate up to 2 mg; repeat q2-3 min | Titration prevents severe withdrawal and agitation |
| **IM/SC** | 0.4-2 mg; onset 3-10 minutes | If no IV access |

- **Total doses up to 10 mg may be needed** for potent synthetic opioids (fentanyl and analogues)
- **Naloxone infusion:** 0.4-0.8 times the effective bolus dose per hour (for long-acting opioids like methadone or sustained-release formulations)

### 6.3.3 Special Considerations

- **Fentanyl and analogues:** Higher potency requires larger naloxone doses; recurrent sedation is common because fentanyl is longer-acting than naloxone
- **Observation period:** Minimum 2-3 hours for short-acting opioids; 4-6 hours for fentanyl; longer for long-acting opioids (methadone, buprenorphine, sustained-release formulations)
- **Naloxone-precipitated withdrawal:** Not life-threatening but extremely uncomfortable; characterized by vomiting, diarrhea, agitation, piloerection
- **Pulmonary edema:** Rare complication of naloxone administration; treat supportively

## 6.4 Benzodiazepine Overdose

### 6.4.1 General Management

Isolated benzodiazepine (BDZ) overdose rarely causes fatal respiratory depression. Supportive care with airway protection and ventilation is usually sufficient. The vast majority of patients recover uneventfully with supportive management alone [^659^].

### 6.4.2 Flumazenil: Cautious Use

Flumazenil is a competitive benzodiazepine receptor antagonist. Its use is associated with significant risks:

**Contraindications (do NOT give flumazenil if):**
- Unknown or mixed overdose (especially co-ingestion of TCAs, cocaine, or other pro-convulsant/pro-arrhythmic agents)
- Benzodiazepine tolerance or dependence (including prescribed chronic use) -- risk of precipitating withdrawal seizures
- History of seizure disorder
- Prolonged QRS interval on ECG (suggests TCA or other sodium channel blocker co-ingestion)
- Signs of TCA overdose (anticholinergic findings, wide QRS) [^659^][^663^]

**When flumazenil may be considered:**
- Accidental pediatric ingestion
- Reversal of iatrogenic oversedation
- Isolated benzodiazepine/Z-drug overdose with respiratory depression AND no contraindications [^662^]

**Dosing:** 0.2 mg IV over 30 seconds; may repeat 0.3-0.5 mg every 1 minute to maximum 3 mg total

**Adverse events:** Seizures and arrhythmias occur at significantly higher rates (relative risk 3.81 vs. placebo) [^659^]. All patients receiving flumazenil require continuous monitoring for at least 2 hours due to risk of re-sedation (flumazenil has shorter half-life than most benzodiazepines).

## 6.5 Tricyclic Antidepressant (TCA) Overdose

### 6.5.1 Mechanism and Manifestations

TCA overdose produces a classic constellation of toxicity:
- **Sodium channel blockade:** QRS widening (>100 ms), ventricular arrhythmias, right axis deviation (R wave >3 mm in aVR)
- **Anticholinergic effects:** Tachycardia, dry skin, urinary retention, ileus, delirium, hyperthermia
- **Alpha-1 blockade:** Vasodilation, hypotension
- **GABA antagonism:** Seizures

### 6.5.2 Key Management

**Sodium Bicarbonate: The Antidote of Choice**

Sodium bicarbonate is the specific antidote for TCA-induced sodium channel blockade and cardiotoxicity [^619^]:

| Indication | Intervention |
|------------|-------------|
| QRS >100 ms | Bolus: 1-2 mEq/kg (1-2 ampules of 8.4%) IV push |
| Ventricular arrhythmias | Repeat bolus; then continuous infusion |
| Refractory arrhythmias/hypotension | Infusion: 150 mEq in 1L D5W at 150-250 mL/hr; titrate to serum pH 7.45-7.55 |
| Seizures | Benzodiazepines first-line (lorazepam 2-4 mg IV) |
| Hypotension | IV fluids; norepinephrine if fluid-resistant |

- **Monitor for hypokalemia** with bicarbonate therapy
- **Avoid class IA and IC antiarrhythmics** (procainamide, flecainide) -- they worsen sodium channel blockade
- **Lidocaine** (class IB) may be used if bicarbonate refractory
- **Intubation** if altered mental status prevents airway protection or if hemodynamic instability requires escalation

## 6.6 Salicylate Overdose

### 6.6.1 Mechanism and Clinical Features

Salicylate toxicity causes direct stimulation of the respiratory center (respiratory alkalosis early), uncoupling of oxidative phosphorylation, and interference with the Krebs cycle (metabolic acidosis). Mixed acid-base disorder is common. Direct toxicity to the CNS causes tinnitus, vertigo, nausea, and altered mental status.

### 6.6.2 Management

1. **Activated charcoal** (if within 1-2 hours of ingestion; sustained-release formulations may benefit from later administration)
2. **Urinary alkalinization:** Sodium bicarbonate to maintain urine pH 7.5-8.0 -- this is the cornerstone of treatment; ion trapping enhances salicylate excretion
3. **Potassium supplementation:** Hypokalemia prevents urinary alkalinization (K+ is exchanged for H+ in the urine); maintain K+ >4.0 mEq/L
4. **Glucose management:** Check and treat blood glucose; even with normal serum glucose, CNS glucose may be low due to impaired transport -- give glucose if any altered mental status
5. **Hemodialysis indications:** [^619^]
   - Salicylate level >100 mg/dL (acute) or >60 mg/dL (chronic)
   - Altered mental status, seizures, or cerebral edema
   - Pulmonary edema
   - Renal failure
   - Severe acid-base disturbance refractory to therapy
   - Clinical deterioration despite supportive care
   - Any salicylate level with clinical toxicity in the setting of impaired renal clearance

## 6.7 Beta-Blocker and Calcium Channel Blocker Overdose

### 6.7.1 Clinical Presentation

Both classes produce cardiovascular suppression, though with important differences:
- **Beta-blocker overdose:** Bradycardia, hypotension, AV block, bronchospasm, hypoglycemia (especially with non-selective agents); seizures with lipophilic agents (propranolol)
- **Calcium channel blocker overdose:** Bradycardia, hypotension, AV block, hyperglycemia (blocks insulin release), bowel ischemia; dihydropyridines (amlodipine) cause more vasodilation; non-dihydropyridines (verapamil, diltiazem) cause more cardiac suppression

### 6.7.2 Treatment Algorithm

| Intervention | Dosing | Primary Indication | Notes |
|-------------|--------|-------------------|-------|
| Atropine | 0.5-1 mg IV; may repeat | Bradycardia | Often ineffective in BB/CCB overdose |
| Calcium salts | Calcium chloride 1 g IV OR calcium gluconate 3 g IV | CCB overdose | Repeat every 10-20 minutes; monitor ionized calcium |
| Glucagon | 3-10 mg IV bolus; then 3-5 mg/hr infusion | BB overdose | Hyperglycemia side effect; antiemetic pre-treatment |
| High-dose insulin (HDI) | 1 U/kg bolus; 1-10 U/kg/hr infusion | Refractory BB/CCB | With dextrose to maintain euglycemia |
| Lipid emulsion (20% Intralipid) | 1.5 mL/kg bolus; 0.25-0.5 mL/kg/min infusion | Severe/refractory | "Lipid sink" theory for lipophilic agents |
| Vasopressors | Norepinephrine, epinephrine, vasopressin | Hypotension | High doses often needed |

[^618^]

### 6.7.3 High-Dose Insulin (HDI) Protocol

High-dose insulin provides inotropic support through metabolic manipulation of cardiac myocytes [^618^]:

- **Bolus:** 1 U/kg IV regular insulin + 0.5 g/kg IV dextrose (D50 or D10)
- **Infusion:** Start 1 U/kg/hour; titrate up to 10 U/kg/hour as needed
- **Dextrose infusion:** 0.5 g/kg/hour; titrate to maintain glucose 100-200 mg/dL
- **Potassium:** Check every 30 minutes; insulin drives K+ intracellularly; aggressive replacement needed
- **Onset of hemodynamic effect:** Up to 60 minutes -- start early if refractory to initial therapy

### 6.7.4 Lipid Emulsion Therapy

- **Bolus:** 1.5 mL/kg of 20% Intralipid emulsion over 2-3 minutes
- **Infusion:** 0.25-0.5 mL/kg/minute
- **May repeat bolus** every 3-5 minutes for persistent cardiovascular collapse or cardiac arrest
- **Mechanism:** Creates a lipid phase in plasma that sequesters lipophilic drugs; also provides direct inotropic support to the heart
- **Complications:** Hypertriglyceridemia, pancreatitis, interference with laboratory assays

## 6.8 Digoxin Toxicity

### 6.8.1 Clinical Presentation

Digoxin toxicity can occur with acute overdose or chronic accumulation (especially with renal impairment, hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, or interacting medications). Manifestations include:
- **GI:** Nausea, vomiting, anorexia (often earliest signs in chronic toxicity)
- **Visual:** Blurred vision, yellow-green halos, photopsia
- **Cardiac:** Virtually any arrhythmia is possible; characteristic findings include bidirectional ventricular tachycardia, atrial tachycardia with AV block, frequent PVCs, accelerated junctional rhythm
- **Metabolic:** Hyperkalemia in acute overdose (indicator of severity); hypokalemia in chronic toxicity (potentiates toxicity)

### 6.8.2 Digoxin Immune Fab (DigiFab)

DigiFab is the specific antidote for life-threatening digoxin toxicity [^620^][^621^]:

**Indications:**
- Life-threatening arrhythmias (ventricular arrhythmias, complete heart block, symptomatic bradycardia unresponsive to atropine)
- Hyperkalemia >5-6.5 mEq/L in acute ingestion
- Hemodynamic instability
- Acute ingestion >10 mg (adult) or >4 mg (child)
- Steady-state serum digoxin concentration >10 ng/mL
- Progressive clinical deterioration

**Dosing:**

| Scenario | Dose Calculation |
|----------|-----------------|
| Acute ingestion (unknown amount) | 20 vials IV (may start with 10, give 10 more if needed) |
| Acute ingestion (known amount) | Dose (vials) = Amount ingested (mg) / 0.5 mg/vial |
| Chronic toxicity | Dose (vials) = (Serum digoxin ng/mL x Weight in kg) / 100 |
| Cardiac arrest | 5 vials IV immediately; repeat if needed after 30 minutes |

- Each vial contains 40 mg digoxin-specific antibody Fab fragments
- **Post-administration considerations:** Standard digoxin immunoassays become unreliable after Fab administration (measured levels are falsely elevated due to bound complexes); watch for hypokalemia as Fab shifts K+ into cells alongside digoxin [^620^]

## 6.9 Carbon Monoxide Poisoning

### 6.9.1 Mechanism

Carbon monoxide (CO) binds hemoglobin with approximately 240 times greater affinity than oxygen, forming carboxyhemoglobin (COHb). This produces tissue hypoxia through multiple mechanisms: reduced oxygen-carrying capacity, left-shifted oxyhemoglobin dissociation curve (impaired oxygen release), and direct inhibition of cytochrome oxidase (cellular toxicity) [^622^].

### 6.9.2 Clinical Features

- **Mild (COHb 10-20%):** Headache, nausea, dizziness, fatigue
- **Moderate (COHb 20-40%):** Severe headache, vomiting, confusion, chest pain, dyspnea
- **Severe (COHb 40-60%):** Syncope, seizures, coma, arrhythmias, hypotension
- **Fatal (COHb >60%):** Cardiovascular collapse, death

**Note:** COHb levels do NOT reliably correlate with clinical severity or neurological outcome. Clinical assessment is more important than the number.

### 6.9.3 Management

1. **Remove from source immediately** (scene safety for rescuers is paramount)
2. **100% oxygen via non-rebreather mask** -- reduces CO half-life from 4-6 hours (room air) to 40-80 minutes
3. **Hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT) indications** [^622^]:
   - Loss of consciousness at any point
   - COHb >25% (>15-20% in pregnancy)
   - Neurologic deficits (other than transient headache)
   - Cardiac ischemia or arrhythmias
   - Severe metabolic acidosis (pH <7.1-7.25)
   - End-organ ischemia
   - Pregnant patients (lower threshold due to fetal hemoglobin's higher CO affinity)
   - Best outcomes when initiated within 6 hours of exposure

## 6.10 Toxic Alcohols: Methanol and Ethylene Glycol

### 6.10.1 Recognition

Toxic alcohol poisoning should be suspected with:
- **Anion gap metabolic acidosis** (elevated anion gap with low bicarbonate)
- **Osmolar gap >10 mOsm/kg** (difference between measured and calculated serum osmolality)
- **Clinical clues:**
  - Methanol: Visual symptoms (blurred vision, photophobia, "snowfield" vision, blindness); formic acid accumulation
  - Ethylene glycol: Calcium oxalate crystalluria (envelope-shaped crystals), renal failure, hypocalcemia

### 6.10.2 Fomepizole (4-Methylpyrazole, Antizol)

Fomepizole is the first-line antidote; it inhibits alcohol dehydrogenase, preventing the formation of toxic metabolites [^623^]:

| Phase | Dose | Frequency |
|-------|------|-----------|
| Loading | 15 mg/kg IV | Single dose |
| Maintenance | 10 mg/kg IV | Every 12 hours for 4 doses |
| Extended maintenance | 15 mg/kg IV | Every 12 hours thereafter |
| During hemodialysis | 1-1.5 mg/kg/hour | Continuous infusion |

**Continue fomepizole until:** Toxic alcohol concentration <20 mg/dL AND arterial pH is normal.

### 6.10.3 Indications for Fomepizole

- Documented toxic alcohol ingestion with osmolar gap >10
- Toxic alcohol concentration >=20 mg/dL
- Suspected ingestion with ANY of: arterial pH <7.3, bicarbonate <20 mEq/L, osmolar gap >10, oxalate crystalluria [^623^]

### 6.10.4 Adjunctive Therapy

| Agent | Indication | Dose |
|-------|-----------|------|
| Sodium bicarbonate | Acidosis (pH <7.3) | Titrate to pH >7.3 |
| Folinic acid (leucovorin) | Methanol poisoning | 1 mg/kg IV (max 50 mg) every 4 hours; enhances formate metabolism |
| Pyridoxine | Ethylene glycol poisoning | 50 mg IV every 6 hours; enhances glyoxylate metabolism to nontoxic glycine |
| Thiamine | Ethylene glycol poisoning | 100 mg IV every 6 hours; enhances glyoxylate metabolism |
| Hemodialysis | Severe acidosis (pH <7.1), renal failure, visual impairment, deteriorating vitals, methanol >=50 mg/dL | Until toxic alcohol <20 mg/dL and acidosis resolved |

### 6.10.5 Ethanol Alternative

If fomepizole is unavailable, ethanol is an effective alternative (competes with toxic alcohols for alcohol dehydrogenase):
- Loading dose: IV ethanol to achieve serum concentration 100-150 mg/dL
- Requires frequent monitoring of serum ethanol levels
- More sedation and respiratory depression than fomepizole
- Preferred in resource-limited settings

## 6.11 Decontamination Methods

| Method | Indications | Contraindications/Limitations |
|--------|------------|------------------------------|
| **Activated charcoal** | Within 1-2 hours of ingestion; extended-release formulations up to 4 hours | Unprotected airway (unless intubated), GI obstruction/ileus, caustic ingestion, hydrocarbon aspiration risk |
| **Whole bowel irrigation** | Body packers/stuffers, sustained-release/large ingestions, iron, lithium | Bowel obstruction, ileus, hemodynamic instability, unprotected airway |
| **Gastric lavage** | Very early (<1 hour), life-threatening ingestions where charcoal is inadequate | Unprotected airway, caustics, hydrocarbons; rarely indicated |
| **Urine alkalinization** | Salicylates, phenobarbital, chlorpropamide | Renal failure, fluid overload, concomitant cerebral edema |

**Key principles:**
- Gastric lavage is rarely indicated; activated charcoal is generally preferred when decontamination is appropriate
- Do NOT delay charcoal administration for imaging or other procedures in serious, potentially lethal ingestions
- Bowel irrigation with polyethylene glycol solution (GoLYTELY) at 1-2 L/hour until rectal effluent is clear

## 6.12 Red Flags in Toxicology

> **CRITICAL:**
- Multi-organ system involvement suggests severe poisoning or multiple agents
- Widened QRS (>100 ms) suggests TCA, diphenhydramine, or other sodium channel blocker
- Prolonged QT suggests risk for torsades de pointes (methadone, haloperidol, macrolides, quetiapine)
- Severe metabolic acidosis (pH <7.1) suggests toxic alcohols, salicylates, cyanide, or metformin
- Hemodynamic instability requiring vasopressors
- Seizures unresponsive to benzodiazepines
- Coma with unknown ingestion = broad differential; supportive care paramount

# Chapter 7: Trauma Assessment

## 7.1 Overview

Trauma remains a leading cause of death worldwide, particularly among individuals aged 1-44 years. Modern trauma care follows Advanced Trauma Life Support (ATLS) principles with emphasis on rapid primary survey, damage control resuscitation, early hemorrhage control, and prevention of secondary brain injury. Uncontrolled hemorrhage accounts for approximately 40% of trauma deaths; early identification and intervention are paramount [^606^][^607^][^613^].

## 7.2 Primary Survey: The xABCDE Approach

### 7.2.1 "x" -- Exsanguination Control (NEW: First Priority)

ATLS 10th Edition added exsanguination control as the first priority. If life-threatening external bleeding is identified, it must be controlled **before** airway management:
- **Direct pressure** with both hands on the wound
- **Tourniquet** for extremity hemorrhage (commercial preferred over improvised; apply proximal to wound; note time of application)
- **Wound packing** with hemostatic gauze for junctional wounds
- **Pelvic binder** for unstable pelvis (reduces venous bleeding significantly)

[^606^]

### 7.2.2 A -- Airway

- Assess patency: ability to speak, presence of gurgling, stridor, foreign body
- Chin lift/jaw thrust (NO head tilt in suspected cervical spine injury)
- Clear airway with suction if needed
- Oropharyngeal airway (if no gag reflex) or nasopharyngeal airway (if intact gag; avoid if basal skull fracture suspected)
- **Definitive airway (endotracheal intubation) indications:**
  - GCS <8 (cannot protect airway)
  - Severe facial fractures compromising airway
  - Airway obstruction not relieved by basic maneuvers
  - Hypoxemia despite supplemental O2
  - Need for transport with high risk of deterioration
- **C-spine immobilization** maintained throughout airway management
- **Video laryngoscopy** as default first-attempt device (improved first-pass success)

### 7.2.3 B -- Breathing

- Inspect, palpate, and auscultate the chest
- Supplemental O2 for all trauma patients initially
- **Life-threatening conditions to identify immediately:**
  - **Tension pneumothorax:** Immediate needle decompression (clinical diagnosis -- do NOT wait for CXR); 14-gauge needle, 4th-5th intercostal space, mid-axillary line or 2nd intercostal space, midclavicular line
  - **Open pneumothorax:** Three-sided occlusive dressing (allows air escape, prevents tension)
  - **Massive hemothorax:** >1500 mL blood or >200 mL/hr for 4 hours -- chest tube thoracostomy; may need thoracotomy
  - **Flail chest:** Paradoxical movement of chest wall segment; manage with analgesia (epidural preferred), positive pressure ventilation if needed
- Target SpO2 >94%

### 7.2.4 C -- Circulation

- Assess pulse, skin color/temperature, capillary refill
- Identify and control hemorrhage sources: external, chest, abdomen, pelvis, retroperitoneum, long bones
- **Two large-bore IVs (14-16 gauge)** or intraosseous access if IV unobtainable
- **Blood products, not crystalloid:** No more than 1 liter crystalloid before blood products in hemorrhagic shock
- **Activate massive transfusion protocol** if criteria met (see Section 7.5)
- **FAST exam** (see Section 7.3) for intra-abdominal hemorrhage
- **Pelvic binder** if mechanical instability or AP compression mechanism
- **Tourniquet** for extremity hemorrhage

### 7.2.5 D -- Disability

- **Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS):** Document all three components (eye, verbal, motor)
- Pupillary exam: Size, shape, symmetry, reactivity
- Blood glucose check (hypoglycemia can mimic brain injury)
- Naloxone if opioid overdose suspected
- Lateralizing signs (suggests focal brain injury)

### 7.2.6 E -- Exposure

- Completely undress patient (trauma team or paramedics should maintain privacy)
- Log-roll with C-spine precautions to examine back
- Prevent hypothermia: warm blankets, warmed IV fluids/blood, raise ambient room temperature
- Hypothermia, acidosis, and coagulopathy form the **"lethal triad"** of trauma -- each exacerbates the others

[^606^][^613^]

## 7.3 FAST Exam (Focused Assessment with Sonography for Trauma)

### 7.3.1 Standard Views

1. **Pericardial:** Subxiphoid (or parasternal) -- assess for hemopericardium/tamponade
2. **Right upper quadrant:** Hepatorenal recess (Morison's pouch) -- most sensitive for free fluid in supine patient
3. **Left upper quadrant:** Splenorenal recess -- assess around spleen and diaphragm
4. **Suprapubic:** Pelvic -- assess for fluid in rectovesical (male) or rectouterine pouch (female; Pouch of Douglas)

### 7.3.2 Interpretation

- **Positive FAST:** Free fluid (anechoic stripe) in any view in a hemodynamically unstable patient = indication for emergent laparotomy
- **Negative FAST:** Does NOT exclude injury; serial exams or CT if clinical suspicion remains
- **eFAST (extended):** Adds bilateral anterior thoracic views for pneumothorax detection (absence of lung sliding)
- If FAST negative but patient remains unstable: consider CT abdomen/pelvis, repeat FAST, or diagnostic peritoneal lavage (DPL)

## 7.4 Massive Transfusion Protocol (MTP)

### 7.4.1 Activation Criteria

- SBP <90 mmHg despite initial resuscitation efforts
- Ongoing hemorrhage with anticipated need for >4 units PRBC in 1 hour
- Clinical picture of hemorrhagic shock not responding to initial measures [^606^]

### 7.4.2 Blood Product Ratio: 1:1:1

The PROPPR trial demonstrated improved survival with a balanced 1:1:1 ratio compared to 1:1:2 [^606^][^617^]:

| Component | Ratio | Notes |
|-----------|-------|-------|
| PRBC | 1 unit | O-negative for females of childbearing potential; O-positive acceptable for males |
| Fresh Frozen Plasma (FFP) | 1 unit | Thawed AB or type-specific; provides clotting factors |
| Platelets | 1 apheresis unit (or 6-pool) | Maintains hemostasis |

### 7.4.3 Additional Interventions

- **Tranexamic acid (TXA):** 1 g IV bolus over 10 minutes + 1 g infusion over 8 hours
  - Must be given **within 3 hours of injury** (CRASH-2 trial); no benefit after 3 hours and possible harm
  - 2 g bolus for TBI patients (CRASH-3 trial) [^606^][^617^]
- **Calcium:** Monitor and replete (citrate in blood products binds ionized calcium); maintain ionized calcium >1.0 mmol/L
- **Goal:** Permissive hypotension (SBP 80-90 mmHg) in penetrating trauma WITHOUT head injury (limits dilution of clotting factors and prevents disruption of forming clots)
- **Avoid:** Excessive crystalloid (worsens coagulopathy, dilutes hemoglobin, causes hypothermia) [^606^]

## 7.5 Traumatic Brain Injury (TBI)

### 7.5.1 Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) -- Full Scoring

| Component | Score | Response |
|-----------|-------|----------|
| **Eye Opening** | 4 | Spontaneous |
| | 3 | To voice |
| | 2 | To pain |
| | 1 | None |
| **Verbal Response** | 5 | Oriented |
| | 4 | Confused |
| | 3 | Inappropriate words |
| | 2 | Incomprehensible sounds |
| | 1 | None |
| **Motor Response** | 6 | Obeys commands |
| | 5 | Localizes pain |
| | 4 | Withdraws from pain |
| | 3 | Decorticate posturing (flexion) |
| | 2 | Decerebrate posturing (extension) |
| | 1 | None |

**GCS ranges:** 3-15 (3 = deepest coma; 15 = fully alert)

### 7.5.2 TBI Severity Classification

| Severity | GCS | Features |
|----------|-----|----------|
| Mild | 13-15 | May have brief LOC (<30 min), PTA; most common; CT often normal |
| Moderate | 9-12 | Prolonged LOC; abnormal CT in ~30% |
| Severe | 3-8 | Prolonged LOC; requires airway protection; high mortality |

### 7.5.3 Indications for CT Head Imaging

CT head is indicated if ANY of the following [^653^]:
- GCS <15 at 2 hours after injury
- Suspected open or depressed skull fracture
- Any sign of basal skull fracture (raccoon eyes, Battle's sign, hemotympanum, CSF otorrhea/rhinorrhea)
- Vomiting >1 episode
- Age >65 years
- Post-traumatic seizure
- Focal neurologic deficit
- Dangerous mechanism (pedestrian struck by vehicle, occupant ejected from vehicle, fall >3 feet/5 stairs)
- Anticoagulation use (warfarin, DOACs) or known coagulopathy

### 7.5.4 Severe TBI Management (Brain Trauma Foundation 4th Edition, 2020)

**ICP Management -- Tiered Approach:** [^653^][^658^]

| Tier | Interventions | Target |
|------|--------------|--------|
| **Tier 0 (Basic)** | Head of bed 30 degrees, midline positioning, adequate sedation/analgesia, normothermia, normocapnia (PaCO2 35-40), normoglycemia, seizure prophylaxis | ICP <22 mmHg |
| **Tier 1 (First-line)** | Mannitol 0.25-1 g/kg IV OR 3% hypertonic saline 250 mL bolus | ICP <22 mmHg |
| **Tier 2 (Second-line)** | CSF drainage via external ventricular drain (EVD), brief hyperventilation (PaCO2 30-35 for rescue only) | ICP <22 mmHg |
| **Tier 3 (Rescue)** | Decompressive craniectomy (RESCUEicp trial showed mortality benefit but potentially worse functional outcomes), barbiturate coma, therapeutic hypothermia | ICP <22 mmHg |

**Key targets:**
- ICP monitoring: Recommended for salvageable severe TBI (GCS 3-8) with abnormal CT
- ICP target: <22 mmHg
- Cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP) target: 60-70 mmHg
- **Avoid hypotension** (SBP <100 mmHg in severe TBI doubles mortality)
- **Avoid hypoxia** (SpO2 <90% is associated with worse outcomes)
- **Anticonvulsants:** Levetiracetam preferred for early post-traumatic seizure prophylaxis (7 days)

## 7.6 Spinal Immobilization and Clearance

### 7.6.1 NEXUS Criteria

Cervical spine imaging is NOT required if ALL of the following criteria are met [^628^][^633^][^636^]:

1. Normal alertness (GCS 15)
2. No intoxication (alcohol or drugs)
3. No painful distracting injury
4. No posterior midline cervical tenderness
5. No focal neurologic deficit

- **Sensitivity:** 99.6% for clinically significant cervical spine injuries
- If ALL criteria met, cervical spine can be clinically cleared without imaging

### 7.6.2 Canadian C-Spine Rule (CCR)

Alternative clinical decision rule for alert, stable patients aged 16-65 [^633^]:

**Exclusion criteria:** Neurologic deficit, dangerous mechanism (fall >1 meter/5 stairs, axial load to head, MVC >100 km/hr, rollover/ejection, bicycle/motorized recreation crash, age >=65)

**Low-risk factors:** Simple rear-end MVC, sitting position in ED, ambulatory at scene, delayed onset of neck pain, absence of midline cervical tenderness

**If low-risk factors present AND able to actively rotate neck 45 degrees left and right = no imaging needed**
- **Sensitivity:** 100% for clinically significant injuries

### 7.6.3 Clearance in Unconscious Patients

- CT cervical spine is the standard initial imaging modality
- If CT negative: MRI may detect ligamentous injury if clinical concern remains
- Remove collar when CT negative AND patient awake for clinical exam OR MRI clears ligamentous injury

## 7.7 Red Flags in Trauma

> **CRITICAL:**
- Hemodynamic instability (SBP <90 mmHg) = ongoing hemorrhage until proven otherwise
- GCS declining = expanding intracranial mass lesion or hypoxia/hypotension
- Unequal pupils = uncal herniation (medical emergency requiring immediate intervention)
- Open skull fracture or CSF leak = risk of meningitis; needs neurosurgical evaluation
- Expanding neck hematoma = airway compromise imminent
- Tension pneumothorax = clinical diagnosis; needle decompression without waiting for imaging
- Peritonitis on examination = operative abdomen
- The "lethal triad" (hypothermia + acidosis + coagulopathy) = circling the drain; requires damage control surgery and aggressive resuscitation

# Chapter 8: Environmental Emergencies

## 8.1 Heat-Related Illness

### 8.1.1 Classification

| Condition | Core Temperature | Clinical Features | Management |
|-----------|-----------------|-------------------|------------|
| **Heat cramps** | Normal | Muscle cramps after exertion; sweating preserved | Rest, oral fluids with electrolytes, salt replacement, gentle stretching |
| **Heat exhaustion** | <40 degrees C (104 F) | Heavy sweating, fatigue, weakness, nausea, headache, dizziness | Cool environment, oral fluids, active cooling, rest; typically reversible |
| **Heat stroke** | **>40 degrees C (104 F)** | **CNS dysfunction** (delirium, seizures, coma) + hyperthermia | **Aggressive active cooling; mortality increases with every minute of delay** |

### 8.1.2 Heat Stroke: Emergency Management

Heat stroke is a **life-threatening emergency** with mortality up to 30-80% if cooling is delayed. The single most important intervention is **rapid active cooling** [^610^][^612^][^616^]:

**Cooling Targets:**
- Rate: 0.15-0.2 degrees C/minute (ideally cool to 38-39 degrees C within 30-60 minutes)
- Target temperature: 38-39 degrees C (100.4-102.2 F); do NOT overcool

**Cooling Methods:**

| Method | Cooling Rate | Best Application |
|--------|-------------|-----------------|
| Cold water immersion | Fastest (0.15-0.35 C/min) | **Exertional heat stroke** (gold standard); collapse tubs, ice water |
| Evaporative + convective | Moderate | Classic heat stroke, hospital setting; spray water + fan |
| Ice packs to groin/axillae/neck | Slow | Adjunctive; massage skin to prevent reflex vasoconstriction |
| Cooled IV fluids | Minimal | Adjunctive only |

**Key Management Points:**
- **NO role for antipyretics** (acetaminophen, aspirin, NSAIDs) -- heat stroke is not a fever; these may worsen hepatic or renal injury
- **NO role for dantrolene** (ineffective in heat stroke; used for malignant hyperthermia only)
- Cooled crystalloid IV fluids for volume resuscitation
- Monitor for complications: rhabdomyolysis, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), acute kidney injury, hepatic failure, acute respiratory distress syndrome
- **Admit for at least 48 hours** (delayed organ dysfunction can occur) [^616^]

### 8.1.3 Exertional vs. Classic Heat Stroke

| Feature | Exertional Heat Stroke | Classic (Non-Exertional) Heat Stroke |
|---------|----------------------|-------------------------------------|
| Population | Young, healthy (athletes, military) | Elderly, chronically ill |
| Setting | Hot + physical activity | Heat wave, no air conditioning |
| Sweating | Often present | Often absent (anhidrosis) |
| Best cooling | Cold water immersion | Evaporative cooling |
| Rhabdomyolysis | Common | Less common |
| Coagulopathy | Less common | More common |

## 8.2 Hypothermia

### 8.2.1 Classification (Swiss Staging System)

| Stage | Core Temperature | Consciousness | Cardiac |
|-------|-----------------|---------------|---------|
| HT I (Mild) | 35-32 C (95-89.6 F) | Conscious, shivering | Normal sinus rhythm |
| HT II (Moderate) | 32-28 C (89.6-82.4 F) | Impaired consciousness; shivering stops | Risk of arrhythmias; AF common |
| HT III (Severe) | 28-24 C (82.4-75.2 F) | Unconscious | Osborn J waves; high risk of VF |
| HT IV (Profound) | 24-13.7 C (75.2-56.7 F) | Apparent death (may appear dead) | VF, asystole; vital signs may be undetectable |

### 8.2.2 ECG Findings

- **Osborn (J) waves:** Positive deflection at the end of the QRS complex; most prominent in leads V3-V6; amplitude roughly proportional to degree of hypothermia
- Bradycardia, prolonged PR and QT intervals
- Atrial fibrillation (common in moderate hypothermia)
- Ventricular fibrillation risk increases dramatically <28 C; can be triggered by rough handling or procedures

### 8.2.3 Management

| Stage | Rewarming Strategy | Details |
|-------|-------------------|---------|
| **Mild (HT I)** | Passive external rewarming | Warm blankets, warm dry environment, warm sweet drinks if alert and able to swallow |
| **Moderate (HT II)** | Active external rewarming | Warm air circulation (Bair Hugger), warm IV fluids (40-42 C) |
| **Severe (HT III-IV)** | Active internal rewarming | Warm IV fluids, warm humidified O2 (42-46 C), bladder/gastric lavage with warm fluid, thoracic lavage |
| **Cardiac arrest** | Extracorporeal rewarming | **ECMO or cardiopulmonary bypass** -- most effective method; preferred if available |

**Special Considerations:**
- CPR: Handle patient gently; rough handling can precipitate VF
- Defibrillation: If VF/VT present at core <30 C, deliver up to 3 shocks; if unsuccessful, withhold further shocks until core temperature >30 C
- Medications: Withhold all medications until core >30 C; double dosing intervals until normothermia (impaired drug metabolism and clearance)
- "You are not dead until you are warm and dead" -- profound hypothermia patients have survived neurologically intact after prolonged CPR [^609^]

## 8.3 Drowning

Drowning is defined as the process of experiencing respiratory impairment from submersion/immersion in liquid. It is a leading cause of accidental death worldwide [^609^].

### 8.3.1 Resuscitation

1. **Start with 5 rescue breaths** before chest compressions (oxygenation is the priority in drowning)
2. Then proceed with standard CPR protocol (30:2 or continuous compressions with advanced airway)
3. **Do NOT attempt to drain water** from the lungs (delays CPR, risks aspiration, ineffective)
4. C-spine immobilization should NOT delay rescue from water unless traumatic mechanism is clear
5. **All drowning victims need hospital evaluation** even if they appear asymptomatic (risk of delayed ARDS)

### 8.3.2 Prognostic Factors

| Factor | Better Outcome | Poorer Outcome |
|--------|---------------|----------------|
| Submersion duration | <5 minutes | >10 minutes |
| Time to effective CPR | <10 minutes | >25 minutes |
| GCS at presentation | >13 | <5 |
| Pupillary response | Reactive | Fixed and dilated |
| Water type | Salt vs fresh water distinction **not clinically relevant** | |

### 8.3.3 Post-Rescue Management

- Monitor for delayed complications: ARDS (24-72 hours), pulmonary edema, electrolyte abnormalities (generally mild despite theoretical concerns), acute kidney injury, CNS anoxic injury
- Consider targeted temperature management for comatose survivors (similar to post-cardiac arrest care)

## 8.4 Altitude Illness

Altitude illness encompasses a spectrum of disorders caused by hypobaric hypoxia at high elevation. Risk increases with faster ascent profiles and higher sleeping altitudes.

### 8.4.1 Classification

| Condition | Elevation (typical) | Features |
|-----------|-------------------|----------|
| **Acute Mountain Sickness (AMS)** | >2500 m (8200 ft) | Headache + GI symptoms (anorexia, nausea) OR fatigue/dizziness; Lake Louise Score |
| **High-Altitude Cerebral Edema (HACE)** | >4300 m (14,000 ft) | End-stage AMS; ataxia, altered mental status; can progress to coma and death |
| **High-Altitude Pulmonary Edema (HAPE)** | >2500 m | Most common cause of death from altitude illness; dyspnea at rest, cough, frothy sputum, cyanosis |

### 8.4.2 Treatment by Condition

| Condition | Primary Treatment | Adjunctive Medications |
|-----------|-------------------|----------------------|
| Mild AMS | Stop ascent; rest; symptomatic treatment | Acetazolamide 250 mg BID PO |
| Moderate-severe AMS | **Descent 300-1000 m** (definitive); supplemental O2 | Dexamethasone 4 mg q6h PO/IM/IV |
| HACE | **Immediate descent** (mandatory) | Dexamethasone 8 mg load, then 4 mg q6h; O2; portable hyperbaric chamber if descent impossible |
| HAPE | **Immediate descent** + strict rest | Nifedipine 30 mg SR BID; O2; PDE-5 inhibitors (sildenafil/tadalafil) if nifedipine unavailable |

[^660^][^661^]

### 8.4.3 Medication Dosing Summary

| Medication | Indication | Dose |
|------------|-----------|------|
| Acetazolamide | AMS/HACE prevention | 125 mg BID PO (250 mg BID if >100 kg) |
| Acetazolamide | AMS treatment | 250 mg BID PO |
| Dexamethasone | AMS/HACE prevention (alternative) | 2 mg q6h or 4 mg q12h |
| Dexamethasone | HACE treatment | 8 mg IV/IM/PO load, then 4 mg q6h |
| Nifedipine | HAPE prevention | 20 mg SR q8h or 30 mg SR q12h |
| Nifedipine | HAPE treatment | 30 mg SR q12h |

### 8.4.4 Prevention

- **Gradual ascent:** No more than 500 m (1,600 ft) sleeping altitude increase per day above 3000 m; add a rest day every 600-900 m gain
- **Pre-acclimatization** if possible
- **Acetazolamide 125 mg BID** starting day before ascent (carbonic anhydrase inhibitor stimulates ventilation)
- **Dexamethasone** alternative if acetazolamide contraindicated
- **Nifedipine 20-30 mg SR** for HAPE prevention in those with prior HAPE history
- Avoid alcohol and sedatives in the first 48 hours at altitude
- Avoid overexertion in the first 24-48 hours

## 8.5 Electrical Injuries

### 8.5.1 AC vs. DC Current

| Feature | AC (Alternating Current) | DC (Direct Current) |
|---------|-------------------------|---------------------|
| Source | Household/business electricity | Batteries, lightning, some industrial |
| Danger | More dangerous at lower voltages | Less dangerous at equivalent voltage |
| Muscle effect | Tetany (continuous contraction) -- victim cannot let go | Single intense contraction -- often throws victim away |
| "Let-go" threshold | ~6 mA (women) to ~9 mA (men) | ~60-75 mA |
| Ventricular fibrillation risk | Higher | Lower |

### 8.5.2 Lightning Strikes

- Mechanism: Direct strike, side flash, or ground current (most common)
- Cardiac: Immediate asystole (primary arrhythmia); respiratory arrest may persist longer
- **keraunoparalysis:** Transient lower extremity paralysis (autonomic dysfunction)
- **Lichtenberg figures:** Pathognomonic fern-like cutaneous pattern (NOT true burns; diagnostic of lightning strike)
- **Reverse triage:** Attend to those in apparent cardiac arrest FIRST (lightning strike victims have better prognosis than typical cardiac arrest victims due to young, healthy population)

### 8.5.3 Management

- ABCs; prolonged CPR may be successful (young victims)
- Fluid resuscitation: Rhabdomyolysis is common; urine output goal 1-2 mL/kg/hour
- Urine alkalinization if rhabdomyolysis confirmed
- Monitor for compartment syndrome (current follows tissue planes)
- ECG for all patients; troponins if high-voltage exposure or symptoms
- Secondary survey for exit wounds (typically worse than entry wounds)
- Fasciotomy if compartment pressures elevated

## 8.6 Envenomation (Snakebite)

### 8.6.1 General Principles

Snakebite envenomation causes significant morbidity and mortality globally. Management principles apply across most venomous species [^650^][^651^][^655^][^656^][^657^].

### 8.6.2 First Aid

| Do | Do NOT |
|----|--------|
| Immobilize patient and bitten limb (splint, keep at heart level) | Apply tourniquet (causes ischemia, necrosis) |
| Remove rings/jewelry from affected limb | Cut or suck the wound |
| Pressure immobilization bandage (elastic bandage over entire limb; controversial; recommended for neurotoxic elapid bites) | Apply electric shock |
| Transport to medical facility rapidly | Apply ice |

### 8.6.3 Hospital Assessment

- ABCs; 20-minute whole blood clotting test (20WBCT) for coagulopathic envenomation
- Monitor for at least 8 hours if asymptomatic; 12-24 hours if envenomated
- Mark leading edge of swelling; measure circumference serially
- Labs: CBC, coagulation studies, creatinine, CK, urinalysis (for myoglobinuria)

### 8.6.4 Antivenom

- **IV administration** (same dose adults and children -- dose is based on venom load, not body weight)
- **Indications:** Systemic envenomation (shock, spontaneous bleeding, coagulopathy, neurotoxicity, AKI, rapidly progressive local swelling crossing a joint)
- **Monitor for anaphylaxis** during and after infusion (first 2 hours highest risk); treat with epinephrine IM if reactions occur
- **Repeat dosing** if bleeding persists or worsens within 2 hours

### 8.6.5 Discharge

- Asymptomatic patients with negative 20WBCT at 24 hours may be discharged
- Bleeding precautions for 2 weeks after hemotoxic envenomation (delayed coagulopathy)
- Follow-up at 3-4 days; watch for serum sickness (fever, arthralgia, rash) 5-14 days post-antivenom

## 8.7 Red Flags

> **CRITICAL:**
- Heat stroke: CNS dysfunction + core temperature >40 C = active cooling NOW; mortality rises with delay
- Hypothermia: Core <28 C = risk of VF with any manipulation; handle gently
- Drowning: Submersion >10 minutes or no CPR within 10 minutes = grave prognosis
- HACE: Ataxia + altered mental status at altitude = immediate descent; death can occur within hours
- HAPE: Dyspnea at rest + pink frothy sputum at altitude = descent + nifedipine
- Lightning strike: Cardiac arrest in young person after storm = reverse triage (resuscitate first)
- Snakebite: Respiratory paralysis (neurotoxic), active bleeding/coagulopathy (hemotoxic), anuria (nephrotoxic)

# Chapter 9: Acute Respiratory Failure

## 9.1 Overview

Acute respiratory failure is the inability of the respiratory system to maintain adequate gas exchange, defined as PaO2 <60 mmHg with FiO2 >0.6 or PaCO2 >50 mmHg with pH <7.35. It is one of the most common reasons for admission to the intensive care unit and carries significant morbidity and mortality. Understanding the classification, non-invasive and invasive support strategies, and ventilator management is essential for all acute care clinicians.

## 9.2 Classification

| Parameter | Type I (Hypoxemic) | Type II (Hypercapnic) |
|-----------|-------------------|----------------------|
| **Mechanism** | V/Q mismatch, intrapulmonary shunt, diffusion limitation, alveolar hypoventilation | Alveolar hypoventilation |
| **PaO2** | Decreased | May be normal or decreased |
| **PaCO2** | Normal or decreased | Elevated (>50 mmHg) |
| **pH** | Normal or elevated | Decreased (<7.35) |
| **Common causes** | Pneumonia, pulmonary edema, ARDS, pulmonary embolism, atelectasis, interstitial lung disease | COPD exacerbation, neuromuscular disease (ALS, MG, GBS), chest wall disorders, opioid/benzodiazepine overdose, severe obesity (OHS) |
| **A-a gradient** | Increased | Normal |

**Mixed Type I and Type II failure** is common in clinical practice, especially in decompensated COPD with pneumonia or in severe ARDS with ventilatory muscle fatigue.

## 9.3 Non-Invasive Ventilation (NIV)

Non-invasive ventilation (delivered via tight-fitting mask) avoids the complications of endotracheal intubation and is the first-line ventilatory support for selected patients with acute respiratory failure [^639^][^641^][^643^].

### 9.3.1 CPAP vs. BiPAP

| Feature | CPAP (Continuous Positive Airway Pressure) | BiPAP/BPAP (Bilevel Positive Airway Pressure) |
|---------|-------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------|
| **Pressure delivery** | Single continuous positive pressure | Two pressure levels: IPAP (inspiratory) and EPAP (expiratory) |
| **Primary mechanism** | Alveolar recruitment, reduces afterload (cardiac) | Augments tidal volume, reduces work of breathing |
| **Primary indication** | Hypoxemic respiratory failure (cardiogenic pulmonary edema) | Hypercapnic respiratory failure (COPD, OHS) |
| **Also used for** | OSA, chest wall trauma, selected ARDS, pre-oxygenation | Neuromuscular disease, post-extubation support |
| **Key difference** | No pressure support for inspiration | Pressure support = IPAP - EPAP |

[^639^][^641^]

### 9.3.2 Initial NIV Settings

| Parameter | CPAP | BiPAP |
|-----------|------|-------|
| Initial setting | 5-10 cmH2O | IPAP 10-15; EPAP 3-5 |
| Titration target | Up to 15 based on O2 and tolerance | Increase IPAP for ventilation; EPAP for oxygenation |
| Maximum (without specialist input) | 15 cmH2O | IPAP 30; EPAP 8 |
| Backup rate | N/A | 12-16 breaths/minute |
| FiO2 | Start 0.4-0.6; titrate to SpO2 88-92% (COPD) or 92-96% | Same |

[^639^][^641^][^643^]

### 9.3.3 NIV Indications by Condition

| Condition | Evidence Level | Recommendation |
|-----------|---------------|----------------|
| **AECOPD** (Acute Exacerbation of COPD) | Strong (NIV reduces intubation by ~50%, mortality by ~46%) | BiPAP when pH <7.35 + PaCO2 >49 mmHg; first-line therapy |
| **Cardiogenic pulmonary edema** | Strong | CPAP or BiPAP both reduce intubation and mortality equivalently |
| **Acute asthma** | Weak/Not recommended | ERS/ATS guidelines do NOT recommend routine NIV [^642^]; intubate if failing medical therapy |
| **AHRF (pneumonia/ARDS)** | Moderate | Select patients only; CPAP or BiPAP; low threshold to intubate |
| **Post-extubation (high-risk)** | Strong | Preventive BiPAP reduces re-intubation rates |
| **Immunocompromised** | Moderate | May avoid intubation and associated infection risks |
| **Obesity hypoventilation** | Moderate | BiPAP effective; high EPAP often needed |

[^641^][^642^]

### 9.3.4 NIV Contraindications

**Absolute contraindications:**
- Cardiac or respiratory arrest
- Inability to protect airway (GCS <8)
- Severe facial deformity, trauma, or burns preventing mask seal
- Fixed upper airway obstruction
- Undrained pneumothorax
- Hemodynamic instability (relative -- may be attempted with close monitoring)

**Relative contraindications:**
- Hemodynamic instability requiring vasopressors
- Severe confusion or agitation
- Copious secretions or active vomiting
- Severe acidosis (pH <7.15)
- Recent upper GI or upper airway surgery
- Bowel obstruction [^641^][^643^][^645^]

### 9.3.5 NIV Failure: When to Intubate

- pH not improving after 1-2 hours of NIV
- Worsening arterial blood gases after 1 hour
- Worsening mental status (somnolence, agitation)
- Respiratory rate >35/minute despite NIV
- Hemodynamic instability
- Inability to maintain SpO2 >88% despite maximal settings
- Patient intolerance despite coaching and adjustment

[^641^][^643^]

## 9.4 Intubation Criteria and Rapid Sequence Intubation (RSI)

### 9.4.1 Indications for Intubation

- Failure of NIV after 1-2 hour trial
- GCS <8 with inability to protect airway
- Severe acidosis (pH <7.25) with clinical deterioration
- Severe hypoxemia (PaO2/FiO2 <100) refractory to supplemental O2 or NIV
- Hemodynamic instability requiring escalating support
- Cardiac or respiratory arrest
- Airway obstruction
- Severe traumatic brain injury requiring controlled ventilation

### 9.4.2 Rapid Sequence Intubation (RSI)

RSI is the standard method for securing the airway in emergent conditions, combining rapid-acting induction agents with neuromuscular blockade to optimize intubating conditions [^646^][^648^][^649^].

**The 7 P's of RSI:**

1. **Preparation:** Suction, oxygen, airway equipment (multiple blades, bougie, supraglottic device, surgical airway kit), drugs, continuous monitoring, end-tidal CO2 capnography
2. **Preoxygenation:** 100% FiO2 for 3-5 minutes; nasal cannula at 15 L/min for apneic oxygenation; position patient head-elevated
3. **Pretreatment:** Lidocaine 1-1.5 mg/kg IV (if time; blunts cough/ICP response); fentanyl for hemodynamic optimization
4. **Paralysis with induction:** (see table below)
5. **Protection and positioning:** Cricoid pressure (controversial; optional); optimal positioning (ear-to-sternal notch alignment)
6. **Placement with proof:** Direct visualization; confirm with waveform capnography (gold standard), chest rise, bilateral breath sounds
7. **Post-intubation management:** CXR, sedation, analgesia, lung-protective ventilation, hemodynamic optimization

### 9.4.3 RSI Medications

| Agent | Class | Dose | Notes |
|-------|-------|------|-------|
| **Ketamine** | Dissociative anesthetic | 1.5-2 mg/kg IV | **Preferred for hemodynamically unstable**; bronchodilation; preserves respiratory drive; sympathomimetic |
| **Etomidate** | Imidazole derivative | 0.3-0.4 mg/kg IV | Hemodynamically neutral; rapid onset; adrenal suppression concern (single dose likely clinically insignificant) |
| **Propofol** | Alkylphenol | 1.5-2.5 mg/kg IV | For hemodynamically stable patients; hypotension risk; antiemetic |
| **Rocuronium** | Non-depolarizing NMBA | 1-1.2 mg/kg IV | **Preferred NMBA**; onset 60 seconds; duration 40-90 minutes; reversible with sugammadex |
| **Succinylcholine** | Depolarizing NMBA | 1.5 mg/kg IV | 45-second onset; 5-10 minute duration; contraindications: hyperkalemia risk (burns >24h, crush injury >24h, denervation, myopathy), MH susceptibility, pseudocholinesterase deficiency |

[^646^][^649^]

**Special Populations:**
- **Shock:** Ketamine or reduced-dose etomidate; avoid propofol
- **Severe asthma:** Ketamine (bronchodilator properties)
- **Increased ICP:** Etomidate or propofol; avoid ketamine (historical concern; evidence mixed)
- **RV failure:** Etomidate; avoid propofol; minimize apnea time

## 9.5 Ventilator Management: Lung-Protective Strategy

### 9.5.1 Core Settings

| Parameter | Setting | Rationale |
|-----------|---------|-----------|
| Mode | Volume or pressure control | Either acceptable; volume control most commonly used |
| Tidal volume | **6 mL/kg predicted body weight** (range 4-8) | Prevents volutrauma; ARDSNet protocol |
| Plateau pressure | **<30 cmH2O** | Prevents barotrauma |
| Driving pressure | **<15 cmH2O** (plateau minus PEEP) | Strongest predictor of survival in ARDS |
| PEEP | 5-15 cmH2O, titrated | Alveolar recruitment; prevents atelectrauma |
| FiO2 | Titrate to SpO2 88-95% (PaO2 55-80) | Avoid hyperoxia (PaO2 >100 may increase mortality) |
| Respiratory rate | 20-35/minute | Maintain pH >7.25; higher rates may be needed |
| I:E ratio | 1:1 to 1:3 | Prolonged expiration for obstructive disease |

### 9.5.2 Predicted Body Weight Calculation

| Gender | Formula |
|--------|---------|
| Male | 50 + 0.91 x (height in cm - 152.4) |
| Female | 45.5 + 0.91 x (height in cm - 152.4) |

## 9.6 ARDS Berlin Definition

The Berlin Definition (2012) replaced the previous American-European Consensus Conference definition, providing greater specificity and prognostic stratification [^611^]:

| Parameter | Definition |
|-----------|-----------|
| **Timing** | Acute onset within 1 week of known clinical insult or new/worsening respiratory symptoms |
| **Chest imaging** | Bilateral opacities not fully explained by effusions, lobar/lung collapse, or nodules |
| **Origin of edema** | Not fully explained by cardiac failure or fluid overload; need objective assessment (echo) if no risk factor |
| **Mild ARDS** | 200 < PaO2/FiO2 <= 300 with PEEP or CPAP >=5 cmH2O |
| **Moderate ARDS** | 100 < PaO2/FiO2 <= 200 with PEEP >=5 cmH2O |
| **Severe ARDS** | PaO2/FiO2 <= 100 with PEEP >=5 cmH2O |

[^611^]

## 9.7 ARDS Management

### 9.7.1 Evidence-Based Interventions

1. **Lung-protective ventilation:** TV 4-8 mL/kg PBW, plateau <30 cmH2O, driving pressure <15 cmH2O [^604^][^611^]
2. **Prone positioning:** For moderate-severe ARDS (PaO2/FiO2 <150); minimum 16 hours per session; demonstrated 16% absolute mortality reduction (PROSEVA trial) [^604^][^614^]
3. **Conservative fluid management:** After initial hemodynamic stabilization; diurese to achieve even or negative fluid balance (FACTT trial)
4. **Neuromuscular blockade:** Cisatracurium infusion for 48 hours if PaO2/FiO2 <150 (ACURASYS trial); intermittent boluses preferred over continuous for longer duration
5. **ECMO (Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenization):** For refractory hypoxemia when conventional management fails; experienced centers only (EOLIA trial showed trend toward benefit) [^615^]

### 9.7.2 PEEP Strategy

- Titrated to oxygenation and compliance using PEEP/FiO2 tables (ARDSNet)
- Higher PEEP for moderate-severe ARDS
- Monitor hemodynamics (higher PEEP can reduce venous return and cardiac output)
- **Driving pressure** (plateau pressure minus PEEP) is the most predictive parameter of survival -- keep <15 cmH2O [^615^]

### 9.7.3 Refractory Hypoxemia

When conventional management fails to achieve adequate oxygenation:
- Prone positioning (if not already initiated)
- Inhaled vasodilators (epoprostenol, inhaled nitric oxide) -- temporary bridge; no proven mortality benefit
- Recruitment maneuvers (controversial; transient high pressures to reopen alveoli)
- ECMO referral
- High PEEP strategy with careful hemodynamic monitoring
- Consider alternative diagnoses (pulmonary vasculitis, fat embolism, amniotic fluid embolism)

## 9.8 Red Flags

> **CRITICAL:**
- PaO2/FiO2 <100 (severe ARDS) = very high mortality; maximal support needed
- Refractory hypoxemia despite lung-protective ventilation and prone positioning = consider ECMO
- Ventilator-induced lung injury: Barotrauma (pneumothorax), volutrauma, atelectrauma, biotrauma
- Rising plateau pressure despite constant tidal volume = decreasing compliance (worsening ARDS, pneumothorax, or patient-ventilator asynchrony)
- Failure of NIV with delayed intubation (worse outcomes than early intubation)

# Chapter 10: Renal and Electrolyte Emergencies

## 10.1 Acute Kidney Injury (AKI)

### 10.1.1 KDIGO Staging and Definition

The Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria are the internationally accepted standard for AKI definition and staging:

| Stage | Serum Creatinine | Urine Output |
|-------|-----------------|--------------|
| **1** | 1.5-1.9 times baseline OR >=0.3 mg/dL increase within 48 hours | <0.5 mL/kg/hour for 6-12 hours |
| **2** | 2.0-2.9 times baseline | <0.5 mL/kg/hour for >=12 hours |
| **3** | >=3.0 times baseline OR >=4.0 mg/dL OR initiation of renal replacement therapy | <0.3 mL/kg/hour for >=24 hours OR anuria >=12 hours |

### 10.1.2 Etiology Framework

| Category | Mechanism | Common Causes |
|----------|-----------|---------------|
| **Prerenal** | Decreased renal perfusion | Hypovolemia (hemorrhage, dehydration, burns), CHF, cirrhosis/hepatorenal syndrome, nephrotic syndrome, renovascular disease, sepsis |
| **Intrinsic** | Direct parenchymal injury | ATN (ischemic or nephrotoxic -- aminoglycosides, contrast, rhabdomyolysis, cisplatin), AIN (NSAIDs, antibiotics, PPIs), glomerulonephritis, thrombotic microangiopathy |
| **Postrenal** | Obstruction of urinary outflow | BPH/prostate cancer, ureteral obstruction (stones, tumor, retroperitoneal fibrosis), neurogenic bladder, obstructed catheter |

### 10.1.3 Management Principles

1. **Treat underlying cause:** Volume resuscitation for prerenal, relieve obstruction for postrenal, discontinue nephrotoxins
2. **Fluid management:** Resuscitate if volume-depleted; restrict if volume-overloaded (goal even or negative balance in ICU)
3. **Avoid nephrotoxins:** NSAIDs, aminoglycosides, IV contrast (when possible), ACE inhibitors in bilateral renal artery stenosis
4. **Medication dose adjustment:** All renally cleared medications require dosing adjustment
5. **Monitor:** Daily creatinine, electrolytes (especially potassium), fluid balance, urine output
6. **Indications for renal replacement therapy (RRT):** Remember **AEIOU**

### 10.1.4 Indications for Dialysis (AEIOU)

| Letter | Indication | Detail |
|--------|-----------|--------|
| **A** | Acidosis | Refractory metabolic acidosis (pH <7.1 despite bicarbonate therapy) |
| **E** | Electrolytes | Refractory hyperkalemia; other life-threatening electrolyte abnormalities |
| **I** | Intoxications | Dialyzable toxins (ethylene glycol, methanol, lithium, salicylates, isopropanol) |
| **O** | Overload | Volume overload refractory to diuretics (pulmonary edema) |
| **U** | Uremia | Uremic pericarditis, uremic encephalopathy, uremic bleeding |

## 10.2 Hyperkalemia

Hyperkalemia is one of the most immediately life-threatening electrolyte abnormalities. It most commonly occurs in the setting of chronic kidney disease, acute kidney injury, or medication-induced potassium retention (ACE inhibitors, ARBs, potassium-sparing diuretics, NSAIDs, heparin, beta-blockers, TMP-SMX).

### 10.2.1 ECG Changes (Progressive and Predictive)

| Stage | ECG Finding | Clinical Significance |
|-------|-------------|----------------------|
| Early | Peaked, narrow-based T waves ("tented" T waves) | Earliest sign; often seen at K+ 5.5-6.5 |
| Progressive | PR interval prolongation, P wave flattening/disappearance | AV conduction impairment |
| Advanced | QRS widening (>120 ms), intraventricular conduction delay | Pre-malignant; cardiac conduction system severely affected |
| Pre-arrest | Sine wave pattern (merged QRS-T waves) | Minutes away from cardiac arrest |
| Arrest | Ventricular fibrillation, asystole, pulseless electrical activity | Immediate ACLS required |

**Critical note:** The correlation between potassium level and ECG changes is imperfect. Some patients may have life-threatening hyperkalemia with minimal ECG changes, while others show marked changes at moderately elevated levels. Do NOT delay treatment pending ECG changes.

### 10.2.2 Three-Step Management Approach

**Step 1: Stabilize Myocardium (Minutes -- Works Immediately)**

| Agent | Dose | Onset | Duration | Key Notes |
|-------|------|-------|----------|-----------|
| **Calcium gluconate 10%** | 30 mL IV over 10 minutes via large-bore IV with continuous cardiac monitoring | 1-3 minutes | 30-60 minutes | Does NOT lower potassium; protects myocardium from depolarization; may repeat in 5-10 minutes if ECG changes persist |
| **Calcium chloride 10%** | 10 mL IV via central line | 1-3 minutes | 30-60 minutes | 3x more elemental calcium than gluconate; preferred if hemodynamically unstable; tissue necrosis if extravasation |

**Do NOT give calcium through a line containing bicarbonate** (precipitation risk). Flush line between administrations.

**Step 2: Shift K+ into Cells (Minutes to Hours -- Temporary)**

| Agent | Dose | Onset | Duration | Key Notes |
|-------|------|-------|----------|-----------|
| **Regular insulin + glucose** | 10 units regular insulin IV + 50 mL D50W IV over 30 minutes | 15-30 minutes | 2-6 hours | Check glucose every 30-60 minutes; recheck K+ at 60 minutes; can reduce K+ by 0.5-1.5 mEq/L [^625^] |
| **Beta-2 agonist (salbutamol/albuterol)** | 10-20 mg nebulized | 30 minutes | 2-4 hours | Additive with insulin; tachycardia side effect; 20 mg more effective than 10 mg |
| **Sodium bicarbonate** | 50-100 mEq IV | 15-30 minutes | 1-2 hours | Most effective if pH <7.2; less effective in anuric patients |

**Step 3: Remove K+ from Body (Hours -- Definitive)**

| Agent | Dose | Onset | Notes |
|-------|------|-------|-------|
| **Sodium zirconium cyclosilicate (SZC, Lokelma)** | 10 g PO three times daily | 1-2 hours | Non-absorbed; exchanges K+ for Na+ and H+; preferred over SPS [^625^] |
| **Sodium polystyrene sulfonate (SPS, Kayexalate)** | 15-30 g PO or PR | 2-6 hours | Sorbitol-free only (intestinal necrosis risk with sorbitol); slower onset |
| **Loop diuretics** | Furosemide 40-80 mg IV (higher if diuretic-naive) | 15-30 minutes | Only if euvolemic or hypervolemic; monitor for volume depletion |
| **Hemodialysis** | Dialysis prescription | Immediate during session | Definitive therapy for severe/refractory hyperkalemia; emergent dialysis if life-threatening ECG changes |

[^625^][^626^]

### 10.2.3 Summary Hyperkalemia Protocol

For **life-threatening hyperkalemia** (ECG changes, K+ >6.5, or rapidly rising):
1. Calcium gluconate/chloride immediately (myocardial protection)
2. Insulin 10U + D50W (intracellular shift)
3. Albuterol 10-20 mg nebulized (adjunct shift)
4. Bicarbonate if acidemic (shift)
5. SZC 10 g PO/NG (removal)
6. Furosemide if not volume-depleted (removal)
7. Emergent dialysis if refractory or severe AKI

## 10.3 Hyponatremia

Hyponatremia (serum Na <135 mEq/L) is the most common electrolyte abnormality in hospitalized patients. The key emergency concern is **symptomatic hyponatremia** requiring rapid partial correction, balanced against the catastrophic risk of **osmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS)** from overly rapid correction.

### 10.3.1 Symptomatic Severe Hyponatremia (Emergency Treatment)

Symptoms: Seizures, altered mental status, coma, respiratory depression, focal neurologic deficits

| Intervention | Dose | Goal |
|-------------|------|------|
| **3% hypertonic saline** | 100 mL IV bolus over 10 minutes | Raise Na by 4-6 mEq/L acutely to stop seizures |
| May repeat | Up to 2 additional 100 mL boluses | Each separated by 10 minutes; check Na between boluses |

**After acute symptom resolution:** Limit further correction rate to prevent ODS.

### 10.3.2 Rate of Correction (Critical Safety Limits)

| Timeframe | Maximum Correction | High-Risk Patients* |
|-----------|-------------------|---------------------|
| **First 24 hours** | <8-12 mEq/L | <8 mEq/L |
| **First 48 hours** | <18 mEq/L | <12 mEq/L |

*High-risk patients for ODS: Na <115 mEq/L at baseline, alcohol use disorder, liver disease, malnutrition/hypokalemia, burns, history of severe hypokalemia [^624^][^627^]

### 10.3.3 Osmotic Demyelination Syndrome (ODS)

ODS (formerly central pontine myelinolysis) is a devastating neurological complication of overly rapid correction of chronic hyponatremia:

- **Pathophysiology:** Rapid water shifts out of brain cells that have adapted to hypotonicity, causing oligodendrocyte apoptosis and demyelination
- **Risk factors:** Alcohol use disorder, liver disease, malnutrition, severe hypokalemia, burns, Na <=105 mEq/L
- **Can occur even with correction <=10 mEq/L/24h if risk factors present**
- **Manifestations:** Locked-in syndrome (pontine myelinolysis), quadriplegia, pseudobulbar palsy, dysarthria, dysphagia, parkinsonism, behavioral changes
- **Onset:** Typically 2-6 days after rapid correction
- **Prevention:** Strict adherence to correction limits; if over-correction is occurring, administer desmopressin 2-4 mcg IV to re-lower sodium; ensure thiamine supplementation before glucose-containing fluids

[^624^][^627^]

### 10.3.4 Asymptomatic Chronic Hyponatremia

- **Fluid restriction:** First-line for SIADH; <800-1200 mL/day total fluid intake
- **Salt tablets:** 1-2 g TID (increase solute intake)
- **Urea:** 15-30 g/day (osmotic diuresis; bitter taste but effective and safe)
- **Tolvaptan:** Vasopressin antagonist; start 15 mg daily; monitor Na every 6-8 hours initially; avoid in liver disease; expensive
- **Demeclocycline:** 300-600 mg BID (induces nephrogenic diabetes insipidus; slower onset)
- **Treat underlying cause:** Discontinue offending medications, treat adrenal insufficiency, address hypothyroidism

### 10.3.5 Hyponatremia Classification by Volume Status

| Volume Status | Common Causes | Treatment Approach |
|--------------|---------------|-------------------|
| **Hypovolemic** | Diuretics, GI losses, adrenal insufficiency, cerebral salt wasting | Isotonic saline repletion; treat underlying cause |
| **Euvolemic** | SIADH (most common), hypothyroidism, glucocorticoid deficiency, psychogenic polydipsia, beer potomania | Fluid restriction; salt/urea/tolvaptan for SIADH |
| **Hypervolemic** | CHF, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome, advanced CKD | Fluid restriction; treat underlying condition; loop diuretics for edema |

## 10.4 Severe Metabolic Acidosis

### 10.4.1 Sodium Bicarbonate Indications

Sodium bicarbonate is NOT indicated for all metabolic acidosis. Specific indications include:
- pH <7.1 with hemodynamic compromise
- Bicarbonate <5 mEq/L
- Cardiac arrest with hyperkalemia
- Salicylate overdose
- Tricyclic antidepressant overdose
- Methanol/ethylene glycol poisoning

### 10.4.2 Bicarbonate Dosing

- **Bolus:** 1-2 mEq/kg IV (1-2 ampules of 8.4% sodium bicarbonate)
- **Infusion:** Titrate to pH target of 7.2 (NOT normalization)
- **Monitor for complications:** Volume overload, hypokalemia, hypocalcemia (ionized calcium decreases with alkalosis), hypernatremia, paradoxical intracellular acidosis (CO2 production)
- Hypertonic bicarbonate (8.4%) preferred in fluid-overloaded patients

## 10.5 Red Flags

> **CRITICAL:**
- Hyperkalemia with ECG changes (peaked T waves, QRS widening, sine wave) = medical emergency requiring immediate calcium + insulin/glucose
- Hyponatremia with seizures or coma = 3% hypertonic saline immediately
- Correction of chronic hyponatremia >12-18 mEq/L in 24 hours = risk of osmotic demyelination syndrome (locked-in syndrome)
- Oliguria <0.3 mL/kg/hour in AKI = possible need for dialysis
- Uremic pericarditis (friction rub, chest pain, tamponade physiology) = emergent dialysis
- Refractory metabolic acidosis pH <7.1 = consider bicarbonate and dialysis

# Chapter 11: Psychiatric Emergencies

## 11.1 Suicide Risk Assessment and Safety Planning

### 11.1.1 Epidemiology and Risk Factors

Suicide is a leading cause of death globally, with approximately 800,000 deaths annually. Risk assessment in the emergency setting is critical for determining safe disposition and intervention intensity.

| Risk Factor Category | Specific Factors |
|---------------------|-----------------|
| **Psychiatric** | Depression (especially with hopelessness, anhedonia, insomnia), bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, substance use disorders, borderline personality disorder, anxiety disorders |
| **Historical** | **Prior suicide attempt (strongest predictor)**, family history of suicide, childhood abuse/trauma |
| **Situational** | Recent loss (relationship, job, status), acute stressors, access to lethal means, social isolation |
| **Demographic** | Male gender (higher completion rate), age >65 or 15-24 years, LGBTQ+ youth, veterans, Indigenous populations |
| **Medical** | Chronic pain, terminal illness, recent diagnosis, traumatic brain injury |
| **Protective factors** | Strong social support, religious/spiritual beliefs, children in the home, therapeutic alliance, future-oriented goals |

### 11.1.2 Assessment Tools

| Tool | Purpose | Application |
|------|---------|-------------|
| **Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS)** | Screening and severity assessment | Standard in EDs; assesses ideation, intent, plan, and behavior |
| **SAFE-T framework** | Structured assessment | Suicide thoughts, plans, actions, means access, protective factors |
| **PAPERS mnemonic** | Memory aid | Plan, Access, Protective factors, Earlier attempts, Resources/Support, Substance use |

### 11.1.3 Key Assessment Elements

1. **Ideation:** Frequency, intensity, duration of suicidal thoughts
2. **Plan:** Specificity, lethality, feasibility of proposed method
3. **Intent:** Stated desire to die; preparations made (giving away possessions, writing notes)
4. **Means:** Access to firearms, medications, sharp objects, ropes -- **means restriction counseling** is one of the most effective interventions
5. **Protective factors:** Reasons for living, social connections, religious beliefs, future plans
6. **Substance use:** Acute intoxication dramatically increases risk

### 11.1.4 Safety Planning

A collaborative, patient-centered safety plan (Stanley-Brown Safety Plan) should include:

1. **Recognize warning signs** (personal triggers and prodromal symptoms)
2. **Employ internal coping strategies** (what the patient can do independently)
3. **Socialize with supportive people** (distract, seek company)
4. **Contact family members or friends** (specific names and phone numbers)
5. **Contact professionals and agencies** (crisis hotline: 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, therapist, emergency department)
6. **Reduce access to lethal means** (lock/remove firearms, secure medications, remove sharp objects)
7. **Identify reasons for living** (personal values, relationships, goals)

### 11.1.5 Disposition

| Risk Level | Characteristics | Disposition |
|------------|----------------|-------------|
| **High** | Active suicidal intent with specific plan and access to means, recent attempt, command hallucinations, severe depression with hopelessness | Voluntary or involuntary psychiatric admission; 1:1 observation |
| **Moderate** | Suicidal ideation without clear plan, some protective factors, stable environment | Crisis intervention, partial hospitalization, intensive outpatient with close follow-up within 24-48 hours |
| **Low** | Passive ideation, strong protective factors, good social support, no plan | Outpatient referral, safety plan, means restriction, follow-up within 1 week |

**Important:** "No-harm contracts" or "contracts for safety" are **NOT considered adequate protection** and should not influence disposition decisions. Means restriction counseling is the most effective immediate intervention.

## 11.2 Agitated and Delirious Patient

### 11.2.1 De-Escalation: First-Line Approach

Verbal de-escalation should ALWAYS be attempted before chemical or physical restraint:

| Technique | Implementation |
|-----------|---------------|
| **Environment** | Quiet, calm setting; reduce stimuli (lights, noise, crowd) |
| **Personal space** | Maintain 2 arm lengths distance; non-threatening posture, open hands |
| **Communication** | Simple, clear statements; active listening; empathetic response; offer choices |
| **Collaboration** | Identify needs and concerns; involve family if helpful |
| **Safety** | Ensure safety of patient, staff, and others; never approach alone |

**What NOT to do:** Crowd the patient, touch without warning, argue, make threats, or use punitive language.

### 11.2.2 Chemical Sedation (If De-escalation Fails)

| Agent | Dose | Onset | Notes |
|-------|------|-------|-------|
| **Midazolam** | 2-5 mg IM/IV | 3-5 min (IV) | Good for alcohol/benzodiazepine withdrawal; respiratory depression risk |
| **Haloperidol** | 5-10 mg IM/IV | 15-30 min | High EPS risk; add lorazepam or benztropine; QT prolongation |
| **Olanzapine** | 5-10 mg IM | 15-30 min | Less EPS; avoid combining with benzodiazepines (respiratory depression) |
| **Ketamine** | 4-5 mg/kg IM | 3-5 min | For severe agitation unresponsive to other agents; dissociative sedation |
| **Lorazepam** | 1-2 mg IM/IV | 15-30 min (IM) | Good for alcohol/BZD withdrawal; less respiratory depression than midazolam |

**Safety monitoring:** Continuous pulse oximetry, cardiac monitoring, frequent vital signs. Have airway equipment and flumazenil available if benzodiazepines used.

### 11.2.3 Physical Restraint

- **Last resort** only -- when imminent danger to self or others exists
- **Never as punishment** or for staff convenience
- **Least restrictive** method effective
- **Continuous monitoring:** Reassess every 15 minutes; offer food, fluids, toileting
- **Time-limited:** Orders expire and require reassessment per institutional policy
- **Document:** Rationale, type, duration, complications, patient response

### 11.2.4 Search for Underlying Cause

Agitation is a symptom, not a diagnosis. Always evaluate for:
- **Medical causes:** Hypoxia, hypoglycemia, infection (UTI, pneumonia), stroke, head injury, thyroid dysfunction
- **Metabolic:** Electrolyte abnormalities, hepatic encephalopathy, uremia
- **Toxicological:** Drug intoxication or withdrawal, medication side effects
- **Pain:** Uncontrolled pain is a common cause of agitation
- **Psychiatric:** Psychosis, mania, anxiety disorders, PTSD

## 11.3 Serotonin Syndrome vs. Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome

These two potentially fatal syndromes are frequently confused but have distinct etiologies, clinical features, and management approaches [^629^][^630^][^631^][^632^][^635^].

### 11.3.1 Comparison

| Feature | Serotonin Syndrome | Neuroleptic Malignant Syndrome (NMS) |
|---------|-------------------|-------------------------------------|
| **Cause** | Serotonergic agents (SSRIs, SNRIs, MAOIs, TCAs, fentanyl, tramadol, MDMA, lithium, linezolid, methylene blue) | Dopamine antagonists (antipsychotics -- typical > atypical; metoclopramide; abrupt withdrawal of dopaminergic agents in Parkinson's disease) |
| **Onset** | Hours (<24 hours after change in serotonergic medication) | Days to weeks (gradual onset; usually 1-3 days after exposure) |
| **Mental status** | Agitation, delirium, confusion; can progress to coma | Stupor, mutism, fluctuating consciousness; severe rigidity limits exam |
| **Muscle findings** | Hyperreflexia, clonus (spontaneous, inducible, or ocular), tremor, myoclonus | "Lead-pipe" severe rigidity (more prominent than in serotonin syndrome) |
| **Deep tendon reflexes** | Hyperreflexia | Hyporeflexia or normal |
| **Pupils** | Mydriasis (dilated) | Normal |
| **Bowel sounds** | Hyperactive (diarrhea common) | Decreased or absent (ileus) |
| **Skin** | Diaphoresis, flushing | Pallor, diaphoresis |
| **Temperature** | Usually <40 C, but can exceed 40 C; less extreme than NMS | >40 C common; marked hyperthermia |
| **Creatine kinase (CK)** | Mildly elevated (<1000) or normal | Markedly elevated (often >10,000-100,000) |
| **WBC** | Normal to mildly elevated | Elevated |
| **Iron studies** | Normal | Decreased serum iron |

### 11.3.2 Diagnostic Criteria

**Hunter Serotonin Toxicity Criteria:**
Patient taking a serotonergic agent PLUS ONE of:
- Spontaneous clonus, OR
- Inducible clonus + agitation or diaphoresis, OR
- Ocular clonus + agitation or diaphoresis, OR
- Tremor + hyperreflexia, OR
- Hypertonia + temperature >38 C + ocular or inducible clonus

**NMS Diagnostic Criteria (DSM-5 / International Consensus):**
- Exposure to dopamine antagonist within 72 hours
- Hyperthermia (>38 C on two occasions)
- Muscle rigidity
- Altered mental status
- Plus 5 of: tachycardia, BP elevation or lability, diaphoresis/sialorrhea, tremor, incontinence, CK >4x ULN, leukocytosis, metabolic acidosis

### 11.3.3 Management

| | Serotonin Syndrome | NMS |
|---|---|---|
| **First-line** | Discontinue ALL serotonergic agents | Discontinue dopamine antagonist; supportive care |
| **Supportive** | External cooling, IV fluids, benzodiazepines (agitation, tremor) | Aggressive cooling, IV fluids, dantrolene |
| **Specific antidote** | Cyproheptadine 12 mg PO load, then 2 mg q2h (max 32 mg/day); serotonin antagonist | Bromocriptine 2.5 mg PO/NG TID-QID (dopamine agonist); dantrolene 1-2.5 mg/kg IV |
| **Benzodiazepines** | First-line for agitation, tremor, hyperreflexia | For rigidity, autonomic symptoms |
| **Dantrolene** | Not indicated | 1-2.5 mg/kg IV; especially if temperature >40 C |
| **Expected resolution** | 24-72 hours after stopping offending agent | Days to 2 weeks; may recur with rechallenge |

[^629^][^630^][^635^]

## 11.4 Alcohol Withdrawal

### 11.4.1 Assessment: CIWA-Ar

The Clinical Institute Withdrawal Assessment for Alcohol (CIWA-Ar) is a 10-item, validated scale used to assess withdrawal severity and guide benzodiazepine dosing:

| Score Range | Severity | Management |
|-------------|----------|------------|
| 0-8 | Minimal | Monitoring only; may not need medication |
| 9-14 | Mild | Symptom-triggered medication; monitor q2-4h |
| 15-20 | Moderate | Symptom-triggered medication; monitor q1-2h |
| >20 | Severe | Aggressive medication; monitor q1h; ICU if >25 |

**Scale components:** Nausea/vomiting, tremor, paroxysmal sweats, anxiety, agitation, tactile disturbance, auditory disturbance, visual disturbance, headache, orientation/clouding of sensorium. Maximum score: 67.

### 11.4.2 Benzodiazepine Protocol

**Symptom-triggered dosing** is preferred over fixed-schedule (shorter total duration, less oversedation) [^634^]:

| CIWA-Ar Score | Action |
|--------------|--------|
| >=20 | Diazepam 10-20 mg (or lorazepam 2-4 mg) q1h PRN; ICU monitoring |
| 15-19 | Diazepam 5-10 mg (or lorazepam 2 mg) q1h PRN; recheck in 1 hour |
| 10-14 | Diazepam 5 mg (or lorazepam 1-2 mg) q2h PRN; recheck in 2 hours |
| <10 | Recheck q4h; medicate if rising or patient symptomatic |

**Continue monitoring and treatment for minimum 48 hours after the last benzodiazepine dose.**

### 11.4.3 Adjunctive Therapy

| Agent | Indication | Dose |
|-------|-----------|------|
| Thiamine | Wernicke prevention | 100 mg IV **before** glucose-containing fluids |
| Multivitamin ("banana bag") | Nutritional deficiency | 1 liter with thiamine, folate, multivitamin |
| Haloperidol | Hallucinations only | 2-5 mg IM/IV q4-6h PRN |
| Phenobarbital | Refractory withdrawal | 130-260 mg IV; load to achieve therapeutic level |

**Critical:** Haloperidol does NOT treat withdrawal seizures or autonomic instability -- benzodiazepines are required.

### 11.4.4 Delirium Tremens (DTs)

DTs represent the most severe form of alcohol withdrawal:

| Feature | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| Onset | 48-96 hours after last drink |
| Characteristics | Delirium, visual/auditory hallucinations, severe autonomic hyperactivity (tachycardia, hypertension, fever), agitation, tremor |
| Mortality | 1-5% with treatment; up to 20% without treatment |
| Management | ICU monitoring; high-dose benzodiazepines (diazepam 20-40 mg IV q5-10 min until sedation achieved); propofol or phenobarbital infusion if benzodiazepines inadequate |
| Monitoring | Continuous cardiac monitoring; frequent vitals; 1:1 observation |

## 11.5 Opioid Withdrawal

### 11.5.1 Assessment: COWS

The Clinical Opiate Withdrawal Scale (COWS) is an 11-item instrument:

| Score | Severity |
|-------|----------|
| 5-12 | Mild |
| 13-24 | Moderate |
| 25-36 | Moderately severe |
| >36 | Severe |

**Components:** Resting pulse rate, sweating, restlessness, pupil size, bone/joint aches, runny nose, GI upset, tremor, yawning, anxiety, gooseflesh.

### 11.5.2 Buprenorphine Microinduction

Traditional buprenorphine induction requires patients to be in moderate withdrawal (COWS >=12, 12-24 hours after last short-acting opioid) to avoid precipitated withdrawal. **Microinduction** allows gradual overlapping transition from full agonist to buprenorphine without a withdrawal period [^654^]:

| Step | Dosing | Duration |
|------|--------|----------|
| Step 1 | 225 mcg buccal (Belbuca) q6h | 3 doses |
| Step 2 | 450 mcg buccal q3h | 6 doses |
| Step 3 | 2 mg sublingual q6h | 4 doses |
| Step 4 | 4 mg sublingual q6h | 4 doses (total 16 mg) |
| Step 5 | Wean full agonist; consolidate to daily dosing | Ongoing |

**Key principles:**
- Continue full agonist (oxycodone, etc.) PRN throughout the microinduction
- Few patients need >24 mg/day buprenorphine
- Advantages: No required withdrawal period; suitable for patients on methadone or fentanyl; can be done inpatient
- **Macrodosing alternative:** Starting dose >=16 mg; may reduce precipitated withdrawal by rapidly saturating receptors

### 11.5.3 Alternative Treatments for Opioid Withdrawal

| Agent | Mechanism | Dosing | Notes |
|-------|-----------|--------|-------|
| **Methadone** | Full mu-agonist | 10-30 mg daily; titrate | Only in controlled settings (OTP); QT prolongation risk |
| **Clonidine** | Alpha-2 agonist | 0.1-0.2 mg q2-4h PRN | Reduces autonomic symptoms; watch for hypotension, sedation |
| **Lofexidine** | Alpha-2 agonist | Per package insert | FDA-approved for opioid withdrawal; similar to clonidine |
| **Loperamide** | Opioid agonist (peripheral) | 4-8 mg initially; max 16 mg/day | For diarrhea |
| **Ondansetron** | 5-HT3 antagonist | 4-8 mg q8h | For nausea/vomiting |
| **NSAIDs** | Anti-inflammatory | Per standard dosing | For bone/muscle aches |
| **Hydroxyzine/trazodone** | Sedating antihistamine | PRN | For insomnia |

## 11.6 Red Flags

> **CRITICAL:**
- Active suicidal intent with specific plan and access to means = immediate psychiatric evaluation and likely involuntary admission
- Psychosis with command hallucinations directing violence = safety risk; requires urgent evaluation
- Severe serotonin syndrome (temperature >40 C, severe hypertonia with clonus) = discontinue serotonergic agents; ICU care
- NMS with CK >10,000 + rhabdomyolysis + acute kidney injury = ICU; dantrolene + bromocriptine
- Delirium tremens (autonomic instability + altered mental status) = ICU; high-dose benzodiazepines
- Severe alcohol withdrawal seizure (status epilepticus risk) = airway protection; benzodiazepines
- Excited delirium with hyperthermia (often stimulant-induced) = risk of sudden death; rapid cooling and sedation

| 13-14 | Mild impairment | Minor head injury; monitor |
| 9-12 | Moderate impairment | Moderate TBI; likely abnormal CT |
| 3-8 | Severe impairment | Severe TBI; cannot protect airway; intubate |
| 3 | Deepest coma | Worst prognosis |

## C.3 Pediatric GCS Modifications

| Component | Modification for Children |
|-----------|--------------------------|
| Verbal | >5 years: standard scale; 2-5 years: smiles, vocalizes, cries, irritable, no response; <2 years: coos/babbles, cries, inappropriate cry, grunts, no response |
| Motor | Same as adult (obeys commands, localizes, withdraws, decorticate, decerebrate, none) |
| Eye | Same as adult |

## C.4 GCS Components in Clinical Practice

Always document **each component separately** (e.g., "GCS 11 = E3 V3 M5") rather than a single number. This provides more clinical information:
- A patient with E1 V1 M6 has a motor GCS of 6 (localizes or withdraws), which is very different prognostically from E2 V2 M1
- The motor component has the strongest predictive value for neurological outcome

# Appendix D: Electrolyte Emergency Protocols

## D.1 Hyperkalemia Management Protocol

| Priority | Intervention | Dose | Timing | Effect |
|----------|-------------|------|--------|--------|
| **IMMEDIATE** | Calcium gluconate 10% | 30 mL IV over 10 min | 1-3 min | Myocardial stabilization (does NOT lower K+) |
| **Priority 2** | Regular insulin + D50W | 10 units + 50 mL IV | 15-30 min | Intracellular shift (reduces K+ by 0.5-1.5) |
| **Priority 2** | Albuterol nebulizer | 10-20 mg | 30 min | Intracellular shift (additive with insulin) |
| **Priority 3** | Sodium zirconium cyclosilicate | 10 g PO/NG TID | 1-2 hours | K+ removal |
| **Priority 3** | Furosemide | 40-80 mg IV | 15-30 min | K+ removal (if not volume-depleted) |
| **Definitive** | Hemodialysis | Per dialysis protocol | Immediate during session | K+ removal in severe/refractory cases |

## D.2 Hyponatremia Emergency Protocol

| Scenario | Intervention | Dose | Rate Limit | Notes |
|----------|-------------|------|-----------|-------|
| **Symptomatic (seizures, coma)** | 3% hypertonic saline | 100 mL IV bolus over 10 min; may repeat x2 | Raise Na 4-6 mEq/L acutely to stop seizures | Then limit correction |
| **Chronic (>48h), asymptomatic** | Fluid restriction +/- salt/urea/tolvaptan | Per underlying cause | **<8-12 mEq/L in 24h**; **<18 mEq/L in 48h** | ODS prevention is paramount |
| **High-risk for ODS** | Fluid restriction + slow correction | Same | **<8 mEq/L in 24h** | Risk factors: alcohol, liver disease, malnutrition, K+ <115 |
| **Over-correction occurring** | Desmopressin + D5W | Desmopressin 2-4 mcg IV; D5W at 3 mL/kg/hr | Re-lowers or stabilizes Na | Prevent ODS when correction exceeds limits |

## D.3 Hypocalcemia Emergency Protocol

| Severity | Symptoms | Treatment |
|----------|----------|-----------|
| **Severe** (Ca <7 mg/dL or ionized <0.8) | Tetany, seizures, laryngospasm, QT prolongation, hypotension | Calcium gluconate 1-2 g IV over 10-20 min; then infusion 0.5-1.5 mg/kg/hr; monitor ECG |
| **Moderate** (Ca 7-8 mg/dL) | Paresthesias, Chvostek sign, Trousseau sign | Calcium gluconate 1 g IV; oral calcium supplementation |
| **Mild** (Ca 8-8.5 mg/dL) | Usually asymptomatic | Oral calcium 1-2 g elemental calcium daily |

**Always check and correct:** Magnesium (hypomagnesemia causes refractory hypocalcemia), vitamin D, and PTH if recurrent.

## D.4 Hypercalcemia Emergency Protocol

| Level | Management |
|-------|-----------|
| **Severe** (Ca >14 mg/dL) | IV normal saline 200-300 mL/hr + zoledronic acid 4 mg IV over 15 min (or pamidronate 60-90 mg); calcitonin 4 IU/kg SC/IM q12h (rapid onset, short duration); hemodialysis if refractory |
| **Moderate** (Ca 12-14 mg/dL) | IV normal saline 150-250 mL/hr + bisphosphonate; treat underlying cause |
| **Mild** (Ca 10.5-12 mg/dL) | Oral hydration; treat underlying cause; avoid thiazides, calcium supplements |

## D.5 Hypomagnesemia Emergency Protocol

| Indication | Treatment |
|-----------|-----------|
| **Torsades de pointes** | Magnesium sulfate 1-2 g IV over 15 minutes; may repeat |
| **Seizures** | Magnesium sulfate 1-2 g IV over 15 minutes |
| **Symptomatic** (tremor, tetany, arrhythmias) | Magnesium sulfate 1-2 g IV over 30-60 minutes |
| **Maintenance** | Magnesium oxide 400-800 mg PO daily or magnesium sulfate 1-2 g IM/IV daily |

**Note:** Hypomagnesemia causes refractory hypokalemia and hypocalcemia. Always check and correct magnesium when these are present.

## D.6 Severe Metabolic Acidosis Protocol

| Indication | Treatment |
|-----------|-----------|
| **pH <7.1 with hemodynamic compromise** | Sodium bicarbonate 1-2 mEq/kg IV bolus; then infusion titrated to pH 7.2 |
| **Bicarbonate <5 mEq/L** | Same as above |
| **Specific toxins** (salicylates, TCAs, toxic alcohols) | Sodium bicarbonate per specific protocol; urinary alkalinization for salicylates |
| **DKA** | Insulin + fluids; bicarbonate ONLY if pH <6.9 |
| **Lactic acidosis** | Treat underlying cause; bicarbonate controversial; no proven survival benefit |

# Appendix E: Sepsis-3 and qSOFA Quick Reference

## E.1 qSOFA (Quick SOFA) -- Bedside Screening

| Criterion | Threshold | Points |
|-----------|-----------|--------|
| Respiratory rate | >=22/min | 1 |
| Altered mentation | GCS <15 | 1 |
| Systolic blood pressure | <=100 mmHg | 1 |

- **Score >=2:** High risk of poor outcome; prompt evaluation for sepsis
- **Score <2:** Does NOT exclude sepsis; use clinical judgment

## E.2 SOFA Score (Full) -- Organ Dysfunction Assessment

| System | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|--------|---|---|---|---|---|
| Respiratory (PaO2/FiO2) | >=400 | <400 | <300 | <200 | <100 |
| Coagulation (Platelets x10^3/uL) | >=150 | <150 | <100 | <50 | <20 |
| Liver (Bilirubin mg/dL) | <1.2 | 1.2-1.9 | 2.0-5.9 | 6.0-11.9 | >=12.0 |
| Cardiovascular | MAP >=70 | MAP <70 | Dopamine <=5 or dobutamine | Dopamine 5.1-15 or Epi <=0.1 or NE <=0.1 | Dopamine >15 or Epi >0.1 or NE >0.1 |
| CNS (GCS) | 15 | 13-14 | 10-12 | 6-9 | <6 |
| Renal (Creatinine mg/dL) | <1.2 | 1.2-1.9 | 2.0-3.4 | 3.5-4.9 | >=5.0 |

- **Delta SOFA >=2** from baseline = organ dysfunction consistent with sepsis
- Baseline SOFA score of 0 in patients not known to have pre-existing organ dysfunction

## E.3 Septic Shock Definition

All three criteria must be met:
1. Sepsis (infection + delta SOFA >=2)
2. Persistent hypotension requiring vasopressors to maintain MAP >=65 mmHg
3. Serum lactate >2 mmol/L despite adequate volume resuscitation

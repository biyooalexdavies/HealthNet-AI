## 1.1 Abnormal Uterine Bleeding (AUB)

### Overview

Abnormal uterine bleeding refers to bleeding from the uterus occurring outside pregnancy in reproductive-aged women that is abnormal in regularity, frequency, volume, or duration. AUB affects up to one-third of women during their reproductive years, with irregularities most common at menarche and perimenopause [^387^]. It is a leading cause of gynecologic morbidity and accounts for significant healthcare utilization.

**Pathophysiology:** AUB can arise from structural abnormalities of the uterus, disorders of hemostasis, ovulatory dysfunction, endometrial disorders, iatrogenic causes, or conditions not otherwise classified. The PALM-COEIN classification system provides a standardized framework for categorizing etiologies.

### PALM-COEIN Classification (FIGO, ACOG-Endorsed)

The PALM-COEIN system categorizes AUB into structural and non-structural etiologies [^387^][^393^]:

**Structural Causes (PALM):**

| Letter | Condition | Key Features |
|--------|-----------|--------------|
| **P** | Polyps | Endometrial or cervical; often cause intermenstrual bleeding; diagnosed on ultrasound or hysteroscopy |
| **A** | Adenomyosis | Endometrial tissue within myometrium; diffuse uterine enlargement, tender "boggy" uterus on exam |
| **L** | Leiomyoma (Fibroids) | Subclassified by location (submucosal, intramural, subserosal); most common structural cause of AUB |
| **M** | Malignancy/Hyperplasia | Endometrial cancer risk increases with age >45, obesity, PCOS, chronic unopposed estrogen exposure |

**Non-Structural Causes (COEIN):**

| Letter | Condition | Key Features |
|--------|-----------|--------------|
| **C** | Coagulopathy | von Willebrand disease most common; heavy bleeding since menarche strongly suggests bleeding disorder [^395^] |
| **O** | Ovulatory Dysfunction | Most common cause of AUB overall; includes PCOS, thyroid disease, hyperprolactinemia, perimenopause |
| **E** | Endometrial | Primary endometrial disorder; diagnosis of exclusion after ruling out other causes |
| **I** | Iatrogenic | Hormonal contraceptives, anticoagulants, IUDs, SSRIs, antipsychotics |
| **N** | Not Otherwise Classified | Endometritis, arteriovenous malformations (AVM), myometrial hypertrophy |

### Clinical Presentation

- Menorrhagia: Heavy or prolonged menstrual bleeding (soaking through a pad/tampon every 1-2 hours, passing clots >1 inch, bleeding >7 days)
- Metrorrhagia: Intermenstrual bleeding or spotting between regular cycles
- Menometrorrhagia: Heavy and irregular bleeding
- Oligomenorrhea: Infrequent periods (>35-day cycles)
- Polymenorrhea: Frequent periods (<21-day cycles)
- Postcoital bleeding: Bleeding after intercourse (evaluate for cervical pathology)
- Postmenopausal bleeding: Any bleeding >12 months after last menstrual period (always requires evaluation)

### Red Flags / Emergency Features

| Red Flag | Significance | Immediate Action |
|----------|------------|-----------------|
| Hemodynamic instability (tachycardia, hypotension, syncope) | Significant acute blood loss | IV access, fluid resuscitation, emergent gynecology consult |
| Acute heavy bleeding with hemoglobin <8 g/dL | Severe anemia requiring intervention | CBC, type & screen, consider transfusion; acute hemostatic therapy |
| Postmenopausal bleeding | Endometrial cancer until proven otherwise | Prompt endometrial biopsy and imaging |
| Fever with heavy bleeding | Possible infection/sepsis | Broad-spectrum antibiotics; hospitalization |
| Bleeding in known/suspected pregnancy | Ectopic pregnancy, miscarriage, molar pregnancy | Urine/serum hCG; urgent ultrasound |

### Diagnostic Workup

**Initial Evaluation (All Patients):**

1. **Detailed menstrual history:** Cycle length, duration, volume (pad/tampon count, clot size), regularity, onset, progression, associated symptoms
2. **Pregnancy test:** Mandatory for all reproductive-age women with AUB
3. **CBC with ferritin:** Assess for anemia and iron deficiency
4. **TSH:** Screen for thyroid dysfunction
5. **Prolactin:** If irregular cycles, galactorrhea, or headaches suggestive

**Selective Testing:**

| Indication | Test |
|------------|------|
| Heavy bleeding since menarche (especially adolescence) | Coagulation screen: PT/INR, aPTT, vWF antigen, ristocetin cofactor, Factor VIII [^395^] |
| Signs of hyperandrogenism | Total testosterone, free testosterone, DHEAS, 17-OHP |
| Signs of perimenopause | FSH, estradiol (if diagnostic uncertainty) |
| Structural cause suspected | Transvaginal ultrasound |

**Coagulopathy Screening in Adolescents:** A structured history instrument is approximately 90% sensitive for detecting underlying coagulopathy [^395^]. A positive screen (heavy bleeding since menarche, bleeding with dental work/surgery, family history) should prompt hematologic testing including von Willebrand panel.

**Imaging Workup:**

| Modality | Indication | Notes |
|----------|-----------|-------|
| **Transvaginal ultrasound** | First-line for all patients with suspected structural AUB | Non-invasive; identifies fibroids, polyps, adenomyosis, ovarian pathology |
| **Saline-infusion sonohysterography (SIS)** | Evaluate endometrial cavity; better than TVUS alone for polyps/submucosal fibroids | Requires cervical cannulation; performed in outpatient setting |
| **Hysteroscopy with directed biopsy** | Gold standard for evaluating focal endometrial lesions | Diagnostic and therapeutic; direct visualization |
| **MRI** | Characterize fibroid location/size for surgical planning; evaluate adenomyosis | Most accurate for myometrial pathology; expensive |

**Endometrial Sampling Indications (Endometrial Biopsy):**

- Age >=45 with new-onset AUB
- Age <45 with risk factors for endometrial cancer (obesity, PCOS, chronic anovulation, unopposed estrogen therapy)
- Failed medical management
- Persistent intermenstrual bleeding
- Any age with abnormal ultrasound findings (thickened/irregular endometrium)
- Postmenopausal bleeding (mandatory)

### Management

#### Acute Heavy Bleeding (ACOG Committee Opinion #557) [^393^]

For hemodynamically unstable patients, the goal is rapid hemostasis and hemodynamic stabilization:

| Treatment | Dosing | Notes |
|-----------|--------|-------|
| **IV Conjugated Equine Estrogen** | 25 mg IV every 4-6 hours | Stabilizes endometrium; most rapid option for acute hemorrhage |
| **Combined Oral Contraceptives** | Multidose regimens (e.g., 1 pill TID x 7 days, then taper) | Effective for hemodynamically stable patients |
| **Oral Progestins** | Medroxyprogesterone 20 mg TID or norethindrone 5 mg TID | Stabilizes endometrium; widely available |
| **Tranexamic Acid** | 1.3 g PO TID or 10 mg/kg IV | Antifibrinolytic; reduces bleeding 40-60%; contraindicated with active VTE |

**After stabilization:** Transition to maintenance therapy (COCs, cyclic progestins, or LNG-IUD). Add iron supplementation (ferrous sulfate 325 mg with vitamin C, or IV iron if severely depleted).

#### Chronic AUB Management by Etiology

| Etiology | First-Line Medical Options | Surgical Options |
|----------|---------------------------|-----------------|
| **Polyps** | None (surgical treatment indicated) | Hysteroscopic polypectomy (preferred) |
| **Adenomyosis** | LNG-IUD 52 mg, GnRH agonists with add-back, COCs, tranexamic acid, NSAIDs | Hysterectomy (definitive); adenomyomectomy (fertility-sparing, rare) |
| **Leiomyomas** | LNG-IUD 52 mg, GnRH agonists, GnRH antagonists + add-back, tranexamic acid, COCs | Myomectomy, UAE, radiofrequency ablation, endometrial ablation, hysterectomy |
| **Malignancy/Hyperplasia** | Progestin therapy for atypical hyperplasia (if fertility desired) | Hysterectomy +/- BSO (definitive); lymphadenectomy if cancer |
| **Coagulopathy** | Tranexamic acid, DDAVP (for vWD), hematology referral | - |
| **Ovulatory Dysfunction** | COCs, cyclic progestins, metformin (if PCOS), treat underlying endocrine disorder | Endometrial ablation (if no fertility desires), hysterectomy |
| **Endometrial** | LNG-IUD (highest efficacy), COCs, cyclic progestins | Hysterectomy if refractory |

**Key Medical Therapies:**

- **LNG-IUD (52 mg):** First-line for heavy menstrual bleeding regardless of etiology; reduces bleeding 80-90% by 12 months [^388^]. Effective for up to 8 years (Mirena/Liletta).
- **Tranexamic acid:** 1.3 g PO three times daily during menses (days 1-5); reduces bleeding 40-60%; non-hormonal; well-tolerated [^388^].
- **GnRH agonists (leuprolide):** Bridge to surgery; 3.75 mg IM monthly for 3-6 months; must use with add-back therapy (estrogen + progestin) to prevent bone loss.
- **GnRH antagonists with add-back (elagolix, relugolix, linzagolix):** FDA-approved for AUB due to fibroids; relugolix 40 mg daily with estradiol 1.0 mg + norethindrone 0.5 mg; up to 24 months [^394^].
- **NSAIDs:** Start at menses onset; reduce bleeding 20-40% via prostaglandin inhibition.

**Surgical Options Overview:**

- **Endometrial ablation:** Destroys endometrial lining; 85-90% satisfaction rate; NOT for fertility preservation; increased ectopic pregnancy risk if pregnancy occurs.
- **Uterine artery embolization (UAE):** Blocks blood supply to fibroids; effective for bulk symptoms; fertility data limited [^394^].
- **Myomectomy:** Fertility-preserving; laparoscopic/hysteroscopic approach preferred; recurrence 15-30%.
- **Hysterectomy:** Definitive treatment; vaginal approach preferred when feasible; laparoscopic/vaginal preferred over abdominal [^394^].

## 1.2 Dysmenorrhea

### Overview

Dysmenorrhea is the most common gynecologic condition affecting women of reproductive age, with prevalence estimates of 45-95% worldwide. It is a leading cause of school and work absenteeism [^441^][^450^]. The condition is broadly categorized into primary (no underlying pelvic pathology) and secondary (associated with identifiable pelvic pathology).

**Primary Dysmenorrhea:** Painful menstruation in the absence of pelvic pathology. Typically begins 6-12 months after menarche (when ovulatory cycles establish). Pathophysiology is driven by overproduction of prostaglandin F2a from the endometrium, causing myometrial hypercontractility and vasoconstriction, leading to ischemic pain [^445^].

**Secondary Dysmenorrhea:** Painful menstruation due to underlying pelvic pathology. The most common cause is endometriosis; other causes include adenomyosis, pelvic inflammatory disease, uterine fibroids, Mullerian anomalies, and ovarian cysts [^445^].

### Clinical Presentation

**Primary Dysmenorrhea Characteristics:**
- Onset: 6-12 months after menarche (with establishment of ovulatory cycles)
- Timing: Begins with onset of menses or 1-2 days before; peaks within 24 hours; lasts 48-72 hours
- Character: Cramping, colicky, suprapubic pain; may radiate to lower back and thighs
- Associated symptoms: Nausea (up to 90%), vomiting (up to 60%), diarrhea, headache, fatigue, dizziness
- Physical examination: Normal pelvic exam

**Distinguishing Primary vs. Secondary Dysmenorrhea:**

| Feature | Primary | Secondary |
|---------|---------|-----------|
| Onset | Early after menarche (6-12 months) | Later onset; may develop years after menarche |
| Character | Cramping, colicky; coincides with menses | May begin days before menses; progressive worsening over time |
| Associated symptoms | Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, headache | Dyspareunia (especially deep), abnormal bleeding, infertility, non-menstrual pelvic pain |
| Physical Exam | Normal | May reveal masses, tenderness, nodularity, fixed uterus |
| Response to NSAIDs/OCPs | Usually excellent | Often incomplete |

### When to Suspect Secondary Dysmenorrhea

- Pain begins after years of previously painless periods
- Progressive worsening of pain over time
- Presence of dyspareunia (painful intercourse)
- AUB (heavy or intermenstrual bleeding)
- Infertility or subfertility
- Abnormal pelvic exam or imaging findings
- Pain persisting beyond menses or occurring at other times in the cycle
- Pain not responsive to first-line therapy after 3-6 months

### Management

#### First-Line: NSAIDs [^441^][^450^]

NSAIDs are the cornerstone of primary dysmenorrhea treatment. All NSAIDs are superior to placebo, and no significant differences exist between agents for pain relief.

| Agent | Dosing | Notes |
|-------|--------|-------|
| **Ibuprofen** | 400-800 mg every 6-8 hours (max 2400 mg/day) | Most widely used; OTC availability |
| **Naproxen** | 500 mg loading, then 250-500 mg every 12 hours | Longer half-life allows BID dosing |
| **Naproxen sodium** | 550 mg loading, then 275 mg every 6-8 hours | Faster onset than naproxen |
| **Mefenamic acid** | 500 mg loading, then 250 mg every 6 hours | Unique prostaglandin synthesis inhibitor |

**Optimal Use:** Start 1-2 days before anticipated menses onset and continue through the first 2-3 days of bleeding. This preemptive approach blocks prostaglandin production before pain pathways are fully activated.

**Contraindications:** Renal insufficiency, peptic ulcer disease/GI bleeding, bleeding disorders, aspirin-sensitive asthma, severe hepatic disease.

#### First-Line: Hormonal Contraception [^441^][^444^]

Hormonal contraception is equally effective as NSAIDs and treats both pain and menstrual irregularity.

| Method | Mechanism | Effectiveness for Dysmenorrhea |
|--------|-----------|-------------------------------|
| **Combined oral contraceptives (COCs)** | Suppress ovulation, thin endometrium, reduce prostaglandin production | Highly effective; continuous/extended regimens preferred |
| **LNG-IUD 52 mg** | Local progestin; endometrial atrophy | Excellent; 20% amenorrhea rate by 1 year |
| **Etonogestrel implant** | Continuous progestin; suppress ovulation | Good; irregular bleeding common |
| **DMPA injection** | Suppresses ovulation and endometrial growth | Excellent; 70% amenorrhea by 1 year |
| **Progestin-only pills** | Variable ovulation suppression; progestin effect | Moderate; useful if estrogen contraindicated |

#### Non-Pharmacologic Approaches [^441^][^450^]

| Modality | Evidence Level | Notes |
|----------|---------------|-------|
| **Heat therapy** (continuous low-level topical heat) | Strong evidence | As effective as ibuprofen; 8-12 hours of heating pad at 40 C |
| **Exercise** | Substantial supporting data | Regular aerobic exercise reduces symptom severity |
| **TENS** (transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation) | Moderate evidence | Non-invasive; particularly useful if NSAIDs contraindicated |
| **Acupuncture** | Some evidence | Use as adjunct; mixed trial results |
| **Yoga** | Emerging evidence | May reduce pain and improve quality of life |

#### Second-Line Options

- **Acetaminophen:** Less effective than NSAIDs; useful for mild symptoms, contraindications to NSAIDs, or as adjunct therapy
- **GnRH analogs (elagolix 150-200 mg daily, relugolix 40 mg daily with add-back):** For endometriosis-related dysmenorrhea refractory to hormonal contraception
- **Aromatase inhibitors (letrozole, anastrozole):** For refractory endometriosis-associated pain; use with add-back progestin or OCP

#### Surgical (Reserved for Refractory Secondary Dysmenorrhea)

- **Endometrial ablation:** For women not desiring fertility; 85-90% satisfaction
- **Laparoscopic excision/ablation of endometriosis:** For confirmed endometriosis
- **Presacral neurectomy:** Reserved for severe, refractory central pelvic pain
- **Laparoscopic uterosacral nerve ablation (LUNA):** Limited evidence; largely replaced by excisional surgery
- **Hysterectomy:** Definitive; last resort after all other options exhausted

## 1.3 Endometriosis

### Overview

Endometriosis is characterized by the presence of endometrial-like tissue outside the uterine cavity, causing a chronic inflammatory reaction. It affects approximately 10% (190 million) of reproductive-age women globally and is a leading cause of chronic pelvic pain and infertility. The disease severity correlates poorly with symptom intensity.

**Pathophysiology:** Retrograde menstruation (Sampson theory) is the most widely accepted mechanism, though implantation, coelomic metaplasia, lymphatic/hematogenous spread, and stem cell theories also contribute. The ectopic endometrial tissue responds to hormonal stimulation, causing inflammation, fibrosis, and adhesions.

**Common Presentations:**
- Progressive dysmenorrhea (most common symptom)
- Chronic pelvic pain (non-cyclical)
- Deep dyspareunia (pain with deep penetration)
- Infertility/subfertility
- Cyclical bowel symptoms (pain with defecation, rectal bleeding during menses)
- Cyclical bladder symptoms (dysuria, hematuria during menses)
- Ovarian endometriomas ("chocolate cysts")

### Diagnosis

**Clinical Diagnosis:** In most cases, empiric treatment can and should be initiated based on history and examination without requiring laparoscopic confirmation. Clinical diagnosis is preferred per ESHRE 2022 guidelines to avoid diagnostic delay [^390^].

**Physical Examination Findings:**
- Fixed, retroverted uterus
- Uterosacral nodularity (pathognomonic on rectovaginal exam)
- Tender adnexal masses (endometriomas)
- Cervical motion tenderness
- Painful pelvic examination

**Imaging:**

| Modality | Utility |
|----------|---------|
| **Transvaginal ultrasound** | Detects ovarian endometriomas (>80% sensitivity); may detect deep infiltrating lesions involving bowel/bladder |
| **MRI** | Best for deep infiltrating endometriosis, especially rectovaginal lesions; useful for surgical planning |

**Definitive Diagnosis:** Laparoscopy with histologic confirmation remains the gold standard, but is no longer required to initiate treatment. Visual inspection alone has limited accuracy; histologic confirmation increases specificity.

**Biomarkers:** No reliable serum biomarker for routine clinical use. CA-125 may be elevated but lacks specificity. Research into miRNA, cytokine panels, and other biomarkers is ongoing.

### Medical Management (ESHRE 2022 Guidelines) [^390^]

**First-Line: Hormonal Contraceptives and Progestogens** (Strong Recommendation)

| Class | Agents | Notes |
|-------|--------|-------|
| **Combined hormonal contraceptives** | COCs (oral, patch, ring) | Strong recommendation; continuous/extended use preferred for dysmenorrhea; reduces number of menstrual cycles |
| **Progestogens** | Dienogest 2 mg daily, norethindrone acetate, medroxyprogesterone acetate | Strong recommendation; dienogest specifically approved for endometriosis in many countries |
| **LNG-IUS 52 mg** (Mirena) | Intrauterine levonorgestrel 52 mg | Strong recommendation; excellent long-term option; may reduce pain and disease progression |
| **Etonogestrel implant** (Nexplanon) | 68 mg subdermal etonogestrel | Strong recommendation; provides continuous progestin exposure |

**Second-Line: GnRH Agonists**

- **Agents:** Leuprolide 3.75 mg IM monthly, goserelin 3.6 mg SC monthly, nafarelin 200 mcg intranasal BID
- **Efficacy:** Highly effective for pain reduction; ~90% response rate
- **Critical Requirement:** Must use with add-back therapy (estrogen + progestin) to prevent bone loss (BMD decreases 4-6% at 6 months without add-back) and hypoestrogenic symptoms
- **Add-back regimens:** 0.625 mg conjugated equine estrogen + 2.5 mg medroxyprogesterone daily; or transdermal estradiol 25 mcg + oral progestin
- **Duration:** Recommended as second-line due to side effect profile; typically limited to 6 months (extendable with add-back)

**GnRH Antagonists (Newer Agents):**

| Agent | Dosing | Duration | Notes |
|-------|--------|----------|-------|
| **Elagolix** (Orilissa) | 150 mg daily (moderate-severe pain) or 200 mg BID (severe pain) | Up to 24 months (150 mg) or 6 months (200 mg) | Dose-dependent bone loss; lowest effective dose |
| **Relugolix** (Myfembree) | 40 mg daily + estradiol 1.0 mg + norethindrone 0.5 mg | Up to 24 months | Contains add-back; better tolerability |

**Other Options:**

- **Aromatase inhibitors** (letrozole 2.5 mg, anastrozole 1 mg): For refractory cases; use with OCP or progestin to prevent bone loss and cyst formation [^390^]
- **NSAIDs:** For pain adjunct; no disease-modifying effect

### Surgical Management

**Indications:**
- Diagnostic confirmation when empiric therapy has failed
- Failed medical management after 3-6 months
- Endometriomas >4 cm in diameter
- Bowel, bladder, or ureteral involvement
- Fertility treatment optimization

**Approaches:**

| Procedure | Indication | Notes |
|-----------|-----------|-------|
| **Laparoscopic excision** | Preferred approach | Superior pain outcomes compared to ablation; removes disease and allows histology |
| **Cystectomy for endometriomas** | Ovarian endometriomas >4 cm | Reduces recurrence vs. drainage alone; may reduce ovarian reserve (discuss with patient desiring fertility) |
| **Hysterectomy with BSO** | Definitive for women not desiring fertility | Highest satisfaction for refractory pain; hormone therapy needed post-oopherectomy |
| **Conservative excision** | Fertility preservation | Preserves uterus and ovaries; recurrence possible |
| **Deep excision surgery** | Rectovaginal or bowel disease | Requires specialized surgical team; may involve bowel/bladder resection |

### Fertility Considerations

- Endometriosis is associated with infertility through multiple mechanisms: distorted pelvic anatomy, impaired oocyte quality, altered endometrial receptivity, chronic inflammation
- Early-stage disease (Stage I-II): Surgical excision may improve spontaneous pregnancy rates
- Advanced disease (Stage III-IV): IVF often required
- Endometrioma excision before IVF: Controversial; may reduce ovarian reserve but may improve IVF outcomes in some cases; individualize decision
- Post-surgery: Consider 3-6 months of GnRH agonist therapy before IVF for Stage III-IV disease (improves pregnancy rates)

## 1.4 Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS)

### Overview

PCOS is the most common endocrine disorder in reproductive-age women, with prevalence estimates of 6-15% depending on diagnostic criteria used. It is a heterogeneous metabolic and reproductive syndrome with lifelong implications extending far beyond ovarian dysfunction, including metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and endometrial cancer risk [^391^].

**Pathophysiology:** Insulin resistance is a key contributing factor in most cases, augmenting LH-stimulated ovarian theca cell androgen production and inhibiting hepatic SHBG synthesis, leading to elevated free androgen levels [^391^]. Hyperandrogenism disrupts normal follicular development, preventing dominant follicle selection and resulting in anovulation and multiple small antral follicles.

### Diagnosis: Modified Rotterdam Criteria [^389^][^391^][^392^]

PCOS is diagnosed when **2 of 3 criteria are met**, with exclusion of other disorders:

| Criterion | Recommended Diagnostic Standard |
|-----------|--------------------------------|
| **1. Hyperandrogenism** (Clinical and/or Biochemical) | Clinical: Modified Ferriman-Gallwey score >=4-8 (ethnicity-dependent); acne, androgenetic alopecia. Biochemical: Elevated total or free testosterone, FAI, or bioavailable testosterone |
| **2. Oligo-anovulation** | Cycles >35 days apart or <8 menses/year; amenorrhea >90 days (if >3 years post-menarche) |
| **3. Polycystic Ovarian Morphology (PCOM)** | >=20 follicles per ovary (2-9 mm) OR ovarian volume >=10 mL (transvaginal, >=8 MHz probe) |

**Exclusion of Other Disorders (Mandatory):**

| Disorder | Test | When to Suspect |
|----------|------|----------------|
| Thyroid dysfunction | TSH | Fatigue, weight changes, goiter |
| Hyperprolactinemia | Prolactin | Galactorrhea, headaches, visual changes |
| Non-classical CAH | 17-OHP (morning) | Early-onset symptoms, high DHEAS, Ashkenazi Jewish/Mediterranean/Hispanic ancestry |
| Cushing syndrome | 24-hour urinary free cortisol or dexamethasone suppression test | Central obesity, striae, proximal muscle weakness |
| Androgen-secreting tumor | DHEAS, androstenedione, imaging | Markedly elevated androgens (>200 ng/dL testosterone or >700 mcg/dL DHEAS), rapid onset virilization |
| Primary ovarian insufficiency | FSH, estradiol | Age <40 with amenorrhea, hot flashes |

**PCOS Phenotypes (by criteria combination):**

| Phenotype | Criteria Met | Notes |
|-----------|-------------|-------|
| A (Classic/Full) | Hyperandrogenism + oligo-anovulation + PCOM | Most common; most severe metabolic features |
| B | Hyperandrogenism + oligo-anovulation | Ovulatory PCOM absent; similar metabolic risk to A |
| C | Hyperandrogenism + PCOM | "Ovulatory PCOS"; menstrual cycles may be regular |
| D | Oligo-anovulation + PCOM | "Mild" or "non-hyperandrogenic"; mildest phenotype |

**Special Populations:**

- **Adolescents:** Diagnosis requires BOTH hyperandrogenism AND oligo-anovulation; PCOM should NOT be used as a diagnostic criterion due to high prevalence of multifollicular ovaries in adolescents [^391^]. Irregular cycles are normal up to 3 years post-menarche.
- **Perimenopause:** PCOM criteria unreliable (follicle count decreases with age); diagnosis based on historical features and long-term metabolic consequences.

### Evaluation

**Initial Labs (Early Follicular Phase, Days 2-5):**
- LH, FSH (LH:FSH ratio may be elevated >2:1 but not required for diagnosis)
- TSH, prolactin, 17-OHP
- Total testosterone; calculate free testosterone or FAI (SHBG often low)
- Consider DHEAS, androstenedione if initial testing normal
- Fasting glucose, HbA1c, fasting insulin
- Lipid panel
- AMH: Optional; elevated in PCOS but not for routine diagnosis; may substitute for ultrasound in adults

**Metabolic Screening:**
- BMI calculation
- Oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT): Recommended over fasting glucose alone; detects impaired glucose tolerance missed by fasting glucose in up to 30% of women with PCOS
- Blood pressure
- Lipid panel

### Management (Goals-Based Treatment)

| Goal | Treatment Options |
|------|-------------------|
| **Menstrual regulation** | Combined hormonal contraceptives, cyclic progestins (medroxyprogesterone 10 mg x 10-14 days/month or norethindrone 5 mg), metformin |
| **Hirsutism/acne** | COCs (antiandrogenic progestins: drospirenone, cyproterone, dienogest), spironolactone 50-200 mg/day, topical eflornithine, mechanical hair removal |
| **Metabolic management** | Lifestyle modification (weight loss 5-10%), metformin 500-2000 mg/day |
| **Fertility** | Weight loss, letrozole (first-line ovulation induction), clomiphene citrate, metformin, gonadotropins, IVF |
| **Endometrial protection** | Ensure regular progestin exposure (COCs, cyclic progestins, LNG-IUD) to prevent endometrial hyperplasia/cancer |

**Key Medications:**

- **Metformin:** Improves insulin sensitivity, aids weight loss, restores ovulation in ~50%, reduces progression to T2DM. Start 500 mg daily with evening meal, titrate by 500 mg weekly to 1500-2000 mg/day. GI side effects common initially.
- **Letrozole:** First-line for ovulation induction (superior to clomiphene per PCOS Trial); 2.5-7.5 mg days 3-7 of cycle.
- **Spironolactone:** Antiandrogen for hirsutism; 50-200 mg/day; MUST use with reliable contraception (feminization of male fetus risk); monitor potassium.
- **Combined hormonal contraceptives:** First-line for menstrual regulation and hirsutism/acne; choose antiandrogenic progestin.

### Long-Term Health Considerations

| Risk | Screening Recommendation |
|------|-------------------------|
| Type 2 diabetes | OGTT at baseline, then every 1-3 years |
| Cardiovascular disease | Address modifiable risk factors; no consensus on enhanced screening |
| Endometrial cancer | Ensure regular withdrawal bleeds or progestin exposure; biopsy if AUB |
| Mood disorders | Screen for depression and anxiety annually |
| Obstructive sleep apnea | Screen if symptoms (especially with obesity) |
| NAFLD | Consider if metabolic syndrome present |

## 1.5 Uterine Fibroids (Leiomyomas)

### Overview

Uterine fibroids are the most common pelvic tumors in women, affecting up to 70% of white women and >80% of Black women by age 50. They are benign monoclonal smooth muscle tumors that cause symptoms based on size, number, and location [^394^].

**FIGO Classification System (by location):**

| Type | Description | Fertility Impact |
|------|-------------|-----------------|
| 0 | Pedunculated intracavitary | Highest impact on fertility and bleeding |
| 1 | Submucosal (<50% intramural) | Significant impact |
| 2 | Submucosal (>=50% intramural) | Significant impact |
| 3 | Intramural, touching endometrium | Moderate impact |
| 4 | Intramural | Variable |
| 5 | Intramural, touching serosa | Less impact |
| 6 | Subserosal | Bulk symptoms |
| 7 | Subserosal pedunculated | Torsion risk |
| 8 | Other (cervical, parasitic) | Variable |

**Common Symptoms:** Heavy menstrual bleeding (most common), bulk symptoms (pelvic pressure, urinary frequency, constipation, abdominal distension), dysmenorrhea, infertility, recurrent pregnancy loss (especially submucosal types).

### Management (ACOG Practice Bulletin, 2021) [^394^]

**Expectant Management:** Appropriate for asymptomatic patients or those with mild symptoms who decline treatment.

#### Medical Management

| Therapy | Dosing | Duration | Key Notes |
|---------|--------|----------|-----------|
| **LNG-IUD 52 mg** | Intrauterine placement | Up to 8 years | First-line for AUB due to fibroids; reduces bleeding 70-90%; contraindicated if cavity distorted |
| **Tranexamic acid** | 1.3 g PO TID during menses | Long-term | Non-hormonal; 40-60% bleeding reduction |
| **GnRH agonists** (leuprolide) | 3.75 mg IM monthly or 11.25 mg q3mo | Up to 6 months (bridge to surgery) | With add-back for extended use; 35-65% volume reduction |
| **GnRH antagonists + add-back** (relugolix) | 40 mg daily + estradiol 1.0 mg + norethindrone 0.5 mg | Up to 24 months | FDA-approved for AUB-L [^394^] |
| **Elagolix + add-back** | Per product labeling | Per labeling | GnRH antagonist with add-back |
| **COCs** | Daily | Long-term | Moderate bleeding reduction; may help dysmenorrhea |
| **NSAIDs** | During menses | Long-term | Modest benefit; 20-40% reduction |
| **Selective progesterone receptor modulators** (ulipristal - limited availability) | 5 mg daily in fibroid treatment cycles | Intermittent | Not widely available due to hepatotoxicity concerns |

#### Procedural/Surgical Options [^394^][^412^]

| Procedure | Best Candidates | Key Considerations |
|-----------|---------------|-------------------|
| **Hysteroscopic myomectomy** | Submucosal fibroids (Type 0-2) | Fertility-preserving; outpatient; best for intracavitary lesions |
| **Laparoscopic/robotic myomectomy** | Intramural/subserosal; fertility desired | Morcellation only in containment bag; recurrence 15-30% |
| **Uterine artery embolization (UAE)** | Women preserving uterus; no fertility desires | 1-2 day recovery; 20-30% failure rate; fertility data limited [^394^] |
| **Radiofrequency ablation** | Symptomatic fibroids; uterus preservation | Laparoscopic, transcervical, or transvaginal approach; limited fertility data [^394^] |
| **Magnetic resonance-guided focused ultrasound (MRgFUS)** | Selected patients | Non-invasive; limited availability; variable results |
| **Endometrial ablation** | AUB-L only; completed childbearing | Minimally invasive; NOT for bulk symptoms; contraindicated with submucosal fibroids |
| **Hysterectomy** | Definitive; failed other treatments; no fertility desires | Vaginal approach preferred; most commonly performed definitive procedure |

### Special Considerations

- **Fertility:** Submucosal fibroids (Types 0-2) clearly reduce fertility; removal recommended prior to conception. Intramural fibroids >4-5 cm may affect fertility and pregnancy outcomes.
- **Pregnancy:** Fibroids may grow during pregnancy (estrogen-stimulated); most managed expectantly. Red (carneous) degeneration can cause acute pain requiring hospitalization.
- **Black women:** Higher prevalence, earlier onset, more severe symptoms, larger fibroids; consider earlier and more aggressive intervention.

## 1.6 Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (PID)

### Overview

PID is an ascending polymicrobial infection of the upper female genital tract involving the endometrium, fallopian tubes, ovaries, and peritoneum. It is most commonly caused by sexually transmitted pathogens (*Neisseria gonorrhoeae*, *Chlamydia trachomatis*) ascending from the lower genital tract, combined with anaerobic and facultative bacteria from the vaginal flora.

**Risk Factors:** Age <25, multiple sexual partners, new sexual partner, prior PID, IUD insertion (highest risk in first 3 weeks), douching, prior STI.

**Long-Term Complications:** Tubal factor infertility (10-15% after one episode, 30% after two episodes, up to 50% after three), ectopic pregnancy, chronic pelvic pain, tubo-ovarian abscess, Fitz-Hugh-Curtis syndrome (perihepatitis).

### Diagnosis

**Minimum Criteria for Empiric Treatment (CDC STI Guidelines, 2021) [^291^]:**

In sexually active young women and other women at risk for PID who have pelvic or lower abdominal pain, empiric treatment should be initiated if **ONE** of the following minimum criteria is present:
- Cervical motion tenderness
- Uterine tenderness
- Adnexal tenderness

**Additional Supportive Criteria:**
- Fever >38.3 C (101 F)
- Abnormal cervical or vaginal mucopurulent discharge
- Elevated ESR or CRP
- Documented cervical infection with *N. gonorrhoeae* or *C. trachomatis*
- WBCs on wet mount of vaginal secretions

**No single historical, physical, or laboratory finding is both sensitive and specific for PID diagnosis.** The positive predictive value of a clinical diagnosis ranges from 65-90% depending on the population.

### Treatment (CDC STI Guidelines, 2021) [^291^][^399^]

#### Outpatient Regimen (Mild-Moderate Disease)

| Component | Dosing |
|-----------|--------|
| **Ceftriaxone** | 1 g IM single dose |
| **PLUS Doxycycline** | 100 mg PO BID x 14 days |
| **PLUS Metronidazole** | 500 mg PO BID x 14 days |

The addition of metronidazole provides anaerobic coverage for organisms associated with BV, which commonly coexists with PID.

**Alternative Outpatient Regimens:**
- Cefoxitin 2 g IM + probenecid 1 g PO single dose, PLUS doxycycline 100 mg BID x 14 days +/- metronidazole 500 mg BID x 14 days
- If cephalosporin allergy: Consider azithromycin-based or levofloxacin-based regimens (consult current CDC guidelines)

#### Inpatient Regimen (Severe Disease) [^291^][^399^]

| Regimen | Components |
|---------|------------|
| **A** | Cefotetan 2 g IV q12h OR cefoxitin 2 g IV q6h PLUS doxycycline 100 mg PO/IV q12h |
| **B** | Clindamycin 900 mg IV q8h PLUS gentamicin IV/IM (2 mg/kg load, then 1.5 mg/kg q8h) |
| **C** | Ampicillin/sulbactam 3 g IV q6h PLUS doxycycline 100 mg PO/IV q12h |

Transition to oral doxycycline 100 mg BID + metronidazole 500 mg BID to complete 14 days after clinical improvement (usually 24-48 hours).

**Indications for Hospitalization [^291^]:**
- Surgical emergencies (appendicitis, ectopic pregnancy) cannot be excluded
- Tubo-ovarian abscess
- Pregnancy
- Severe illness, nausea/vomiting, temperature >38.5 C (101 F)
- Unable to follow or tolerate outpatient oral regimen
- Failed response to outpatient antimicrobial therapy

### Special Considerations

- **Partner management:** Sexual partners within the preceding 60 days should be evaluated and treated empirically for gonorrhea and chlamydia, regardless of symptoms.
- **Tubo-ovarian abscess:** Hospital admission; IV antibiotics (Regimen B preferred); percutaneous or surgical drainage if no response to antibiotics within 48-72 hours.
- **Fertility counseling:** All women with PID should be counseled about the risk of future infertility and ectopic pregnancy.
- **IUD management:** Can be left in place if patient is clinically improving; remove if no improvement within 48-72 hours.
- **Follow-up:** Re-examine within 72 hours for clinical improvement; if no improvement, consider hospitalization, abscess, or alternative diagnosis.

## 1.7 Vulvovaginal Candidiasis and Bacterial Vaginosis

### 1.7.1 Bacterial Vaginosis (BV)

#### Overview

BV is the most common vaginal condition in women of reproductive age, resulting from the replacement of normal vaginal lactobacilli with anaerobic bacteria (*Gardnerella vaginalis*, *Prevotella*, *Mobiluncus*). It is associated with increased risk of STI acquisition, PID, post-procedural infections, and adverse pregnancy outcomes.

**Diagnostic Criteria:**

**Amsel Criteria** (diagnostic if 3 of 4 present): [^398^][^401^]
1. Thin, homogeneous gray-white discharge
2. Vaginal pH >4.5
3. Clue cells on wet mount (>20% of epithelial cells)
4. Positive whiff test (amine/fishy odor after adding 10% KOH)

**Nugent Score** (Gram stain): 0-3 normal, 4-6 intermediate, >=7 BV

**Spiegel Criteria:** Modified Amsel; pH >4.7 required

#### Treatment (CDC STI Guidelines, 2021) [^401^]

| Regimen | Dosing | Notes |
|---------|--------|-------|
| **Metronidazole** (oral) | 500 mg PO BID x 7 days | Recommended regimen |
| **Metronidazole gel 0.75%** | 5 g intravaginally once daily x 5 days | Avoid alcohol (theoretical disulfiram-like reaction, though evidence mixed) |
| **Clindamycin cream 2%** | 5 g intravaginally at bedtime x 7 days | Oil-based; may weaken latex condoms for 5 days |
| **Clindamycin** (oral) | 300 mg PO BID x 7 days | Alternative |
| **Clindamycin ovules** | 100 mg intravaginally at bedtime x 3 days | Shorter course |
| **Secnidazole** | 2 g oral granules single dose | Single-dose convenience; higher cost |
| **Tinidazole** | 2 g PO daily x 2 days OR 1 g PO daily x 5 days | Alternative nitroimidazole |

**Pregnancy:** Treat symptomatic BV in pregnancy with oral metronidazole or clindamycin. Treatment reduces risk of postpartum endometritis but evidence for preventing preterm birth is mixed.

**Recurrent BV (>=3 episodes/year) [^401^]:**
- Suppressive therapy: Metronidazole gel 0.75% twice weekly for 4-6 months
- Alternative: Oral nitroimidazole followed by intravaginal boric acid 600 mg daily x 21 days, then suppressive metronidazole gel
- Lactobacillus crispatus CTV-05 (Lactin-V) vaginal probiotic after metronidazole: Shows promise in clinical trials [^401^]
- Partner treatment: Evidence limited; may consider in recurrent cases

### 1.7.2 Vulvovaginal Candidiasis (VVC)

#### Overview

VVC is the second most common vaginal infection after BV. Approximately 75% of women will experience at least one episode; 40-50% will have >=2 episodes. Candida albicans accounts for 85-90% of cases; non-albicans species (*C. glabrata*, *C. tropicalis*, *C. parapsilosis*) account for the remainder and are often more resistant.

**Clinical Features:** Vulvar pruritus (hallmark symptom), burning, soreness; thick white "cottage cheese" discharge; vulvar erythema/edema; excoriations; vaginal pH typically normal (<4.5) - this is a key distinguishing feature from BV (pH >4.5).

#### Uncomplicated VVC Treatment [^402^]

| Route | Agents | Duration |
|-------|--------|----------|
| **Topical azoles** (OTC) | Butoconazole, clotrimazole, miconazole, tioconazole | 1-7 days (varies by product) |
| **Oral fluconazole** | 150 mg | Single dose |

#### Complicated VVC

Defined by: severe symptoms (extensive erythema/edema/excoriations), non-albicans Candida, immunocompromised host (HIV, diabetes, corticosteroid use), recurrent (>=3 episodes/year), or pregnancy.

| Scenario | Treatment |
|----------|-----------|
| **Severe symptoms** | Fluconazole 150 mg every 72 hours x 2-3 doses OR topical azole 7-14 days |
| **Non-albicans Candida** | Fluconazole 800 mg x 3 doses OR boric acid 600 mg vaginal capsule daily x 14 days OR nystatin 100,000 units vaginal tablet daily x 14 days |
| **Recurrent VVC** | Induction: Fluconazole 100-200 mg every 3rd day x 3 doses (days 1, 4, 7); Maintenance: Fluconazole 100-150-200 mg weekly x 6 months [^402^] |
| **Pregnancy** | Topical azole 7-day regimen ONLY; avoid oral fluconazole |

**Recurrent VVC evaluation:** Obtain culture to confirm C. albicans vs. non-albicans species; evaluate for underlying diabetes, immunosuppression; consider partner treatment if uncircumcised male partner with balanitis.

## 1.8 Ovarian Cysts

### Overview

Ovarian cysts are common fluid-filled sacs within or on the ovary. Most are functional (physiologic) and resolve spontaneously. Pathologic cysts may require intervention. Transvaginal ultrasound is the primary diagnostic tool; O-RADS (Ovarian-Adnexal Reporting and Data System) provides standardized risk stratification.

### Functional (Physiologic) Cysts

| Type | Origin | Typical Size | Natural History |
|------|--------|-------------|----------------|
| **Follicular cyst** | Failed follicular rupture/ovulation | Usually <3 cm | Resolves in 1-2 cycles |
| **Corpus luteum cyst** | Post-ovulatory corpus luteum persistence | 2-6 cm | Resolves in 4-8 weeks; may cause acute pain if rupture/hemorrhage |
| **Theca lutein cyst** | hCG stimulation | Variable, often bilateral | Resolves when hCG stimulus removed |
| **Hemorrhagic cyst** | Bleeding into functional cyst | Variable | Usually resolves; follow-up to confirm |

### Pathologic Cysts

| Type | Characteristics | Management |
|------|----------------|------------|
| **Endometrioma** | "Chocolate cyst"; associated with endometriosis | Medical management for endometriosis; surgery if >4 cm, symptomatic, or fertility concerns |
| **Dermoid cyst** (mature cystic teratoma) | Contains ectodermal elements (hair, teeth, sebaceous material) | Surgical removal (risk of torsion; 10-15% bilateral) |
| **Cystadenoma** | Serous (unilocular) or mucinous (multilocular) epithelial tumors | Surgical evaluation; size >10 cm or symptomatic |
| **Functional cyst persisting >2-3 cycles** | >5 cm persisting beyond 2-3 cycles | Evaluate for neoplasm |

### Management

#### Premenopausal Women

| Cyst Type | Size/Management |
|-----------|----------------|
| Simple cyst <5 cm | Expectant; repeat ultrasound in 6-12 weeks to confirm resolution |
| Simple cyst 5-7 cm | Serial ultrasounds; most are physiologic |
| Simple cyst >7 cm | MRI for characterization; surgical evaluation |
| Hemorrhagic cyst | Usually resolves; follow-up if symptomatic or concerning features |
| Endometrioma | Medical management; surgery if >4 cm, symptomatic, or fertility concerns |
| Dermoid cyst | Surgical removal (torsion risk) |

#### Postmenopausal Women

| Cyst Type | Management |
|-----------|-----------|
| Simple cyst <3 cm | Normal; no follow-up needed |
| Simple cyst 3-5 cm | Risk very low; optional follow-up |
| Simple cyst >5 cm | Close follow-up or surgical evaluation |
| Complex features (septations, solid components, papillary projections) | Surgical evaluation warranted |

### Surgical Indications
- Persistent or enlarging cysts
- Symptomatic cysts (pain, mass effect)
- Suspicious features on imaging (solid components, papillary projections, ascites)
- Large cysts (>7-10 cm)
- Elevated tumor markers (CA-125 in appropriate context)

### Ovarian Torsion (EMERGENCY)

**Definition:** Rotation of the ovary on its ligamentous supports, compromising blood supply.

**Key Features:**
- Acute onset severe unilateral pelvic/abdominal pain
- Nausea and vomiting (very common; 70-80%)
- Adnexal mass on examination
- Ultrasound: Enlarged ovary (>4 cm), multiple peripheral follicles, absent or decreased Doppler flow (note: preserved flow does NOT exclude torsion)

**Management:**
- **EMERGENCY:** Requires emergent surgical detorsion with ovarian preservation when possible
- Oophorectomy only if ovary is clearly nonviable (necrotic/black)
- Oophoropexy (fixation) may be considered in recurrent torsion or high-risk patients
- Conservative management with close monitoring may be considered in select cases with viable-appearing ovary on imaging

## 2.1 Preconception Counseling

### Overview

Preconception counseling aims to optimize maternal health before conception to improve pregnancy outcomes. It should be offered to all women of reproductive age during routine healthcare visits. Approximately 50% of pregnancies are unplanned, making preconception health a critical public health priority.

### Key Recommendations

#### Folic Acid Supplementation
- **All women of reproductive age:** 400 mcg (0.4 mg) folic acid daily, beginning at least 1 month before conception and continuing through the first trimester
- **High-risk women** (prior neural tube defect-affected pregnancy, on antiepileptic medications, pregestational diabetes, obesity BMI >30): 4 mg daily starting 1 month before conception through first trimester
- Dietary sources: Fortified grains, leafy green vegetables, legumes, citrus fruits

#### Chronic Disease Optimization

| Condition | Preconception Goals |
|-----------|-------------------|
| **Diabetes** | Target HbA1c <6.5% before conception; optimize medications (switch from ACE inhibitors, ARBs, statins to pregnancy-safe alternatives) |
| **Hypertension** | Optimize control; switch to pregnancy-safe agents (labetalol, nifedipine, methyldopa); avoid ACE inhibitors, ARBs |
| **Thyroid disease** | Target TSH <2.5 mIU/L; levothyroxine dose typically needs 25-30% increase in early pregnancy |
| **Seizure disorders** | Transition to lowest effective regimen with least teratogenic potential; folic acid 4 mg; avoid valproic acid if possible |
| **Obesity** | Weight loss before conception improves fertility and reduces pregnancy complications |
| **Mental health disorders** | Optimize psychiatric medications; balance maternal need with fetal risk |

#### Medication Safety Review [^435^]

| Category X (Contraindicated) | Effect | Action |
|------------------------------|--------|--------|
| Isotretinoin | Craniofacial, cardiac, CNS defects | Discontinue 1 month before conception; iPLEDGE program required |
| Warfarin | Nasal hypoplasia, stippled epiphyses, CNS defects | Switch to LMWH (enoxaparin) |
| ACE inhibitors, ARBs | Renal agenesis, oligohydramnios, IUGR | Switch before conception |
| Statins | Teratogenic (cholesterol essential for fetal development) | Discontinue before conception |
| Methotrexate | Craniofacial, limb defects | Discontinue; wait at least 1 ovulatory cycle |
| Valproic acid | Neural tube defects (1-2% risk), craniofacial defects | Avoid if possible |

#### Immunizations
- **Preconception (ideally):** MMR, varicella (live vaccines; administer >1 month before conception or postpartum)
- **During pregnancy:** Tdap (27-36 weeks), influenza (any trimester)
- **Hepatitis B:** If non-immune and high-risk

#### Genetic Screening
- **Carrier screening:** Cystic fibrosis, spinal muscular atrophy, hemoglobinopathies (all women); expanded panel based on ethnicity (Ashkenazi Jewish panel, etc.)
- **Rubella/varicella immunity testing:** Vaccinate if non-immune
- **Discuss aneuploidy screening options**

## 2.2 Antenatal Care Schedule

### Initial Visit (8-10 Weeks or First Prenatal Visit)

**Comprehensive Assessment:**
- Complete medical, obstetric, family, genetic, and psychosocial history
- Physical examination including pelvic exam
- Confirm gestational age by last menstrual period and dating ultrasound if dates uncertain

**Baseline Laboratory Testing:**
- CBC with differential
- Blood type and Rh factor, antibody screen
- Urinalysis and urine culture (asymptomatic bacteriuria screening)
- Hepatitis B surface antigen
- HIV screening (opt-out recommended)
- Syphilis screening (nontreponemal test with treponemal confirmation)
- Gonorrhea and chlamydia screening (age <25 or risk factors)
- Rubella and varicella immunity testing
- Pap smear if due for cervical cancer screening
- One-time HCV screening
- TSH if clinically indicated
- ABO/Rh and antibody screen

**Counseling:**
- Prenatal vitamin with folic acid
- Nutrition, exercise, weight gain goals
- Substance use (tobacco, alcohol, drugs)
- First trimester aneuploidy screening options
- Genetic carrier screening
- Signs and symptoms requiring urgent evaluation

### Routine Subsequent Visits

| Gestational Age | Frequency | Key Components |
|-----------------|-----------|----------------|
| 10-28 weeks | Every 4 weeks | Weight, BP, urine dip, fundal height, fetal heart rate (after 10-12 weeks) |
| 28-36 weeks | Every 2 weeks | Above + glucose screening (24-28 weeks), Tdap (27-36 weeks), Rhogam if Rh-negative (28 weeks) |
| 36 weeks-delivery | Weekly | Above + GBS screening (36-37 weeks), cervical exam as indicated |

### Ultrasound Schedule
- **Dating ultrasound:** 8-13+6 weeks (if uncertain dates, prior cesarean for placenta location)
- **Anatomy scan:** 18-22 weeks (detailed fetal structural evaluation)
- **Growth ultrasound:** Third trimester for high-risk pregnancies (GDM, hypertension, prior IUGR, etc.)

### High-Risk Pregnancy Indicators
May require additional visits, earlier/more frequent ultrasounds, and specialist consultation:
- Maternal age >=35
- Chronic medical conditions (diabetes, hypertension, thyroid disease, autoimmune disease)
- Prior pregnancy complications (preterm birth, preeclampsia, stillbirth)
- Multiple gestation
- Fetal anomalies or growth concerns
- Infectious diseases (HIV, Hepatitis B)
- Substance use disorders

## 2.3 Common Pregnancy Complaints

### 2.3.1 Nausea and Vomiting of Pregnancy (NVP) / Hyperemesis Gravidarum

**NVP** affects 70-80% of pregnancies; typically begins at 4-6 weeks, peaks at 9 weeks, and resolves by 16-20 weeks.

**Treatment Algorithm:**

| Step | Intervention |
|------|-------------|
| 1 (Lifestyle) | Small frequent meals; avoid triggers (spicy, fatty, strong odors); ginger 250 mg QID; vitamin B6 (pyridoxine) 10-25 mg TID |
| 2 (First-line pharmacologic) | Doxylamine 12.5 mg TID + pyridoxine 10-25 mg TID; or Diclegis/Diclectin (delayed-release doxylamine-pyridoxine) |
| 3 (Second-line) | Promethazine, metoclopramide, prochlorperazine |
| 4 (Third-line) | Ondansetron 4-8 mg every 8 hours PRN (data on first-trimester safety evolving; generally considered safe) |
| 5 (Refractory) | Corticosteroids (methylprednisolone) for refractory cases after 10 weeks (avoid in first trimester if possible) |

**Hyperemesis Gravidarum** (severe NVP): Defined by weight loss >5% prepregnancy weight, ketonuria, electrolyte abnormalities (hypokalemia, hyponatremia), dehydration. Requires hospitalization for:
- IV fluid resuscitation (normal saline or lactated Ringer's)
- Electrolyte correction
- IV antiemetics
- Thiamine 100 mg IV BEFORE dextrose-containing IV fluids (prevents Wernicke encephalopathy)
- Nutritional support if prolonged (enteral or parenteral nutrition)

### 2.3.2 Other Common Pregnancy Complaints

| Complaint | Safe Management |
|-----------|----------------|
| **Constipation** | Increased fiber and water, exercise; docusate, psyllium, polyethylene glycol 3350 |
| **Heartburn/GERD** | Dietary modifications, antacids (calcium carbonate), H2 blockers (famotidine), PPIs (omeprazole - safe in pregnancy) |
| **Back pain** | Exercise, physical therapy, support belts, acetaminophen, heat |
| **Leg cramps** | Magnesium supplementation (250-500 mg nightly), stretching, hydration |
| **Hemorrhoids** | Fiber, fluids, stool softeners, topical hydrocortisone, witch hazel pads |
| **Varicose veins** | Compression stockings, leg elevation, exercise |
| **Insomnia** | Sleep hygiene, relaxation techniques; doxylamine or diphenhydramine if needed |
| **Round ligament pain** | Rest, position changes, support belts, acetaminophen |
| **Carpal tunnel syndrome** | Wrist splints (neutral position), steroid injection if severe |

## 2.4 Pregnancy Complications

### 2.4.1 Gestational Diabetes Mellitus (GDM) [^425^][^429^][^431^]

#### Overview

GDM is glucose intolerance first recognized during pregnancy, typically in the second or third trimester. It complicates approximately 2-10% of pregnancies in the US, with prevalence increasing due to rising obesity rates and older maternal age. GDM increases risks of macrosomia, cesarean delivery, shoulder dystocia, neonatal hypoglycemia, and long-term metabolic disease in both mother and child.

#### Screening

**Universal screening** at 24-28 weeks gestation for all pregnant women not previously diagnosed with diabetes. Earlier screening (first trimester) in high-risk women: obesity (BMI >=30), prior GDM, glycosuria, PCOS, strong family history of T2DM, previous macrosomic infant.

**Two-Step Approach (US Standard):**
- **Step 1:** 50 g 1-hour glucose challenge test (GCT); >=140 mg/dL is abnormal (some centers use 130-135 mg/dL for higher sensitivity)
- **Step 2:** 100 g 3-hour OGTT (Carpenter-Coustan criteria); GDM diagnosed if >=2 abnormal values:
  - Fasting: >=95 mg/dL
  - 1-hour: >=180 mg/dL
  - 2-hour: >=155 mg/dL
  - 3-hour: >=140 mg/dL

**One-Step Approach (IADPSG/WHO):**
- 75 g 2-hour OGTT; >=1 abnormal value:
  - Fasting: >=92 mg/dL
  1-hour: >=180 mg/dL
  2-hour: >=153 mg/dL

#### Management

**Lifestyle Modifications (First-line):**
- Nutrition counseling: Carbohydrate counting, balanced meals, avoiding simple sugars
- Moderate exercise: 30 minutes of moderate-intensity activity most days
- Target weight gain per IOM guidelines

**Glucose Monitoring:**
- Fasting: <95 mg/dL
- 1-hour postprandial: <140 mg/dL
- 2-hour postprandial: <120 mg/dL

**Pharmacologic Therapy (when >=50% of glucose values elevated):**

| Agent | Dosing | Notes |
|-------|--------|-------|
| **Insulin** (first-line pharmacologic) | Starting TDD: 0.7-1.0 units/kg/day; split 50% basal (NPH or glargine/detemir), 50% prandial (lispro or aspart) | Does NOT cross placenta; gold standard [^429^] |
| **Metformin** | 500-2500 mg/day in divided doses | Alternative for patients declining insulin; crosses placenta; 50% may still need insulin [^431^] |
| **Glyburide** | 2.5-20 mg/day | NOT recommended as first-line; crosses placenta; inferior outcomes compared to insulin [^431^] |

**Obstetric Management:**
- Growth ultrasounds every 3-4 weeks in third trimester to monitor fetal growth
- Antenatal testing (NST/BPP) starting 32-34 weeks if on insulin or poorly controlled
- Delivery timing: 39-40 weeks for well-controlled GDM on diet alone; 39 weeks if on medications; earlier for poor glycemic control
- Consider cesarean delivery if estimated fetal weight >=4500 g (to reduce shoulder dystocia risk)

**Postpartum Care:**
- Screen with 75 g 2-hour OGTT at 4-12 weeks postpartum [^425^]
- 15-70% lifetime risk of developing T2DM (highest in women requiring insulin, diagnosed early, or with obesity)
- Screen every 1-3 years thereafter with fasting glucose or HbA1c
- Counsel on lifestyle modifications (weight loss, exercise, diet) to reduce future risk

### 2.4.2 Preeclampsia and HELLP Syndrome [^424^][^426^][^427^]

#### Definitions

| Condition | Definition |
|-----------|-----------|
| **Gestational hypertension** | New-onset BP >=140/90 mmHg after 20 weeks without proteinuria or severe features |
| **Preeclampsia** | Gestational hypertension PLUS proteinuria (>=300 mg/24h or protein/creatinine >=0.3) OR hypertension with thrombocytopenia, renal insufficiency, impaired liver function, pulmonary edema, or cerebral/visual symptoms |
| **Severe features** | SBP >=160 or DBP >=110 (twice, 4 hours apart); platelets <100,000; creatinine >1.1 mg/dL; transaminases 2x normal or RUQ/epigastric pain; pulmonary edema; new-onset cerebral or visual disturbances |
| **HELLP syndrome** | Hemolysis (schistocytes, LDH >600 U/L, indirect bilirubin >1.2 mg/dL), Elevated Liver enzymes (AST/ALT 2x upper limit), Low Platelets (<100,000/mcL) [^428^] |
| **Eclampsia** | New-onset tonic-clonic seizures in a woman with preeclampsia |
| **Chronic hypertension with superimposed preeclampsia** | New proteinuria, worsening hypertension, or development of severe features in patient with chronic hypertension |

**Preeclampsia risk factors:** Nulliparity, prior preeclampsia, multifetal gestation, chronic hypertension, pregestational diabetes, renal disease, autoimmune disease (SLE, APS), obesity, age >=35, family history, Black race.

#### Prevention

**Low-dose aspirin prophylaxis [^427^]:**
- Indicated for: high-risk (history of preeclampsia, multifetal gestation, renal disease, autoimmune disease, T1DM or T2DM, chronic hypertension) OR >=2 moderate-risk factors (nulliparity, obesity, family history, Black race, age >=35, low socioeconomic status, prior low birth weight/small for gestational age, >10-year pregnancy interval)
- Dose: 81 mg daily
- Timing: Initiate between 12-28 weeks (optimally before 16 weeks); continue until delivery
- Reduces preeclampsia risk by approximately 10-20% in high-risk populations

#### Management

| Condition | Gestational Age | Management |
|-----------|----------------|------------|
| Gestational HTN or preeclampsia WITHOUT severe features | <37 weeks | Expectant management with close monitoring; antenatal testing 1-2x/week |
| Gestational HTN or preeclampsia WITHOUT severe features | >=37 weeks | Delivery recommended |
| Preeclampsia WITH severe features | >=34 weeks | Delivery after maternal stabilization |
| Preeclampsia WITH severe features | <34 weeks | Expectant management if stable; antenatal corticosteroids for fetal maturity; deliver if maternal/fetal deterioration |
| Eclampsia | Any | Magnesium sulfate seizure prophylaxis + BP control + delivery after stabilization |

**Magnesium Sulfate Protocol [^427^][^428^]:**
- Loading dose: 4-6 g IV over 20-30 minutes
- Maintenance: 1-2 g/hour IV continuous infusion
- Duration: Continue 24 hours postpartum or after last seizure
- **Monitoring:** Deep tendon reflexes, urine output (must be >25-30 mL/hr), respiratory rate
- **Toxicity levels:** Loss of DTR at 8-12 mg/dL; respiratory paralysis >12 mg/dL; cardiac arrest >24 mg/dL
- **Antidote:** Calcium gluconate 1 g (10 mL of 10%) slow IV push

**Antihypertensive Therapy:**
- Acute severe hypertension (>=160/110): Labetalol 20 mg IV, then 40-80 mg every 10 min (max 300 mg); OR hydralazine 5-10 mg IV; OR oral nifedipine 10 mg
- Chronic: Labetalol, nifedipine, methyldopa
- **Contraindicated:** ACE inhibitors, ARBs, spironolactone (teratogenic)

**HELLP Management [^428^]:**
- Hospital admission to tertiary care facility
- Magnesium sulfate + BP control
- Immediate delivery if >=34 weeks
- If <34 weeks and stable: 48 hours of expectant management for corticosteroid benefit
- Platelet transfusion if <50,000/mcL for surgery/vaginal delivery
- Monitor CBC, liver enzymes, LDH every 12 hours until improving
- Corticosteroids for fetal lung maturity if preterm; may transiently improve platelet count

### 2.4.3 Anemia in Pregnancy

- **Physiologic anemia:** Dilutional (plasma volume expands 40-50% while red cell mass increases only 20-30%); Hgb nadir ~11 g/dL in 2nd trimester
- **Iron deficiency anemia:** Defined as Hgb <11 g/dL (1st/3rd trimester) or <10.5 g/dL (2nd trimester)
- **Treatment:** Ferrous sulfate 325 mg (65 mg elemental iron) daily; increase to BID/TID if severely iron deficient
- **IV iron:** Indicated for malabsorption, oral iron intolerance, or moderate-severe anemia (especially late 3rd trimester); ferric carboxymaltose, iron sucrose
- **Folate deficiency:** Folic acid 1-4 mg daily
- **Sickle cell disease:** Higher transfusion thresholds; multidisciplinary care with maternal-fetal medicine and hematology

### 2.4.4 Urinary Tract Infections in Pregnancy

- **Asymptomatic bacteriuria:** Screen at first prenatal visit; treat if >=10^5 CFU (reduces pyelonephritis risk by 80-90%)
- **Cystitis:** Nitrofurantoin 100 mg BID x 5-7 days, cephalexin 500 mg QID x 5-7 days, fosfomycin 3 g single dose
  - Avoid TMP-SMX in 1st trimester (folate antagonism) and near term (kernicterus risk)
  - Avoid nitrofurantoin at term (hemolytic anemia in G6PD deficiency)
- **Pyelonephritis:** Hospital admission; IV ceftriaxone or ampicillin + gentamicin; 10-14 day total course

### 2.4.5 Group B Streptococcus (GBS)

- **Universal screening:** Vaginal-rectal culture at 36 0/7 - 37 6/7 weeks gestation
- **Intrapartum antibiotic prophylaxis (IAP)** if GBS-positive or risk factors present:
  - Penicillin G: 5 million units IV load, then 2.5-3 million units IV q4h until delivery
  - Alternative (penicillin-allergic, non-anaphylactic): Cefazolin 2 g IV load, then 1 g q8h
  - Alternative (penicillin-allergic, anaphylactic, GBS susceptible): Clindamycin 900 mg IV q8h
  - Alternative (penicillin-allergic, anaphylactic, unknown susceptibility): Vancomycin 20 mg/kg IV q8h
- **IAP also indicated if:** Prior infant with invasive GBS disease, GBS bacteriuria during current pregnancy, unknown GBS status with risk factors (delivery <37 weeks, ROM >=18 hours, intrapartum fever >=38 C)

## 2.5 Medication Safety in Pregnancy

### FDA Pregnancy Categories (Historical System) [^435^]

| Category | Definition | Examples |
|----------|------------|----------|
| **A** | Adequate studies in pregnant women show no risk | Folic acid, levothyroxine, prenatal vitamins |
| **B** | Animal studies show no risk but human studies inadequate; OR animal studies show adverse effect but human studies show no risk | Penicillins, cephalosporins, metformin, loratadine, acetaminophen, ondansetron |

## 2.6 Postpartum Care

### Postpartum Visit Timeline
- **Early postpartum contact:** Within 1-3 weeks (earlier for high-risk: cesarean, preeclampsia, diabetes, depression history, substance use)
- **Comprehensive postpartum visit:** By 12 weeks postpartum (ACOG recommends no later than 12 weeks)
- **Ongoing care:** As needed; transition to well-woman care

### 2.6.1 Postpartum Depression (PPD)

- **Prevalence:** Affects 10-20% of postpartum women; up to 50% begin during pregnancy
- **Screening:** Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) or PHQ-9 at postpartum visit; screen all women
- **Risk factors:** Prior depression/anxiety, PPH, infant complications/NICU admission, lack of social support, traumatic delivery, unplanned pregnancy, financial stress

**Treatment:**
- Psychotherapy (CBT, interpersonal therapy): First-line for mild-moderate PPD
- SSRIs (sertraline, paroxetine preferred for breastfeeding): Moderate-severe PPD
- **Brexanolone (Zulresso):** IV infusion approved for severe PPD; requires 60-hour monitored infusion setting; rapid onset
- **Zuranolone (Zurzuvae):** Oral neuroactive steroid; FDA-approved for PPD; 14-day course

**Red Flags (Emergency):**
- Postpartum psychosis: Medical emergency; delusions, hallucinations, extreme confusion, homicidal or suicidal ideation; incidence 1-2 per 1000 births; requires immediate psychiatric hospitalization

### 2.6.2 Postpartum Thyroiditis

- **Prevalence:** 5-10% of postpartum women
- **Phase 1 (Hyperthyroid):** Occurs 1-4 months postpartum; transient; usually mild; may not require treatment
- **Phase 2 (Hypothyroid):** Occurs 4-8 months postpartum; treat if symptomatic or TSH >10
- **Recovery:** Most recover by 12 months; 20-40% develop permanent hypothyroidism
- **Follow-up:** Screen if symptoms suggestive; check TSH at 6-12 months; annual TSH thereafter if episode occurred

### 2.6.3 Contraception Postpartum

| Method | Timing | Notes |
|--------|--------|-------|
| **Lactational amenorrhea method (LAM)** | Immediately | Effective if ALL criteria met: <6 months postpartum, amenorrheic, exclusively/fully breastfeeding |
| **Progestin-only pills** | Immediately postpartum | Preferred for breastfeeding; no effect on milk supply |
| **DMPA injection** | Immediately postpartum | Preferred for breastfeeding |
| **Etonogestrel implant** | Immediately postpartum | Preferred for breastfeeding |
| **LNG-IUD** | Immediately postpartum (within 10 min of placental delivery), 4+ weeks, or delayed | Immediate placement has higher expulsion rate (~10-20%) but acceptable |
| **Copper IUD** | Same as LNG-IUD | Non-hormonal option |
| **Combined hormonal contraceptives** | Delay to 21 days postpartum if not breastfeeding; 6 weeks if breastfeeding | Delay due to VTE risk; progestin-only preferred while breastfeeding |

### 2.6.4 Breastfeeding

- **Recommendation:** Exclusive breastfeeding for first 6 months, then continued with complementary foods for >=1 year (WHO/AAFP/AAP)
- **Absolute contraindications:** Maternal HIV (in developed countries), infant galactosemia, maternal use of chemotherapy agents, radioactive compounds, untreated active TB, HTLV-1/2 infection, maternal use of illicit drugs
- **Common issues:** Sore nipples (improper latch), mastitis, low milk supply, engorgement, plugged ducts; lactation consultation support

## 3.1 Perimenopause and Diagnosis

### Overview

The menopausal transition (perimenopause) is the period from the onset of menstrual cycle changes to 12 months after the final menstrual period (FMP). The average age of natural menopause in the United States is 51 years. The transition typically lasts 4-8 years and is characterized by hormonal fluctuations and progressive ovarian follicular depletion.

**Stages of Reproductive Aging (STRAW +10):**

| Stage | Name | Characteristics |
|-------|------|----------------|
| -5 | Early reproductive | Regular cycles |
| -4 | Peak reproductive | Regular cycles |
| -3b | Late reproductive (early) | Subtle changes in cycle length |
| -3a | Late reproductive (late) | Notable cycle variability |
| -2 | Early perimenopause | Increased cycle variability (>7 days difference from normal) |
| -1 | Late perimenopause | Amenorrhea >=60 days; increased vasomotor symptoms; FSH elevated |
| +1a | Early postmenopause | First 12 months after FMP |
| +1b | Early postmenopause | Years 1-5 after FMP |
| +2 | Late postmenopause | >5 years after FMP |

### Clinical Presentation

| Symptom Category | Specific Symptoms | Prevalence |
|-----------------|-------------------|------------|
| **Vasomotor** | Hot flashes, night sweats, flushing, palpitations | 60-80% |
| **Genitourinary syndrome** | Vaginal dryness, dyspareunia, urinary urgency/frequency, recurrent UTIs, vaginal burning/itching | 50-70% (increases with time since menopause) |
| **Psychological** | Mood changes, irritability, anxiety, depression, cognitive concerns ("brain fog") | 20-40% |
| **Sleep disturbance** | Difficulty falling asleep, frequent awakenings, night sweats-related disruption | 40-50% |
| **Physical** | Joint aches/stiffness, headache, skin changes (dryness, thinning), breast tenderness | Variable |
| **Menstrual changes** | Irregular cycles, heavier or lighter bleeding, skipped periods | Universal during transition |
| **Sexual** | Decreased libido, arousal difficulties, orgasmic changes | 20-40% |

### Diagnosis

**Clinical diagnosis** is sufficient in women >45 with typical symptoms:
- Perimenopause: Changing menstrual patterns + vasomotor symptoms
- Menopause: 12 consecutive months of amenorrhea (no other cause) in appropriate age group

**FSH Testing:** NOT routinely required for diagnosis in women with typical age-appropriate symptoms. May be helpful in:
- Women <45 with suspected premature ovarian insufficiency (POI)
- Women who have had hysterectomy (no menstrual marker)
- Diagnostic uncertainty
- FSH >25-30 IU/L on two occasions 4-6 weeks apart supports perimenopause/menopause

**Other Testing (to exclude alternative diagnoses):**
- TSH (thyroid dysfunction can mimic menopausal symptoms)
- Prolactin (if amenorrhea with galactorrhea)
- Pregnancy test (if any possibility)
- AMH: May reflect remaining ovarian reserve but is NOT diagnostic of menopause status

### 3.1.1 Premature Ovarian Insufficiency (POI)

- **Definition:** Cessation of ovarian function before age 40
- **Diagnosis:** Amenorrhea/oligomenorrhea + FSH >40 IU/L on two occasions >4 weeks apart in a woman <40
- **Causes:** Genetic (FMR1 premutation, Turner syndrome mosaic), autoimmune, iatrogenic (chemotherapy, radiation), idiopathic (majority)
- **Management:** Hormone therapy (MHT) recommended until average age of natural menopause (age 51) to protect bone and cardiovascular health; fertility counseling; genetic counseling

## 3.2 Menopausal Hormone Therapy (MHT)

### Overview

MHT is the most effective treatment for vasomotor symptoms and genitourinary syndrome of menopause. When initiated in appropriate candidates, it provides significant quality-of-life improvements and has a favorable benefit-risk profile.

### Indications [^440^]

- Moderate to severe vasomotor symptoms (hot flashes, night sweats) - **first-line**
- Genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM) - **first-line** (with vaginal preparations)
- Prevention of bone loss/fractures in select symptomatic women at high fracture risk

**NOT indicated for:** Cardiovascular disease prevention, dementia prevention, or in asymptomatic women

### Timing Hypothesis [^440^]

The timing hypothesis posits that the benefit-risk profile of MHT varies significantly based on when therapy is initiated:

| Timing | Cardiovascular Effect | Recommendation |
|--------|----------------------|----------------|
| **Initiated <10 years from menopause onset OR before age 60** | Neutral or potentially beneficial | Most favorable benefit-risk profile |
| **Initiated >10 years from menopause OR after age 60** | May increase cardiovascular risk | Greater caution; evaluate individual risk carefully |

This is supported by the "window of opportunity" concept: estrogen has beneficial effects on vascular endothelium when started early after menopause, but may be harmful when started in older women with established atherosclerosis.

### Transdermal vs. Oral Estrogen [^440^]

| Feature | Oral Estrogen | Transdermal Estrogen |
|---------|--------------|----------------------|
| **First-pass hepatic metabolism** | Yes | No |
| **Effect on SHBG** | Significant increase | Minimal increase |
| **Effect on lipids** | Increases HDL, triglycerides | Neutral or minimal |
| **VTE risk** | Higher (OR ~3-4) | Lower (OR ~1-2) |
| **Stroke risk** | Slightly increased | Lower |
| **Breast cancer risk** | May be higher with long-term CEE+MPA use | Potentially lower (especially with lower doses) |
| **Blood pressure** | May increase | Neutral |
| **Gallbladder disease** | Increased risk | Less risk |
| **Convenience** | Daily pill | Patch change 1-2x/week |
| **Typical dosing** | CEE 0.3-0.625 mg; estradiol 0.5-2 mg | Estradiol 0.025-0.1 mg/day |

**Transdermal route is preferred for women with:** [^440^]
- Increased VTE risk (obesity, age >60, smokers, personal/family history)
- Cardiovascular risk factors
- Hypertriglyceridemia (triglycerides >300 mg/dL)
- Gallbladder disease
- Migraine without aura (estrogen contraindicated entirely if migraine WITH aura)
- Liver disease
- History of VTE

### Progestogen Component

**Required if uterus is present** to prevent endometrial hyperplasia and cancer.

| Regimen | Description | Suitable For |
|---------|-------------|-------------|
| **Continuous combined** | Estrogen + progestin daily every day | Postmenopausal women (no bleeding desired) |
| **Cyclic/sequential** | Estrogen daily + progestin 12-14 days per month | Perimenopausal women (predictable withdrawal bleeding) |

**Progestogen Options:**

| Agent | Dosing | Advantages |
|-------|--------|-----------|
| **Micronized progesterone** | 100-200 mg nightly (continuous) or 200 mg (cyclic) | Most favorable metabolic profile; may improve sleep; no increase in VTE risk; potentially lower breast cancer risk [^440^] |
| **Medroxyprogesterone acetate** | 2.5-5 mg daily (continuous) or 5-10 mg (cyclic) | Most extensively studied; inexpensive |
| **Norethindrone acetate** | 0.5-1 mg daily | Lower dose options |
| **LNG-IUD 52 mg** | Off-label for endometrial protection | For women who cannot tolerate systemic progestin; provides local protection only |

### Risks and Contraindications

**Absolute Contraindications (Do NOT prescribe MHT):**
- Known or suspected breast cancer
- Known or suspected estrogen-dependent neoplasia (endometrial cancer)
- Undiagnosed abnormal genital bleeding
- Active VTE (DVT or PE) or history of VTE
- Active or recent arterial thromboembolic disease (stroke, MI within past year)
- Severe hepatic disease
- Known hypersensitivity to hormone components
- Pregnancy

**Relative Contraindications (Individualize risk):**
- Migraine with aura (estrogen contraindicated; consider non-hormonal options)
- History of breast cancer (case-by-case with oncology; non-hormonal preferred)
- Risk factors for VTE (use transdermal route if proceeding)
- Cardiovascular risk factors
- Hypertriglyceridemia
- Gallbladder disease
- Endometriosis history

### Duration of Therapy

- Individualize based on symptoms, age, and risk factors
- Annual re-evaluation of benefits vs. risks
- No mandatory discontinuation at a specific age
- If continuing beyond age 60 or >10 years since menopause, use lowest effective dose with transdermal route preferred
- Taper gradually rather than abrupt discontinuation to minimize symptom recurrence

## 3.3 Non-Hormonal Options for Vasomotor Symptoms

### FDA-Approved Non-Hormonal Therapy

**Fezolinetant (Veozah) - NK3 Receptor Antagonist [^417^][^418^][^420^]:**
- **FDA approved:** May 2023
- **Mechanism:** Neurokinin-3 (NK3) receptor antagonist; blocks neurokinin B signaling in hypothalamic KNDy neurons to normalize thermoregulatory balance in the hypothalamus
- **Dosing:** 45 mg PO once daily
- **Efficacy:** Reduces VMS frequency by 50-95%; onset within 1 week; sustained at 12 weeks
- **Side effects:** Headache (~9%), fatigue, nausea, abdominal pain, insomnia, elevated hepatic transaminases
- **Monitoring:** Baseline LFTs; repeat at 3, 6, and 9 months; discontinue if ALT/AST >3x ULN with symptoms or >5x ULN without symptoms
- **Contraindications:** Cirrhosis, severe renal impairment (eGFR <15 mL/min), concurrent use of strong CYP1A2 inhibitors
- **Advantages:** No hormonal effects; safe for breast cancer survivors; no VTE risk

### Other Non-Hormonal Pharmacologic Options

| Agent | Dosing | Efficacy | Key Side Effects |
|-------|--------|----------|-----------------|
| **SSRIs/SNRIs** | Paroxetine 7.5 mg (Brisdelle - FDA-approved for VMS); venlafaxine 75 mg; escitalopram 10-20 mg | 40-60% reduction in VMS frequency and severity | Sexual dysfunction, GI upset, insomnia |
| **Gabapentin** | 300-900 mg at bedtime | 45-60% reduction | Drowsiness, dizziness, weight gain |
| **Clonidine** | 0.1 mg patch changed weekly OR 0.05-0.1 mg PO BID | 20-40% reduction | Dry mouth, hypotension, fatigue, constipation |
| **Oxybutynin** | 2.5-5 mg BID-TID | 50-70% reduction | Anticholinergic effects (dry mouth, constipation, urinary retention) |

### Non-Pharmacologic Approaches

| Modality | Evidence | Notes |
|----------|----------|-------|
| **Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)** | Moderate evidence | Improves perception of symptom severity and coping; does not reduce physiologic frequency |
| **Clinical hypnosis** | Moderate evidence | May reduce VMS frequency and improve sleep |
| **Stellate ganglion block** | Emerging evidence | Injection of local anesthetic; effects may last weeks to months |
| **Weight loss** | Substantial evidence | 5-10% weight loss reduces VMS frequency and severity |
| **Exercise** | Moderate evidence | Regular aerobic exercise associated with fewer VMS |
| **Layered clothing, fans, trigger avoidance** | Standard recommendations | Avoid alcohol, spicy foods, hot beverages, hot environments |
| **Mindfulness-based stress reduction** | Emerging evidence | May improve coping and reduce bother |
| **Supplements** | Limited evidence | Black cohosh, phytoestrogens, omega-3s, vitamin E have inconsistent data |

## 3.4 Genitourinary Syndrome of Menopause (GSM)

### Overview

GSM, formerly known as vulvovaginal atrophy or atrophic vaginitis, refers to the collection of symptoms and signs due to estrogen deficiency affecting the vulva, vagina, urethra, and bladder. GSM affects up to 50-70% of postmenopausal women and increases with time since menopause. Unlike vasomotor symptoms, GSM does not improve with time and often worsens without treatment.

### Symptoms

**Vulvovaginal:** Vaginal dryness, burning, irritation, itching, dyspareunia (entry and/or deep), vaginal bleeding after intercourse, decreased lubrication, vaginal laxity/shortening

**Urinary:** Urinary urgency, frequency, dysuria, recurrent urinary tract infections, urinary incontinence (stress and urge)

**Sexual:** Decreased libido, decreased arousal, orgasm changes

### Treatment

| Treatment | Dosing | Notes |
|-----------|--------|-------|
| **Low-dose vaginal estrogen** (estradiol 10 mcg tablet) | 1 tablet vaginally twice weekly | First-line; minimal systemic absorption; safe for most women including many breast cancer survivors (discuss with oncology) |
| **Vaginal estrogen cream** (estradiol 0.01%) | 0.5 g vaginally twice weekly | More rapid symptom relief than tablets; may have slightly more systemic absorption |
| **Vaginal estrogen ring** (Estring) | Inserted vaginally; changed every 3 months | Continuous low-dose release; convenient; well-tolerated |
| **Vaginal DHEA** (prasterone, Intrarosa) | 6.5 mg vaginal insert nightly | Converts to estrogen and testosterone locally; FDA-approved for dyspareunia |
| **Oral ospemifene** (Osphena) | 60 mg daily | SERM; FDA-approved for moderate-severe dyspareunia; boxed warning for VTE; hot flashes may worsen |
| **Vaginal moisturizers** (Replens, etc.) | Regular use (2-3x/week) | Non-hormonal; hydrates vaginal tissues; use regardless of sexual activity |
| **Vaginal lubricants** | With intercourse | Water-based or silicone-based for intercourse comfort; avoid products with irritants |
| **Pelvic floor physical therapy** | As indicated | For urinary incontinence and pelvic floor dysfunction |
| **Vaginal laser/RF therapy** | Series of treatments | Emerging; limited long-term safety data; not FDA-approved for GSM |

### Key Points on Vaginal Estrogen Safety

- Low-dose vaginal estrogen has minimal systemic absorption and does NOT require progestin opposition for endometrial protection
- Vaginal estrogen is safe for most women, including many breast cancer survivors - consultation with oncology recommended
- Vaginal estrogen does NOT increase risk of VTE, stroke, or MI
- The Endocrine Society and NAMS support the use of vaginal estrogen in most postmenopausal women with GSM

## 3.5 Long-Term Health Considerations After Menopause

### Bone Health

- Bone loss accelerates in the first 3-5 years after menopause due to estrogen deficiency (increased osteoclast activity)
- DXA screening: All women age 65 and older; earlier if risk factors (prior fragility fracture, glucocorticoid use >=3 months, low body weight, smoking, parental hip fracture)
- **Prevention:** Weight-bearing exercise, calcium 1200 mg/day, vitamin D 800-1000 IU/day, smoking cessation, limit alcohol
- **MHT:** Reduces vertebral fractures by 30-40% and hip fractures during use; benefit limited to duration of therapy
- **Pharmacologic therapy for established osteoporosis:** Bisphosphonates (alendronate, risedronate, zoledronic acid), denosumab, raloxifene, teriparatide, romosozumab

### Cardiovascular Risk

- Cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of death in postmenopausal women
- Estrogen decline contributes to adverse lipid changes, vascular dysfunction, and increased central adiposity
- MHT is NOT indicated for cardiovascular disease prevention
- Screen and treat modifiable risk factors: Blood pressure, lipids (LDL target <100 if high risk), diabetes, smoking, obesity, physical inactivity

## 4.2 Combined Hormonal Contraceptives (CHCs)

### Methods

| Method | Components | Schedule |
|--------|-----------|----------|
| **Combined oral contraceptives (COCs)** | Ethinyl estradiol 10-50 mcg + progestin | Daily; 21/7, 24/4, or continuous/extended regimens |
| **Transdermal patch** (Xulane, Twirla) | Norelgestromin 150 mcg + EE 35 mcg | Weekly x 3 weeks; patch-free week |
| **Vaginal ring** (NuvaRing) | Etonogestrel 120 mcg + EE 15 mcg | 3 weeks in, 1 week out; or continuous use |
| **Vaginal ring** (Annovera) | Segesterone 103 mcg + EE 17 mcg | 1-year ring; 3 weeks in, 1 week out cycles |

### Non-Contraceptive Benefits

- Regulation of menstrual cycles
- Reduction of dysmenorrhea and heavy menstrual bleeding
- Treatment of acne and hirsutism (antiandrogenic progestins)
- Reduction in ovarian cancer risk (protection persists years after discontinuation)
- Reduction in endometrial cancer risk
- Reduction in colorectal cancer risk
- Improvement in PMS/PMDD (especially drospirenone-containing COCs)
- Improvement in menstrual migraines (in some women; may worsen in others)

### Side Effects and Risks

**Common (usually resolve within 2-3 months):**
- Breakthrough bleeding/spotting (first 3-6 months)
- Breast tenderness/enlargement
- Nausea
- Headache
- Mood changes
- Acne changes (may improve or worsen)
- Weight gain (mostly fluid; average <2 kg)

**Serious (rare):**
- VTE (venous thromboembolism): Absolute risk small (~3-9/10,000 women/year vs. 1-5/10,000 non-users); risk varies by progestin type and estrogen dose
- Arterial thrombosis (stroke, MI): Rare in healthy young women; higher with age, smoking, hypertension
- Hypertension: Check BP at initiation and periodically

## 4.3 Progestin-Only Contraceptives

### Progestin-Only Pills (POPs)

| Agent | Dosing | Key Consideration |
|-------|--------|------------------|
| **Norethindrone** | 0.35 mg daily | Take at same time daily; 3-hour window before backup needed |
| **Norgestrel** | 0.075 mg daily | Same strict timing requirement |
| **Drospirenone** (Slynd) | 4 mg daily | 24-hour window (more forgiving); antiandrogenic properties |

**Mechanism:** Primarily thickens cervical mucus; may suppress ovulation (drospirenone more consistently)

### Etonogestrel Implant (Nexplanon)

- **Dose:** 68 mg etonogestrel subdermal implant in upper inner arm
- **Duration:** FDA-approved 3 years; effective up to 5 years (off-label)
- **Effectiveness:** >99.9%; most effective reversible contraceptive method
- **Insertion/removal:** Requires trained provider; in-office procedure under local anesthesia
- **Side effects:** Irregular bleeding/spotting (most common; 50-70%); amenorrhea (20-25%); headache; weight gain; acne; mood changes
- **Return to fertility:** Immediate upon removal

### Depot Medroxyprogesterone Acetate (DMPA, Depo-Provera)

- **Dose:** 150 mg IM every 3 months OR 104 mg SC (Depo-SubQ Provera) every 3 months
- **Effectiveness:** 99.8% perfect use; 94% typical use
- **Advantages:** Highly effective; private; no daily adherence; reduces sickle cell crises and seizures
- **Disadvantages:** Average weight gain 5-10 lbs in first year; delayed return to fertility (average 10 months; up to 18 months); bone mineral density decrease (recoverable after discontinuation; not a reason to limit use to 2 years in most women per ACOG)
- **Black box warning:** Not for long-term use (>2 years) unless other methods inadequate - however, ACOG states benefits may outweigh risks for many women

## 4.4 Intrauterine Devices (IUDs)

### Types and Comparison

| Device | Hormone/Dose | FDA Duration | Bleeding Pattern | Indication |
|--------|-------------|-------------|-----------------|------------|
| **Copper IUD** (Paragard) | None | 10-12 years | Heavier menses, more cramping | Emergency contraception; non-hormonal preference |
| **LNG-IUD 52 mg** (Mirena) | Levonorgestrel 52 mg | 8 years | Lighter menses; 20% amenorrhea at 1 year | HMB; dysmenorrhea; contraception |
| **LNG-IUD 52 mg** (Liletta) | Levonorgestrel 52 mg | 8 years | Lighter menses | HMB; contraception |
| **LNG-IUD 19.5 mg** (Kyleena) | Levonorgestrel 19.5 mg | 5 years | Lighter menses; less amenorrhea than 52 mg | Contraception |
| **LNG-IUD 13.5 mg** (Skyla) | Levonorgestrel 13.5 mg | 3 years | Lighter menses | Nulliparous women; smaller uterus |

### Key Considerations

- All IUDs: >99% effective; most cost-effective long-term reversible contraception
- Copper IUD: Can be used as emergency contraception (within 5 days of unprotected intercourse); no hormonal effects; may worsen dysmenorrhea and HMB
- LNG-IUDs: Reduce menstrual bleeding 70-90% by 12 months; treat dysmenorrhea; may have systemic progestin effects (acne, mood changes, ovarian cysts)
- Fertility: Returns immediately after removal for all IUD types
- Expulsion: ~5% in first year; higher in first 3 months, immediately postpartum, and in nulliparous women
- Perforation: Rare (<1/1000 insertions); higher risk in lactating women and immediately postpartum

## 4.5 Emergency Contraception (EC) [^403^][^404^]

### Methods and Comparison

| Method | Timing | Mechanism | Efficacy | Key Considerations |
|--------|--------|-----------|----------|-------------------|
| **Levonorgestrel 1.5 mg** (Plan B One-Step, others) | Within 72 hours of UPSI (effective up to 120 hours) | Delays/inhibits ovulation; may interfere with fertilization | ~85% if within 72h; decreases with time and higher BMI | OTC availability; less effective with BMI >30; can start/restart hormonal contraception immediately |
| **Ulipristal acetate 30 mg** (Ella) | Within 120 hours (5 days) | Selective progesterone receptor modulator; delays ovulation | ~98% within 120h; more effective than LNG at 72-120h and in overweight women | Requires prescription; wait 5 days before starting/resuming hormonal contraception; less effective with enzyme-inducing drugs |
| **Copper IUD** | Within 5 days of UPSI (or within 5 days of estimated ovulation) | Prevents fertilization and implantation | >99% effective | Most effective EC method; provides ongoing contraception; preferred for BMI >30 |

### Important EC Considerations

- EC pills do NOT terminate an existing pregnancy (not abortifacients)
- EC pills are less effective in women with BMI >30 kg/m^2; copper IUD or ulipristal preferred in this population [^403^]
- After ulipristal: Wait 5 days before starting hormonal contraception; use barrier methods during this interval and for 7 days after starting hormones
- After levonorgestrel: Can start hormonal contraception immediately; use backup for 7 days
- Vomiting within 2-3 hours of EC pill: Repeat the dose
- CYP3A4 inducers (rifampin, carbamazepine, phenytoin, St. John's Wort, enzyme-inducing antiretrovirals): Reduce EC pill efficacy; copper IUD preferred
- Fertility returns quickly after EC; provide ongoing contraception counseling

## 4.6 Fertility Awareness-Based Methods (FABMs)

| Method | Description | Perfect Use Effectiveness | Typical Use Effectiveness |
|--------|-------------|--------------------------|--------------------------|
| **Calendar/rhythm method** | Abstain during fertile window based on past cycle length | 91% | 76-88% |
| **Standard Days method** | Abstain days 8-19 for cycles 26-32 days | 95% | 88% |
| **Symptothermal method** | Basal body temperature + cervical mucus + calendar observations | 99% | 76-88% |
| **Cervical mucus only** (Billings/Ovulation) | Identify and track fertile mucus patterns | 97% | 78% |
| **Basal body temperature** | Track temperature rise after ovulation | 99% | 76-88% |
| **Lactational amenorrhea** | See Section 2.6.4 | 98% | 98% |
| **Marquette Model** | Cervical mucus + Clearblue fertility monitor | 98% | 86% |

**Considerations:**
- Require high motivation, regular cycles, and partner cooperation
- Not suitable for irregular cycles, perimenopause, postpartum (unless LAM criteria met), shift workers
- Higher failure rates with typical use compared to hormonal/LARC methods
- Useful for women with medical contraindications to other methods
- Can be used for both pregnancy achievement and prevention

## 4.7 Permanent Contraception (Sterilization)

### Female Methods

| Procedure | Description | Effectiveness | Notes |
|-----------|-------------|--------------|-------|
| **Bilateral tubal ligation** | Laparoscopic clips, rings (Falope ring), or fulguration (bipolar cautery) | >99% | Laparoscopic; outpatient; general or regional anesthesia |
| **Bilateral salpingectomy** | Complete removal of both fallopian tubes | >99% | Preferred over ligation; may reduce ovarian cancer risk (opportunistic salpingectomy); slightly longer procedure |
| **Hysteroscopic tubal occlusion** (Essure) | Microinserts placed hysteroscopically | No longer available | Discontinued due to adverse event reports |

**Important counseling points:**
- Sterilization should be considered permanent; reversal is expensive, major surgery, and not always successful
- Failure rate: 0.5% cumulative over 10 years; if pregnancy occurs, higher risk of ectopic
- Younger age at sterilization (<30) associated with higher regret rates
- Does not affect menstrual cycle or ovarian function
- Vasectomy is safer, simpler, and equally effective

### Vasectomy

- **Procedure:** Outpatient; local anesthesia; interruption of vas deferens (bilateral)
- **Effectiveness:** >99%; simplest and safest permanent contraception
- **Critical requirement:** Must use backup contraception until azoospermia confirmed (usually 8-12 weeks or after 20 ejaculations; semen analysis required)
- **Reversal:** Possible via vasovasostomy or vasoepididymostomy but expensive ($5,000-15,000), not always covered by insurance; success rates vary (30-90% depending on time since vasectomy and technique)
- **Complications:** Rare; hematoma, infection, chronic scrotal pain (<1-2%)

**Shared Decision-Making Framework:**
1. Assess pregnancy intention/desires (reversible vs. permanent)
2. Review medical history for contraindications (apply US MEC)
3. Discuss effectiveness priorities
4. Evaluate lifestyle and adherence factors
5. Address non-contraceptive benefits desired
6. Discuss side effect profiles and acceptability
7. Ensure understanding of backup methods and emergency contraception

**Tiered Effectiveness Counseling (CDC):**
- Tier 1 (most effective): IUDs, implant, sterilization
- Tier 2: DMPA, POPs, COCs, patch, ring
- Tier 3: Barrier methods, FABMs, withdrawal
- Tier 4: Spermicide alone, sponge

## 5.1 Breast Cancer Screening

### Overview

Breast cancer is the most common cancer in women worldwide and the second leading cause of cancer death in women in the United States. Early detection through screening reduces breast cancer mortality. Screening recommendations vary by organization, and the choice of strategy should involve shared decision-making.

### Mammography Guidelines Comparison [^409^][^416^]

| Guideline | Start Age | Frequency | End Age | Supplemental Screening | Notes |
|-----------|-----------|-----------|---------|----------------------|-------|
| **USPSTF 2024** | 40 years | Biennial (every 2 years) | 74 years | Insufficient evidence for supplemental screening in dense breasts | Updated April 2024; lowered from age 50 to age 40 [^409^] |
| **ACS 2023** | 45 years (optional 40-44 with shared decision-making) | Annual (45-54); biennial or annual (55+) | When life expectancy <10 years | Consider for dense breasts | Emphasizes shared decision-making |
| **ACOG** | 40 years | Annual or biennial (shared decision-making) | Individualize >75 years | Consider for dense breasts | Shared decision-making approach |
| **NCCN 2026** | Risk assessment by age 25; average-risk screening at 40 | Annual | Individualize | For dense breasts or high risk | Incorporates AI-based risk assessment tools [^446^] |
| **AAFP** | 50 years (individualize 40-49) | Biennial | 74 years | Insufficient evidence | Most conservative recommendation |

### Key Points

- **USPSTF 2024 update:** Changed recommendation to biennial screening starting at age 40 (previously recommended starting at age 50) [^409^]; this change reflects modeling data suggesting benefit of earlier screening and accounts for the increasing incidence of breast cancer in younger women
- All guidelines recommend shared decision-making, especially for ages 40-49 when benefits and harms are more closely balanced
- **Dense breast notification:** Required by FDA starting September 2024; all mammography reports must include breast density information [^409^]
- Supplemental screening (ultrasound, MRI, contrast-enhanced mammography) for dense breasts: Evidence evolving; not universally recommended for average-risk women

### Breast Density

Mammographic breast density is an independent risk factor for breast cancer and reduces the sensitivity of mammography.

- Approximately 50% of women aged 40-74 have dense breasts (heterogeneously or extremely dense)
- Dense tissue appears white on mammography, as do cancers, making detection more difficult
- **Screening considerations for dense breasts:**
  - **Tomosynthesis (3D mammography):** Improves cancer detection and reduces false positives in dense breasts; now standard of care in most centers
  - **Supplemental ultrasound:** May detect additional 2-4 cancers per 1000; increases false positive rate and recalls
  - **Contrast-enhanced mammography:** Emerging role; detects cancers independent of density
  - **MRI:** Reserved for high-risk patients (lifetime risk >20%); most sensitive imaging modality

## 5.2 Risk Assessment Models [^442^][^446^][^451^]

| Model | Risk Factors Included | Best Use |
|-------|---------------------|----------|
| **Tyrer-Cuzick (IBIS v8)** | Age, menarche, parity, BMI, HRT use, family history, breast density, biopsy history, genetic test results | Lifetime risk calculation; 20% threshold triggers annual MRI eligibility |
| **Gail (BCRAT)** | Age, age at menarche, age at first live birth, number of prior breast biopsies, atypical hyperplasia, first-degree relatives with breast cancer | Chemoprevention eligibility (5-year risk >=1.67%); limited for strong family history |
| **BRCAPRO** | Detailed family history, genetic testing results, tumor markers | BRCA1/2 mutation probability |
| **CanRisk/BOADICEA** | Comprehensive family history, genetic testing, lifestyle risk factors, polygenic risk scores | Integrated risk assessment; increasingly used in genetics clinics |

**Which model to use:**
- **Average-risk women:** Gail model for chemoprevention eligibility
- **Family history present:** Tyrer-Cuzick or CanRisk/BOADICEA
- **Genetic counseling referral:** BRCAPRO or CanRisk
- **MRI eligibility determination:** Tyrer-Cuzick (>=20% lifetime risk) or other validated models

## 5.3 BRCA Testing Criteria (NCCN Guidelines) [^442^][^446^]

### Personal History Indicating Genetic Testing

- Breast cancer diagnosed <=45 years of age
- Breast cancer diagnosed <=60 years with triple-negative subtype (ER-/PR-/HER2-)
- Breast cancer at any age with Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry
- Ovarian cancer (epithelial, including fallopian tube and primary peritoneal) at any age
- Male breast cancer at any age
- Pancreatic cancer with >=1 close relative with breast/ovarian/pancreatic cancer
- Metastatic prostate cancer (germline testing)

### Family History Indicating Genetic Testing

- First- or second-degree relative meeting above criteria
- Known familial pathogenic variant in BRCA1/2 or other high/moderate penetrance gene
- >=2 breast cancers in close relatives on the same side of family
- Ovarian cancer in close relative
- Triple-negative breast cancer in relative <=60 years
- **Note:** Testing the affected relative is preferred when possible; if deceased, consider testing the unaffected individual with appropriate counseling about limitations

### Management of BRCA Carriers

| Modality | Recommendation | Notes |
|----------|---------------|-------|
| **Breast screening** | Annual breast MRI starting age 25-30 + annual mammogram starting age 30 (alternate every 6 months) | MRI is more sensitive than mammography in younger women |
| **Risk-reducing mastectomy** | Reduces breast cancer risk >90% | Individual decision; consider timing with childbearing plans |
| **Risk-reducing BSO** | Recommended age 35-40 (BRCA1) or 40-45 (BRCA2) upon completion of childbearing | Reduces ovarian cancer risk ~85-90%; also reduces breast cancer risk by removing ovarian hormones |
| **Chemoprevention** | Tamoxifen 20 mg x 5 years or raloxifene reduces breast cancer risk ~50% | For women not choosing surgery |
| **Ovarian cancer screening** | Transvaginal ultrasound + CA-125 every 6-12 months | Limited effectiveness; high false-positive rate; risk-reducing surgery preferred |
| **Prostate cancer screening** | Consider PSA screening for male BRCA carriers | Discuss with physician |

### Other High-Risk Genes

Beyond BRCA1/2, multigene panels may identify pathogenic variants in:
- **TP53 (Li-Fraumeni syndrome):** Very high cancer risk; early and frequent screening
- **PTEN (Cowden syndrome):** Breast, thyroid, endometrial cancer risk
- **PALB2:** Moderate-high breast cancer risk; similar management to BRCA2
- **CHEK2:** Moderate breast cancer risk
- **ATM:** Moderate breast cancer risk
- **STK11 (Peutz-Jeghers):** Gastrointestinal and breast cancer risk

## 5.4 Palpable Breast Mass - The "Triple Test"

The Triple Test is a validated diagnostic approach for evaluating palpable breast masses that achieves >99.5% accuracy when all three components are concordant.

| Component | Purpose | Notes |
|-----------|---------|-------|
| **1. Clinical breast examination** | Characterize mass (size, location, consistency, mobility, skin changes, nipple changes, lymphadenopathy) | Tenderness, mobility, and smooth borders favor benign; hard, fixed, irregular borders favor malignant |
| **2. Imaging** | Age <30: Ultrasound first; Age 30-39: Ultrasound or diagnostic mammogram; Age >=40: Diagnostic mammogram + targeted ultrasound | BI-RADS assessment; masses in young women are usually benign (fibroadenoma, cyst) |
| **3. Tissue sampling** | FNA or core needle biopsy for solid masses; cyst aspiration for simple cysts | Core needle biopsy preferred for most solid masses; provides histologic diagnosis |

**Interpretation:**
- **All three components concordant benign:** >99.5% negative predictive value; observation acceptable
- **All three components concordant malignant:** Proceed with treatment planning
- **Discordant results:** Excisional biopsy is mandatory regardless of individual component results

## 5.5 Common Benign Breast Conditions

### Fibrocystic Changes
- Most common benign breast condition
- Characterized by breast pain, lumpiness, nodularity (often bilateral, fluctuating with menstrual cycle)
- Worse before menses; improves after menses
- Not associated with increased breast cancer risk UNLESS atypical hyperplasia present on biopsy
- Management: Supportive measures, supportive bra, dietary modifications (reduce caffeine, fat - limited evidence), NSAIDs, vitamin E, evening primrose oil (limited evidence)
- Aspiration of symptomatic cysts for diagnosis and treatment
- Core biopsy for solid components or complex cysts

### Fibroadenoma
- Most common breast tumor in women <30 years
- Well-circumscribed, mobile, smooth, rubbery mass ("breast mouse")
- Diagnosis confirmed by ultrasound; core needle biopsy if atypical features
- Management: Observation if classic features and small; excision if large (>3 cm), growing, atypical, or patient preference
- Complex fibroadenoma: May have slightly increased cancer risk; excisional biopsy recommended

### Mastalgia (Breast Pain)

| Type | Characteristics | Management |
|------|----------------|------------|
| **Cyclical** | Related to menstrual cycle; bilateral, diffuse, upper outer quadrants; worse premenstrually | Supportive bra, dietary modifications, evening primrose oil, COCs, tamoxifen (refractory cases) |
| **Non-cyclical** | Not related to menses; may be focal or diffuse | Evaluate for extramammary causes (chest wall, Tietze syndrome, cervical radiculopathy, shingles); caffeine reduction, NSAIDs |

**Worrisome features requiring evaluation:**
- Focal, persistent pain (not diffuse)
- Associated mass
- Skin changes (erythema, peau d'orange, dimpling)
- Nipple changes (discharge, retraction, new inversion)
- Pain not responsive to supportive measures

### Nipple Discharge

| Characteristic | Likely Etiology | Workup |
|----------------|----------------|--------|
| **Bilateral, multi-duct, non-spontaneous, various colors** (white, yellow, green, brown) | Physiologic, duct ectasia, medication-related | Reassurance; stop nipple manipulation; evaluate medications; usually benign |
| **Unilateral, single-duct, spontaneous, bloody/serosanguineous** | Intraductal papilloma (most common benign cause), ductal carcinoma | Surgical referral; galactography; core needle biopsy; excisional biopsy |
| **Purulent** | Infection/abscess | Culture, antibiotics, drainage if abscess |
| **Milky (galactorrhea)** | Hyperprolactinemia, pregnancy, endocrine disorder | Pregnancy test, prolactin level, TSH, medication review |
| **Green/brown/black** | Duct ectasia, fibrocystic changes | Usually benign; excise if associated mass or atypical features |

**Medications causing nipple discharge:** Antipsychotics (risperidone, haloperidol), antidepressants (SSRIs), COCs, antihypertensives (verapamil, methyldopa, reserpine), H2 blockers (cimetidine, ranitidine), opioids, hormones.

## 6.1 Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH)

### Overview

BPH is the most common cause of lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) in aging men. Histologic prevalence increases with age: ~50% by age 60 and ~90% by age 85. BPH symptoms result from two components: a **static component** (anatomical enlargement of the prostate causing urethral compression) and a **dynamic component** (alpha-adrenergic tone in prostatic smooth muscle and bladder neck) [^408^][^412^].

**Pathophysiology:** Androgens (primarily DHT) stimulate prostatic growth. Over time, proliferation of stromal and epithelial elements in the periurethral transition zone leads to prostate enlargement and bladder outlet obstruction. Secondary detrusor changes (hypertrophy, instability, decompensation) contribute to storage and voiding symptoms.

### Clinical Presentation

**Storage symptoms (irritative):** Urinary frequency, urgency, nocturia, urge incontinence
**Voiding symptoms (obstructive):** Hesitancy, weak stream, intermittency, straining, incomplete emptying, terminal dribbling
**Complications:** Acute urinary retention, bladder stones, recurrent UTIs, hydronephrosis, renal insufficiency (rare), hematuria

### Assessment

#### International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS)

The IPSS is a validated 7-item questionnaire assessing LUTS severity over the past month. Each item scored 0-5; total score interpreted as:

| Severity | Score | Management Approach |
|----------|-------|-------------------|
| Mild | 0-7 | Watchful waiting if not bothersome |
| Moderate | 8-19 | Medical therapy indicated |
| Severe | 20-35 | Medical therapy; consider surgical options |

The IPSS also includes a quality-of-life question ("If you were to spend the rest of your life with your urinary condition the way it is now, how would you feel about that?") scored 0 (delighted) to 6 (terrible).

#### Recommended Workup

| Test | Purpose |
|------|---------|
| IPSS questionnaire | Baseline symptom severity; monitoring response |
| Digital rectal exam (DRE) | Assess prostate size, consistency, nodules |
| Urinalysis | Exclude infection, hematuria, diabetes |
| Serum creatinine | Screen for renal impairment (rarely affected) |
| PSA (if clinically appropriate) | Baseline; screen for prostate cancer; monitor response to 5-ARIs |
| Post-void residual (PVR) | Assess bladder emptying; elevated PVR (>100-150 mL) suggests significant obstruction |
| Uroflowmetry (optional) | Objective measurement of voiding function |
| Voiding diary (optional) | Quantify frequency, volumes, nocturia |

### Medical Management [^408^][^412^]

#### Watchful Waiting

For mild symptoms (IPSS <8) not bothersome to the patient:
- Lifestyle modifications: Fluid restriction 2 hours before bedtime, limit alcohol and caffeine, timed voiding, bladder training, pelvic floor exercises
- Review medications that may worsen LUTS (decongestants, anticholinergics, diuretics)
- Reassess annually with IPSS

#### Alpha-Blockers (First-Line for Moderate-Severe Symptoms)

Relax smooth muscle in the prostate and bladder neck via alpha-1 adrenergic receptor blockade. Rapid onset (days).

| Agent | Dosing | Selectivity | Key Notes |
|-------|--------|-------------|-----------|
| **Tamsulosin** (Flomax) | 0.4 mg daily; may increase to 0.8 mg | Alpha-1A selective | Most commonly prescribed; retrograde ejaculation most common side effect |
| **Silodosin** (Rapaflo) | 8 mg daily | Highly alpha-1A selective | Highest rate of ejaculatory dysfunction; take with food |
| **Alfuzosin** (Uroxatral) | 10 mg daily | Alpha-1A selective | Extended-release; take after same meal daily |
| **Doxazosin** (Cardura) | 1-8 mg daily (start 1 mg, titrate) | Non-selective alpha-1 | Also antihypertensive; requires dose titration |
| **Terazosin** (Hytrin) | 1-10 mg daily (start 1 mg, titrate) | Non-selective alpha-1 | Also antihypertensive; bedtime dosing to minimize orthostasis |

**Common side effects:** Dizziness, orthostatic hypotension (less with tamsulosin/silodosin), fatigue, retrograde ejaculation, nasal congestion
**Cataract surgery caution:** Intraoperative floppy iris syndrome (IFIS) with tamsulosin, silodosin, doxazosin; inform ophthalmologist if patient on alpha-blocker

#### 5-Alpha Reductase Inhibitors (5-ARIs)

Block conversion of testosterone to DHT, reducing prostate volume over 3-6 months.

| Agent | Dosing | 5-AR Type | Key Notes |
|-------|--------|-----------|-----------|
| **Finasteride** (Proscar) | 5 mg daily | Type II | Reduces prostate volume ~25%; PSA decreases ~50% after 6 months |
| **Dutasteride** (Avodart) | 0.5 mg daily | Type I + II (dual) | Slightly greater volume reduction; may have faster onset |

**5-ARI Key Considerations:**
- Only effective in men with enlarged prostate (typically >30-40 mL on DRE or ultrasound)
- **PSA adjustment:** Measured PSA must be doubled after 6 months of therapy for accurate cancer screening interpretation
- **Sexual side effects:** Decreased libido (3-5%), erectile dysfunction (5-8%), ejaculatory disorders (3-7%); often improve with continued use
- **Teratogenicity:** Men should not donate blood during treatment and for 1 month after discontinuation (5-ARIs in semen could affect male fetus of pregnant transfusion recipient); pregnant women should not handle crushed tablets
- **Combination therapy:** Alpha-blocker + 5-ARI (dutasteride-tamsulosin combination/Jalyn) more effective than either alone for men with enlarged prostate and moderate-severe symptoms

#### PDE5 Inhibitors

- **Tadalafil 5 mg daily:** FDA-approved for BPH/LUTS; improves erectile function simultaneously
- Mechanism: Relaxation of smooth muscle in prostate, bladder neck, and urethra via nitric oxide/cGMP pathway
- Particularly useful in men with concurrent ED

#### Other Agents

| Class | Agent | Role |
|-------|-------|------|
| **Anticholinergics** | Oxybutynin, tolterodine, solifenacin | Predominant storage symptoms with adequate bladder emptying (PVR <100-150 mL); avoid if elevated PVR |
| **Beta-3 agonists** | Mirabegron 25-50 mg daily | Storage symptoms; less anticholinergic side effects; monitor blood pressure |
| **Combination** | Alpha-blocker + anticholinergic | Mixed storage and voiding symptoms |

### Surgical and Procedural Options [^411^][^412^]

Indicated for: Failed medical therapy, recurrent urinary retention, recurrent UTIs, bladder stones, hydronephrosis, hematuria due to BPH, patient preference.

| Procedure | Best Candidates | Pros | Cons/Considerations |
|-----------|---------------|------|-------------------|
| **TURP** (Transurethral resection of prostate) | Moderate-large prostates (30-80 g); gold standard for decades | Highly effective; durable results; tissue for pathology | Bleeding risk; TUR syndrome (rare with modern techniques); retrograde ejaculation (~65%); incontinence (~2%) |
| **HoLEP** (Holmium laser enucleation) | All sizes including very large prostates (>100 g) | Complete enucleation; highly durable; minimal bleeding | Steep learning curve; retrograde ejaculation |
| **GreenLight laser** (Photoselective vaporization/PVP) | Small-large prostates | Less bleeding; outpatient; shorter catheterization | Less tissue for pathology; retreatment rates slightly higher |
| **Aquablation** | Prostate 30-80 g | Robotically guided waterjet; preserves sexual function better than TURP | Newer technology; limited long-term data |
| **UroLift** (Prostatic urethral lift) | Prostate 30-80 g, no obstructing middle lobe | Preserves sexual function (ejaculation); rapid recovery; office-based | May not work for large prostates; less symptom reduction than TURP |
| **Rezum** (Water vapor thermal therapy) | Prostate 30-80 g | Preserves sexual function; office-based | Longer time to symptom relief (4-6 weeks); limited long-term data |
| **iTIND** (Temporary implantable nitinol device) | Prostate 25-75 g | Temporary 5-7 day implant; no tissue removal; preserves sexual function | Newer technology; less robust long-term data |
| **Simple prostatectomy** | Very large prostates (>75-100 g) | Definitive removal | Open, laparoscopic, or robotic; more invasive; longer recovery |
| **PAE** (Prostatic artery embolization) | Select patients who are poor surgical candidates | Minimally invasive; preserves sexual function | Radiation exposure; limited long-term data; not widely available |

## 6.2 Prostate Cancer Screening

### Overview

Prostate cancer is the most common non-skin cancer and second leading cause of cancer death in American men. Screening with prostate-specific antigen (PSA) testing remains controversial due to concerns about overdiagnosis and overtreatment of indolent disease, but when used appropriately with shared decision-making, it reduces metastatic disease and prostate cancer mortality.

### Guidelines Summary (AUA/SUO 2023) [^410^][^413^][^415^]

| Recommendation | Grade |
|---------------|-------|
| PSA-based screening in the context of shared decision-making is recommended for men ages 55-69 | Strong, Grade A |
| PSA should be used as the first screening test | Strong, Grade A |
| Repeat PSA if newly elevated before secondary biomarker/imaging/biopsy | Expert Opinion |
| Offer baseline PSA to ages 45-50 | Conditional, Grade B |
| Offer screening at age 40-45 for men at increased risk (Black ancestry, germline mutations, strong family history) | Strong, Grade B |
| Regular screening every 2-4 years for ages 50-69 | Strong, Grade A |
| Personalize re-screening interval based on age, PSA, risk, life expectancy | Conditional, Grade B |
| DRE may be used alongside PSA to establish risk | Conditional, Grade C |
| Do NOT use PSA velocity as sole indication for biopsy | Strong, Grade B |
| Use validated risk calculators to inform shared decision-making | Conditional, Grade B |
| May forgo near-term biopsy if risk sufficiently low | Clinical Principle |

### PSA Interpretation Framework

| PSA Level | Interpretation | Next Steps |
|-----------|---------------|------------|
| <1.0 ng/mL | Low risk; low probability of significant cancer | Extended screening interval (every 4 years if age 50-69) |
| 1.0-2.5 ng/mL | Low-intermediate risk | Continue routine screening every 2-4 years |
| 2.5-4.0 ng/mL | Intermediate risk | Consider additional risk factors; continue screening annually |
| 4.0-10 ng/mL | Elevated; ~25% cancer risk | Free PSA, PHI, 4Kscore, or MRI to refine risk |
| >10 ng/mL | High suspicion; ~50% cancer risk | Prompt urology referral; MRI; consider biopsy |

**Factors Affecting PSA (non-cancer causes of elevation):**
- Age (PSA normally rises with age)
- Prostate volume/BPH
- Prostatitis (acute or chronic)
- Ejaculation (transient increase; avoid PSA testing within 48 hours)
- DRE (minimal effect on PSA)
- 5-ARIs (reduce PSA by ~50% after 6 months; double measured PSA for interpretation)

### Additional Biomarkers

| Biomarker | Description | Role |
|-----------|-------------|------|
| **Free PSA (% free)** | Percentage of unbound PSA | Higher % suggests BPH; lower % (<10%) suggests cancer |
| **PHI** (Prostate Health Index) | Combines total PSA, free PSA, and p2PSA | Increases specificity for clinically significant cancer |
| **4Kscore** | Risk calculator combining 4 kallikrein markers with clinical data | Predicts high-grade cancer risk; reduces unnecessary biopsies |
| **SelectMDx** | Urine HOXC6 and DLX1 mRNA | Helps determine biopsy need |
| **ExoDx (EPI)** | Urine exosome RNA test | Non-invasive risk stratification |
| **MRI before biopsy** | Multiparametric MRI with PI-RADS scoring | Increasingly standard; identifies suspicious lesions for targeted biopsy; reduces unnecessary biopsies |

### Shared Decision-Making for PSA Screening

**Discuss with patient:**
- Potential benefits: Reduced risk of metastatic prostate cancer; reduced prostate cancer mortality (1 life saved per 200-1000 men screened over 10-15 years depending on risk)
- Potential harms: False positives, overdiagnosis (treatment of cancers that would never cause symptoms), biopsy complications (infection, bleeding), treatment side effects (incontinence, erectile dysfunction)
- Individual risk factors: Age, race, family history, genetic factors
- Patient values and preferences

### PSA Decision Framework by Age and Risk

| Population | Screening Recommendation |
|-----------|-------------------------|
| Average risk, age <40 | Not recommended |
| Average risk, age 40-45 | Consider baseline PSA if desired |
| Increased risk (Black, BRCA+, strong FH), age 40-45 | Baseline PSA recommended |
| Average risk, age 45-50 | Consider baseline PSA |
| All men, age 50-69 | Shared decision-making; PSA every 2-4 years based on baseline |
| Age >70 or life expectancy <10 years | Generally not recommended (harm may exceed benefit) |

## 6.3 Prostatitis

### NIH Classification [^437^][^438^]

| Category | Name | Presentation |
|----------|------|-------------|
| **I** | Acute bacterial prostatitis | Fever, chills, dysuria, frequency, urgency, perineal/suprapubic pain; acute illness; tender, swollen prostate on DRE |
| **II** | Chronic bacterial prostatitis | Recurrent UTIs with same organism; relatively asymptomatic between episodes; positive bacterial cultures |
| **IIIA** | Chronic prostatitis/chronic pelvic pain syndrome (CP/CPPS) - inflammatory | Chronic pelvic pain >3 months; WBCs in expressed prostatic secretion (EPS), semen, or VB3 |
| **IIIB** | CP/CPPS - non-inflammatory | Chronic pelvic pain >3 months; NO WBCs in EPS/semen/VB3 |
| **IV** | Asymptomatic inflammatory prostatitis | No symptoms; incidental finding (e.g., elevated PSA, biopsy) |

### Acute Bacterial Prostatitis (Category I)

**Pathogens:** *E. coli* (most common, ~80%); other Enterobacteriaceae (*Klebsiella*, *Proteus*, *Enterobacter*, *Pseudomonas*); sexually transmitted pathogens in sexually active men <35

**Treatment:**
- **Outpatient (mild-moderate):** Ciprofloxacin 500 mg BID x 2-4 weeks OR levofloxacin 500-750 mg daily x 2-4 weeks; TMP-SMX DS BID x 4-6 weeks if susceptible
- **Inpatient (severe/sepsis):** IV ceftriaxone + fluoroquinolone; transition to oral after afebrile 24-48 hours
- **Acute urinary retention:** Use suprapubic catheter (NOT urethral catheterization, which may worsen infection)
- **Follow-up:** Repeat urine culture 1-2 weeks after completing therapy

**Complications:** Prostatic abscess (suspect if persistent fever despite antibiotics; drain percutaneously or transurethrally), chronic prostatitis, epididymo-orchitis, bacteremia.

### Chronic Prostatitis/Chronic Pelvic Pain Syndrome (CP/CPPS) - AUA 2024 Guideline [^436^][^437^]

**Key Principle:** CP/CPPS is a syndrome of chronic pelvic pain, NOT primarily an infection. A multimodal, multidisciplinary approach is required.

**CP/CPPS Treatment Algorithm:**

| Category | Treatment | Evidence Grade |
|----------|-----------|---------------|
| **Lifestyle modifications** | Dietary changes (avoid spicy, acidic, caffeine, alcohol); aerobic exercise; heat therapy; stress management | Conditional, Grade C |
| **Alpha-blockers** | Tamsulosin, alfuzosin (if predominant voiding symptoms) | Moderate, Grade B |
| **NSAIDs** | Ibuprofen, naproxen (short course) | Conditional, Grade B |
| **Phytotherapeutics** | Saw palmetto, quercetin, pollen extract (Cernilton) | Conditional, Grade B |
| **Physical therapy** | Pelvic floor myofascial release (internal and external); trigger point therapy | Conditional, Grade C |
| **Neuropathic medications** | TCAs (amitriptyline), gabapentinoids, SSRIs | Conditional, Grade C |
| **PDE5 inhibitors** | Daily tadalafil | Conditional, Grade B |
| **Psychological** | Cognitive behavioral therapy, biofeedback | Conditional, Grade C |
| **ESWT** (extracorporeal shockwave therapy) | Low-intensity shockwave to perineum | Moderate, Grade A |
| **Acupuncture** | Traditional acupuncture | Conditional, Grade B |
| **TENS** | Transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation | Conditional, Grade B |

**What NOT to do in CP/CPPS [^437^]:**
- Do NOT send for psychological evaluation before medical evaluation
- Do NOT prescribe repeated courses of antibiotics with negative cultures
- Do NOT perform prostate surgery solely for pain relief
- Do NOT use long-term systemic glucocorticoids

# Chapter 7: Erectile Dysfunction

---

## 7.1 Clinical Overview

Erectile dysfunction (ED) is defined as the consistent or recurrent inability to achieve or maintain an erection sufficient for satisfactory sexual performance. ED affects approximately 30 million men in the United States and 150 million men worldwide. Prevalence increases with age but ED is NOT a normal consequence of aging. It is a significant cause of decreased quality of life, relationship distress, and psychological burden.

**Prevalence by Age:**
- Age 40: ~40% experience some degree of ED
- Age 70: ~70% experience some degree of ED
- Complete ED: ~5% at age 40, ~15% at age 70

### Etiology

ED is frequently multifactorial, with vascular, neurologic, hormonal, psychogenic, and medication-related components often coexisting.

| Category | Causes | Notes |
|----------|--------|-------|
| **Vascular** | Atherosclerosis, hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, smoking, pelvic trauma, Peyronie disease | Most common cause; endothelial dysfunction is the shared mechanism |
| **Neurologic** | Spinal cord injury, multiple sclerosis, Parkinson disease, stroke, pelvic surgery (prostatectomy), diabetic neuropathy | Nerve damage impairs initiation and maintenance of erection |
| **Hormonal** | Hypogonadism (low testosterone), hyperprolactinemia, thyroid disease (hypo- and hyper-), Cushing syndrome | Testosterone deficiency reduces libido and erectile function |
| **Psychogenic** | Performance anxiety, depression, relationship conflict, stress, history of sexual trauma | Often coexists with organic causes; "situational" ED suggests psychogenic component |
| **Medication-related** | Thiazide diuretics, beta-blockers, SSRIs, antipsychotics, finasteride/dutasteride, opioids, anticonvulsants, alcohol | Review all medications; do not stop essential medications without discussion |
| **Structural** | Peyronie disease (penile fibrosis/curvature), penile trauma, congenital abnormalities | Penile deformity may impair penetration |
| **Lifestyle** | Obesity, smoking, sedentary lifestyle, excessive alcohol use, recreational drugs | Modifiable risk factors |

### Critical Concept: ED as Cardiovascular Risk Marker [^414^]

ED and cardiovascular disease (CVD) share common risk factors, particularly endothelial dysfunction. The penile arteries are smaller than coronary arteries, so vascular impairment manifests earlier in erectile function than in cardiac function.

- ED often precedes coronary events by 2-5 years
- Men with ED have significantly increased risk of MI, stroke, and cardiovascular death
- **Recommendation:** All men presenting with ED should be screened for cardiovascular risk factors (BP, lipids, fasting glucose, smoking status, BMI)

**Princeton Consensus III Risk Stratification for Sexual Activity:**

| Risk Level | Criteria | Recommendation |
|------------|----------|---------------|
| **Low risk** | Asymptomatic; <3 cardiac risk factors; controlled hypertension | Can safely proceed with sexual activity and ED treatment |
| **Intermediate risk** | 3+ cardiac risk factors; mild stable angina; recent MI (2-6 weeks); heart failure (NYHA II) | Requires further cardiac evaluation (stress testing) before resuming sexual activity |
| **High risk** | Unstable angina, uncontrolled hypertension, recent MI (<2 weeks), severe heart failure (NYHA III-IV), high-risk arrhythmias, obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy | Defer sexual activity until cardiac condition stabilized and cleared by cardiology |

## 7.2 Evaluation

### History
- Detailed sexual history: Onset (sudden vs. gradual), severity, situational vs. global, morning/nocturnal erections
- Medical history: Cardiovascular disease, diabetes, hypertension, neurologic disease, pelvic surgery/radiation
- Psychosocial: Depression, anxiety, relationship issues, stressors
- Medication review: All prescription, OTC, and supplements
- Lifestyle: Smoking, alcohol, exercise, diet, recreational drugs
- Validated questionnaires: IIEF (International Index of Erectile Function), SHIM (Sexual Health Inventory for Men)

### Physical Examination
- Vital signs (BP, BMI, waist circumference)
- Cardiovascular examination
- Genital examination: Penile abnormalities (Peyronie plaques), testicular size/consistency
- DRE for prostate (if age-appropriate)
- Neurologic: Lower extremity sensation, bulbocavernosus reflex, anal tone
- Secondary sexual characteristics

### Laboratory Testing

| Test | Purpose |
|------|---------|
| **Morning total testosterone** (7-10 AM) | Screen for hypogonadism; two separate low values required |
| **Prolactin** | If low testosterone with low/normal LH; if visual changes/headaches |
| **TSH** | Screen for thyroid dysfunction |
| **Fasting glucose or HbA1c** | Screen for diabetes |
| **Fasting lipid panel** | Cardiovascular risk assessment |
| **PSA** | Age-appropriate prostate cancer screening before TRT |
| **LH, FSH** | If testosterone low, to distinguish primary vs. secondary |

## 7.3 PDE5 Inhibitors (First-Line Therapy) [^414^]

Phosphodiesterase type 5 inhibitors are the first-line pharmacologic therapy for ED in most men. They enhance the natural erectile response to sexual stimulation by increasing cGMP in the corpus cavernosum, leading to smooth muscle relaxation and increased blood flow.

| Agent | Onset | Duration | Dosing | Food Effect | Distinctive Features |
|-------|-------|----------|--------|-------------|---------------------|
| **Sildenafil** (Viagra) | 30-60 min | 4-6 hours | 25-100 mg PRN | Delayed by high-fat meal | Most well-known; blue-tinted visual changes rare |
| **Tadalafil** (Cialis) | 30-60 min | 24-36 hours | 10-20 mg PRN or 2.5-5 mg daily | No effect | Long duration allows spontaneity; daily option; also FDA-approved for BPH |
| **Vardenafil** (Levitra, Staxyn) | 30-60 min | 4-6 hours | 5-20 mg PRN | Delayed by high-fat meal | Similar to sildenafil; available as ODT |
| **Avanafil** (Stendra) | 15-30 min | 4-6 hours | 50-200 mg PRN | No effect | Fastest onset; fewer side effects |

### Administration Guidance

- Take 30-60 minutes before anticipated sexual activity (15-30 min for avanafil)
- Sexual stimulation is REQUIRED for efficacy (PDE5 inhibitors do not cause automatic erections)
- May take 2-3 attempts/doses to determine optimal dose and technique
- Limit to once daily
- Avoid heavy alcohol use (impairs erectile function independently)

### Common Side Effects

| Side Effect | Sildenafil | Tadalafil | Vardenafil | Avanafil |
|-------------|-----------|-----------|------------|----------|
| Headache | 10-16% | 5-15% | 10-16% | 5-10% |
| Flushing | 5-13% | 2-5% | 5-13% | 3-5% |
| Dyspepsia | 4-10% | 1-4% | 4-10% | 1-2% |
| Nasal congestion | 2-9% | 2-5% | 2-9% | 1-2% |
| Dizziness | 2-3% | 2-3% | 2-3% | 1% |
| Myalgia | <1% | 3-6% | <1% | <1% |
| Visual changes (blue tinge) | 2-3% | Rare | Rare | Rare |

### Contraindications and Precautions

**ABSOLUTE CONTRAINDICATION:**

**Nitrates** (nitroglycerin tablets, spray, patch; isosorbide mononitrate/dinitrate; recreational "poppers") in ANY form. The combination can cause profound, potentially fatal hypotension.

**Mandatory nitrate washout periods:**
- Sildenafil and vardenafil: At least 24 hours before nitrate administration
- Tadalafil: At least 48 hours before nitrate administration (longer half-life)

**Relative Contraindications/Cautions:**
- Severe hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh C)
- Severe renal impairment (CrCl <30 mL/min - reduce dose)
- Hereditary retinal disorders (retinitis pigmentosa)
- History of non-arteritic anterior ischemic optic neuropathy (NAION)
- Severe cardiovascular disease (see Princeton Consensus above)

**Alpha-blocker precaution:** PDE5 inhibitors + alpha-blockers can cause symptomatic hypotension. If used together:
- Ensure patient is on stable alpha-blocker dose first
- Start PDE5 inhibitor at lowest dose
- Separate dosing times (alpha-blocker at bedtime, PDE5 at different time)
- Tamsulosin and silodosin have lower interaction risk than doxazosin/terazosin

## 7.4 Second-Line Therapy

| Therapy | Mechanism | Efficacy | Notes |
|---------|-----------|----------|-------|
| **Vacuum erection device (VED)** | Negative pressure draws blood into corpus cavernosum + constriction ring at base | 70-90% success rate | Non-invasive; high satisfaction with proper instruction; excellent for penile rehabilitation after prostatectomy; safe with all medications |
| **Intracavernosal alprostadil (ICI)** | Prostaglandin E1 injected directly into corpus cavernosum | 85-90% efficacy | Highly effective; side effects: penile pain (10-20%), priapism (1-5%), fibrosis (2-5%); requires patient education |
| **Intraurethral alprostadil (MUSE)** | Pellet placed in urethra; absorbed into corpus cavernosum | 40-60% efficacy | Less effective than ICI; penile/urethral burning (25-30%); requires partner education |

## 7.5 Third-Line Therapy

### Penile Prosthesis Implantation

- **Indication:** Failure or contraindication of all other therapies; patient preference
- **Types:**
  - **Inflatable 3-piece:** Reservoir, pump, cylinders; most natural appearance and function; highest satisfaction
  - **Inflatable 2-piece:** No separate reservoir; easier to place
  - **Malleable/semi-rigid:** Bendable rods; simplest; always firm; easiest to use for patients with limited dexterity
- **Patient/partner satisfaction:** ~90-95% for inflatable devices
- **Risks:** Infection (1-3% in primary implantation; higher in revision/diabetic patients), mechanical failure (10-15% at 10 years for inflatable), erosion, device malfunction

## 7.6 Treatment Algorithm Summary

| Step | Therapy | When to Use |
|------|---------|-------------|
| 1 | Lifestyle modification + address risk factors | All patients |
| 2 | PDE5 inhibitor | First-line pharmacologic for most men |
| 3 | Optimize PDE5 (dose, timing, food, testosterone correction) | If initial PDE5 fails |
| 4 | Second-line: VED, ICI, MUSE | PDE5 failure or contraindication |
| 5 | Third-line: Penile prosthesis | All other options exhausted |

# Chapter 8: Male Hypogonadism

---

## 8.1 Clinical Overview

Male hypogonadism is defined as the failure of the testes to produce adequate testosterone and/or sperm. It can be classified by location of dysfunction:

- **Primary (hypergonadotropic):** Testicular failure; LH and FSH are elevated
- **Secondary (hypogonadotropic):** Hypothalamic or pituitary dysfunction; LH and FSH are low or inappropriately normal
- **Combined/mixed:** Elements of both

### Symptoms

| Category | Symptoms |
|----------|----------|
| **Sexual** | Decreased libido (hallmark), erectile dysfunction, reduced morning/spontaneous erections, decreased ejaculate volume |
| **Physical** | Decreased muscle mass and strength, increased body fat (especially visceral), reduced bone density/osteoporosis, decreased shaving frequency, diminished body hair, gynecomastia, reduced testicular volume, hot flashes |
| **Energy/Mood/Cognitive** | Fatigue, decreased energy, irritability, depression, poor concentration, decreased motivation, sleep disturbance |
| **Metabolic** | Insulin resistance, metabolic syndrome, anemia (normocytic) |

### Diagnosis

#### Morning Total Testosterone (Critical Requirement)

- **Measure between 7:00-10:00 AM** (peak of circadian rhythm; testosterone varies 20-30% throughout the day)
- **Confirm with repeat testing:** Low total testosterone on two separate mornings is required for diagnosis
- **Threshold for treatment:** Typically total testosterone <300 ng/dL (10.4 nmol/L) on two occasions PLUS consistent symptoms
- **Important caveat:** SHBG increases with age, liver disease, and some medications; in these situations, free testosterone or bioavailable testosterone may be more accurate

#### Additional Testing

| Test | Purpose | When to Order |
|------|---------|--------------|
| **LH and FSH** | Differentiate primary vs. secondary hypogonadism | All patients with confirmed low testosterone |
| **Prolactin** | Screen for prolactinoma/hyperprolactinemia | Secondary hypogonadism (low/normal LH) |
| **Ferritin** | Screen for hemochromatosis | All patients; hemochromatosis causes both primary and secondary |
| **Estradiol** | Assess aromatization | If gynecomastia present |
| **DHEAS** | Screen for adrenal source | If adrenal androgen excess suspected |
| **Semen analysis** | Assess fertility status | If fertility desired |
| **Bone density (DXA)** | Screen for osteoporosis | If prolonged hypogonadism suspected |
| **Pituitary MRI** | Evaluate for pituitary adenoma | Secondary hypogonadism with elevated prolactin OR if no clear cause |
| **Karyotype** | Genetic testing | If primary hypogonadism with small, firm testes (Klinefelter syndrome) |

### Causes

#### Primary (Hypergonadotropic - Elevated LH/FSH)

| Cause | Key Features |
|-------|-------------|
| **Klinefelter syndrome** (47,XXY) | Most common genetic cause; small firm testes, tall stature, gynecomastia, infertility; karyotype confirms |
| **Testicular trauma, torsion** | History of event; unilateral or bilateral damage |
| **Orchitis** (mumps most common) | Viral orchitis; occurs 4-6 days after parotitis; 30% bilateral |
| **Chemotherapy/radiation** | Dose-dependent; may be reversible if low dose |
| **Cryptorchidism** (undescended testes) | Increased risk even if surgically corrected |
| **Aging** (andropause/testicular senescence) | Gradual decline; ~1% per year after age 40 |
| **Hemochromatosis** | Iron deposition in testes and pituitary; check ferritin |
| **Autoimmune testicular failure** | Rare; associated with other autoimmune diseases |
| **Anabolic steroid abuse** | Exogenous androgens suppress endogenous production; may be reversible |

#### Secondary (Hypogonadotropic - Low/Normal LH/FSH)

| Cause | Key Features |
|-------|-------------|
| **Kallmann syndrome** | Congenital GnRH deficiency + anosmia; delayed puberty |
| **Pituitary tumors** (prolactinoma most common) | Headache, visual field defects, elevated prolactin |
| **Hyperprolactinemia** | Elevated prolactin suppresses GnRH; medications, prolactinoma, renal failure |
| **Hemochromatosis** | Iron deposition in pituitary |
| **Cushing syndrome** | Elevated cortisol suppresses HPG axis |
| **Opioids** | Common cause; dose-dependent suppression |
| **Exogenous testosterone/anabolic steroids** | Negative feedback on HPG axis |
| **GnRH analogs** (leuprolide, etc.) | Therapeutic suppression |
| **Severe obesity** | Adipose tissue aromatizes testosterone to estradiol; elevated estradiol suppresses GnRH |
| **Uncontrolled diabetes** | Associated with low testosterone; improves with glycemic control |
| **Chronic illness, malnutrition** | Any severe illness can suppress gonadotropins |
| **Sleep deprivation, shift work** | Disrupts circadian testosterone rhythm |

## 8.2 Testosterone Replacement Therapy (TRT)

### Pre-Treatment Requirements

Before initiating TRT, the following must be confirmed:
1. Diagnosis of hypogonadism (low morning testosterone on 2 occasions + symptoms)
2. Baseline PSA and DRE (age-appropriate prostate cancer screening)
3. Baseline hematocrit/hemoglobin
4. Fertility status assessed and discussed (exogenous testosterone suppresses spermatogenesis)

### TRT Delivery Methods

| Method | Typical Dosing | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|--------|---------------|------------|---------------|
| **Testosterone cypionate/enanthate** | 50-200 mg IM/SQ every 1-2 weeks or 100-200 mg every 2 weeks | Inexpensive; well-established; effective | Peaks and troughs; injections; polycythemia risk; not physiologic profile |
| **Testosterone undecanoate** (Aveed) | 750 mg IM, then 750 mg at 4 weeks, then q10 weeks | Longer interval between injections | Requires REMS-certified provider; rare risk of POME (pulmonary oil microembolism) and anaphylaxis |
| **Testosterone gel 1-1.62%** (AndroGel, Testim) | 1-2 packets (25-100 mg) applied daily to shoulders/upper arms | Steady physiologic levels; easy application | Skin transfer risk to women/children (must wash hands, cover site); daily application; cost; variable absorption |
| **Testosterone patch** (Androderm) | 2-4 mg patch applied nightly | Steady levels | Skin irritation at application site; poor adherence; falls off with sweating |
| **Testosterone pellets** (Testopel) | 150-450 mg subcutaneous pellets every 3-6 months | Long-acting; convenient; steady levels | In-office procedure with incision; extrusion risk (5-10%); difficult to remove |
| **Buccal tablet** (Striant) | 30 mg applied to gum BID | Steady levels; no liver toxicity | Gum irritation; BID dosing; bitter taste |
| **Nasal gel** (Natesto) | 11 mg per nostril TID (total 33 mg) | Preserves some fertility; no polycythemia concern; mimics pulsatile delivery | TID dosing inconvenient; nasal irritation; may affect smell |
| **Oral testosterone undecanoate** (Jatenzo, Kyzatrex, Tlando) | 225-400 mg BID with food | Oral; convenient; no liver toxicity | Variable absorption; requires monitoring of blood pressure; take with fatty food; cost |

### Monitoring on TRT

| Parameter | Baseline | Follow-up Schedule | Action if Abnormal |
|-----------|----------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Testosterone (trough level) | Yes | 2-3 months after initiation or dose change, then annually | Target 400-700 ng/dL trough; adjust dose if outside range |
| Hematocrit/hemoglobin | Yes | 3-6 months after initiation, then annually | Hold TRT if Hct >54%; therapeutic phlebotomy if >54% |
| PSA | Yes | 3-6 months after initiation, then annually | Urology referral if PSA increases >1.4 ng/mL over 12 months or abnormal DRE |
| DRE | Yes | Annually | Urology referral if nodule or induration |
| Bone density | If indicated | If prolonged deficiency was present | DXA repeat at 1-2 years |
| Cardiovascular risk assessment | Yes | Ongoing | Manage BP, lipids, glucose, weight |

### Risks and Side Effects

| Risk/Side Effect | Incidence | Management |
|-----------------|-----------|------------|
| **Polycythemia** (Hct >54%) | 5-20% | Dose reduction; switch to transdermal or nasal; therapeutic phlebotomy if symptomatic or >54% |
| **Infertility/suppressed spermatogenesis** | Universal with exogenous testosterone | Expected; do NOT prescribe to men trying to conceive; use HCG or SERM instead |
| **Acne, oily skin** | 10-20% | Skin care; may improve with time |
| **Gynecomastia** | 5-10% | If estradiol elevated, consider aromatase inhibitor; surgical excision if persistent |
| **Fluid retention/edema** | 5-10% | Usually mild; monitor if heart failure |
| **Sleep apnea** | May worsen existing OSA | Screen with STOP-BANG; refer for sleep study if symptoms |
| **Prostate effects** | No clear evidence TRT causes prostate cancer | Monitor PSA and DRE; may promote growth of existing cancer |
| **Cardiovascular risk** | Evidence mixed; controversial | Use caution in high-risk patients; transdermal may be safer |

## 8.3 Fertility Preservation and Alternatives [^419^][^421^]

### Critical Warning

**Exogenous testosterone suppresses gonadotropins (LH and FSH) via negative feedback, which impairs spermatogenesis.** All men should be counseled about this effect before starting TRT.

**AUA Guidelines (2024) [^421^]:**
- Discuss the long-term impact of exogenous testosterone on spermatogenesis with all men considering TRT (Strong Recommendation, Grade A)
- Do NOT prescribe exogenous testosterone to men currently trying to conceive (Strong Recommendation, Grade A)
- For men desiring fertility, may use AIs, hCG, SERMs, or combination therapy (Conditional Recommendation, Grade C)

### Alternatives for Men Desiring Fertility

| Approach | Agent | Dosing | Mechanism | Notes |
|----------|-------|--------|-----------|-------|
| **SERM (Clomiphene citrate)** | Clomiphene citrate | 25-50 mg PO daily (or EOD) | Stimulates hypothalamus; increases GnRH pulsatility; raises LH, FSH, and endogenous testosterone; preserves spermatogenesis | Off-label; most commonly used alternative; monitor for visual changes, mood effects |
| **Enclomiphene citrate** | Enclomiphene ( purified trans-isomer) | 12.5-25 mg daily | Similar to clomiphene but pure estrogen antagonist isomer | Not FDA-approved for hypogonadism; available through compounding; fewer estrogenic side effects |
| **hCG monotherapy** | Human chorionic gonadotropin | 1,500-3,000 IU SC/IM 2-3x/week | Directly stimulates Leydig cells; maintains intratesticular testosterone (critical for spermatogenesis) | Effective for secondary hypogonadism; expensive; injections required |
| **hCG + TRT** | hCG 500 IU every other day + ongoing TRT | As above | hCG maintains intratesticular testosterone while TRT maintains systemic levels | Allows continued TRT with some spermatogenesis preservation [^419^] |
| **FSH + hCG** | hCG (as above) + FSH 75-150 IU 2-3x/week | As above | FSH directly stimulates Sertoli cells and spermatogenesis | For men with persistent oligospermia on hCG alone |
| **Aromatase inhibitors** | Anastrozole 1 mg daily OR letrozole 2.5 mg daily | As above | Reduces estradiol conversion; decreases negative feedback; increases LH, FSH, testosterone | Off-label; for men with elevated estradiol or obese men with high aromatase activity |

### Approach to Hypogonadal Man Desiring Fertility

1. If fertility is immediately desired: Start with clomiphene citrate or hCG monotherapy (NOT TRT)
2. If fertility desired in the future but symptomatic now: Consider hCG + TRT combination, with plan to transition off TRT when ready to conceive
3. If already on TRT and fertility desired: TRT must be discontinued; transition to hCG, SERM, or combination; spermatogenesis recovery may take 6-12 months or longer
4. If azoospermic after TRT discontinuation: May require hCG + FSH therapy; refer to reproductive urologist

# Chapter 9: Testicular Disorders

---

## 9.1 Testicular Cancer

### Overview

Testicular cancer is the most common solid malignancy in men ages 15-35. Despite being relatively uncommon overall, it has excellent prognosis with modern treatment (5-year survival >95% for all stages). Germ cell tumors account for 95% of cases.

**Epidemiology:**
- Incidence: 6-7 per 100,000 men per year in the US
- Bimodal age distribution: Seminoma (peak 30-40 years); Non-seminoma (peak 20-30 years)
- Incidence increasing in most developed countries over past several decades

### Risk Factors

| Risk Factor | Relative Risk | Notes |
|-------------|--------------|-------|
| **Cryptorchidism (undescended testis)** | 5-10x | Risk elevated even with surgical correction; highest for intra-abdominal testis |
| **Personal history** | 12-20x | Contralateral testis at risk |
| **Family history** | 4-10x | First-degree relative |
| **Klinefelter syndrome** | 50x | Exclusively mediastinal non-seminomatous germ cell tumors |
| **HIV infection** | 1.5-2x | |
| **Intratubular germ cell neoplasia (ITGCN/CIS)** | 50% progress to cancer within 5 years | |

### Pathology

| Type | Features | Tumor Markers |
|------|----------|---------------|
| **Seminoma** | Age 30-40; pure histology; radiosensitive; excellent prognosis; large cells with clear cytoplasm | LDH elevated; AFP always normal (if AFP elevated, indicates non-seminoma component) |
| **Non-seminomatous germ cell tumor (NSGCT)** | Age 20-30; more aggressive; includes embryonal carcinoma, yolk sac tumor, choriocarcinoma, teratoma | AFP elevated (yolk sac, embryonal); hCG elevated (choriocarcinoma, embryonal, some seminomas); LDH elevated |
| **Mixed germ cell tumor** | Combination of seminoma and NSGCT elements | Variable depending on components |

### Screening

- **USPSTF/AAFP:** No routine screening recommended in asymptomatic men
- However, testicular self-examination may be discussed with high-risk men (history of cryptorchidism, prior testicular cancer, family history)
- Most testicular cancers are discovered by the patient or partner

### Diagnosis

| Test | Purpose | Notes |
|------|---------|-------|
| **Scrotal ultrasound** | Nearly 100% sensitivity; characterizes mass | Hypoechoic intratesticular mass is highly suspicious; extratesticular masses usually benign |
| **Tumor markers (AFP, hCG, LDH)** | Pre-treatment baseline; aid diagnosis; monitor response | Draw before orchiectomy; check half-life after treatment (AFP 5-7 days, hCG 1-3 days) |
| **CT chest/abdomen/pelvis** | Staging | Evaluate retroperitoneal lymph nodes and distant metastases |
| **Orchiectomy** (inguinal approach) | Diagnostic and therapeutic | Inguinal (NOT scrotal) approach to avoid scrotal violation; provides tissue for histology |
| **Chest X-ray** | Evaluate lung metastases | If CT chest not performed |
| **Brain imaging** (MRI) | If choriocarcinoma or very high hCG | Choriocarcinoma has propensity for brain metastases |

### Staging (AJCC TNMS System)

| Stage | Features |
|-------|----------|
| **I** | Tumor confined to testis/epididymis; no nodal/distant metastases; tumor markers normalize after orchiectomy |
| **IA** | pT1 (limited to testis/epididymis without vascular/lymphatic invasion), tumor markers normalizing |
| **IB** | pT2-4 (tumor beyond tunica vaginalis, spermatic cord, or scrotum; or vascular/lymphatic invasion) |
| **IS** | Persistent elevation of tumor markers after orchiectomy (S for serum marker elevation) |
| **II** | Retroperitoneal lymph node involvement |
| **IIA** | Lymph nodes <=2 cm |
| **IIB** | Lymph nodes 2-5 cm |
| **IIC** | Lymph nodes >5 cm |
| **III** | Distant metastases or non-retroperitoneal nodal involvement |
| **IIIA** | Non-pulmonary visceral metastases absent; marker levels low |
| **IIIB** | Intermediate marker levels; non-pulmonary visceral metastases absent |
| **IIIC** | High marker levels OR non-pulmonary visceral metastases present |

### Treatment

| Stage/Type | Treatment | Prognosis |
|------------|-----------|-----------|
| **Stage I seminoma** | Surveillance (preferred for compliant patients), single-agent carboplatin x 1-2 cycles, or para-aortic radiotherapy | 99%+ survival; 15-20% relapse with surveillance (salvageable) |
| **Stage I non-seminoma** | Surveillance (preferred), RPLND (nerve-sparing), or BEP x 1 cycle adjuvant | 99%+ survival; 25-30% relapse with surveillance (salvageable) |
| **Stage II seminoma** | Chemotherapy (BEP x 3-4 cycles) or radiotherapy (for IIA-B) | 95%+ survival |
| **Stage II non-seminoma** | Chemotherapy (BEP x 3-4 cycles) +/- post-chemo RPLND | 95%+ survival |
| **Stage III (good risk)** | BEP x 3 cycles | 90-95% survival |
| **Stage III (intermediate/poor risk)** | BEP x 4 cycles | 80-90% intermediate; 50-70% poor risk |
| **Post-chemo residual mass** | RPLND (NSGCT) or surveillance (seminoma if <3 cm and PET negative) | Depends on histology |

**BEP regimen:** Bleomycin, Etoposide, Cisplatin (platinum-based chemotherapy)

### Follow-up

- **Surveillance after Stage I:** Physical exam, tumor markers, and CT abdomen/pelvis at scheduled intervals for years
- **Fertility:** Offer sperm banking before any treatment (chemotherapy, radiation, RPLND)
- **Late effects:** Secondary malignancies (especially with radiation), cardiovascular disease, hypogonadism, retrograde ejaculation (after RPLND), hearing loss (cisplatin), pulmonary toxicity (bleomycin)
- **Contralateral testis:** Lifelong surveillance; annual ultrasound may be considered in high-risk patients

## 9.2 Testicular Torsion (EMERGENCY) [^433^]

### Overview

Testicular torsion is a surgical emergency caused by twisting of the spermatic cord, compromising blood supply to the testis. It is most common in neonates and adolescents (peak incidence: neonatal period and 12-18 years). The "bell-clapper deformity" (abnormally high insertion of tunica vaginalis allowing testis to rotate freely) is the underlying anatomic predisposition in adolescents.

### Clinical Features

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Pain** | Sudden onset, severe, unilateral scrotal pain; may radiate to inguinal region or abdomen |
| **Nausea/vomiting** | Very common (50-70%); reflects severity of pain and autonomic response |
| **Physical exam** | Acutely tender, elevated testis; horizontal "bell-clapper" lie; absent cremasteric reflex (most sensitive physical finding); scrotal swelling and erythema develop over time |
| **Prehn sign** | Pain NOT relieved by elevation of scrotum (unlike epididymitis, where elevation may help) |

### Diagnosis

- **Doppler ultrasound:** Enlarged, hypoechoic testis with absent or decreased blood flow; however, preserved flow does NOT exclude torsion (intermittent torsion)
- **Physical examination:** Often diagnostic in classic presentation
- **Do NOT delay treatment for imaging if clinical suspicion is high**

### Management

**EMERGENCY - TIME-CRITICAL:**

| Time Frame | Testicular Salvage Rate |
|------------|------------------------|
| <6 hours from onset | 80-100% |
| 6-12 hours | 50-70% |
| 12-24 hours | 20-50% |
| >24 hours | <10% |

- **Surgical exploration is mandatory** when torsion is suspected (do not wait for imaging if high suspicion)
- **Manual detorsion** may be attempted if surgical delay is anticipated ("open the book" - rotate testis outward/laterally, typically 1.5 turns); this is a temporizing measure only
- **Surgical treatment:** Scrotal exploration; detorsion if testis viable; orchiectomy if nonviable (black/necrotic); **contralateral orchiopexy at same surgery** (prevents future torsion in other testis due to high incidence of bilateral bell-clapper deformity)
- **Neonatal torsion:** Usually extravaginal (torsion of entire spernic cord including tunica vaginalis); typically occurs in utero or perinatally; often presents with firm, painless scrotal mass; salvage rate very low due to delayed recognition; urgent exploration recommended

### Differential Diagnosis

| Condition | Distinguishing Features |
|-----------|------------------------|
| **Epididymitis** | Gradual onset; dysuria; fever; elevated testis not horizontal; preserved cremasteric reflex; increased Doppler flow; leukocyturia |
| **Torsion of appendix testis** (appendix testis) | "Blue dot sign" (necrotic appendix visible through scrotal skin); gradual onset; less severe pain; age 7-14; cremasteric reflex present |
| **Trauma** | History of injury; scrotal hematoma |
| **Hernia** | Palpable inguinal mass; reducible |
| **Idiopathic scrotal edema** | Painless edema of scrotum and penis; self-limited; age 5-11 |

## 9.3 Epididymitis and Orchitis

### Acute Epididymitis

**Etiology varies by age and sexual activity:**
- **Sexually active men <35 years:** *C. trachomatis* and *N. gonorrhoeae* most common
- **Men >35 years and insertive anal sex:** Enteric organisms (*E. coli*, *Pseudomonas*, etc.)
- **Post-procedural/structural abnormality:** Enteric organisms
- **Amiodarone:** Chemical epididymitis (dose-dependent)

**Diagnosis:** Urinalysis (may show pyuria/bacteriuria), urine culture, STI nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs) if sexually active, scrotal ultrasound to exclude torsion if diagnostic uncertainty.

**Treatment:**

| Patient Type | Regimen | Duration |
|-------------|---------|----------|
| **Sexually active <35 years (suspected STI)** | Ceftriaxone 500 mg IM x 1 + doxycycline 100 mg BID | 10 days |
| **Men >35 years or enteric organisms** | Levofloxacin 500 mg daily OR ofloxacin 300 mg BID | 10 days |
| **Supportive care** | Scrotal elevation, ice, NSAIDs, rest | Until symptoms resolve |

**Follow-up:** Re-evaluate at 72 hours; if not improving, re-evaluate diagnosis and consider ultrasound for abscess.

### Orchitis

- **Mumps orchitis:** Most common viral cause; occurs 4-6 days after parotitis; 30% bilateral; may cause testicular atrophy and infertility; prevention via MMR vaccination
- **Bacterial orchitis:** Usually associated with epididymitis (epididymo-orchitis)
- **Viral orchitis:** Mumps, coxsackievirus, EBV, CMV
- **Treatment:** Supportive; NSAIDs, scrotal support, ice; antibiotics only if bacterial cause confirmed

## 9.4 Varicocele

### Overview

Varicocele is dilation of the pampiniform plexus veins in the scrotum (left-sided in 85-95% due to left testicular vein draining into left renal vein at right angle vs. right testicular vein draining directly into IVC).

**Grading:**
- Grade I: Palpable only with Valsalva maneuver
- Grade II: Palpable without Valsalva (standing)
- Grade III: Visible through scrotal skin ("bag of worms" appearance)

### Clinical Significance

| Effect | Notes |
|--------|-------|
| **Infertility** | Most common treatable cause of male infertility; impairs sperm quality via elevated testicular temperature |
| **Testicular atrophy** | Affected testis may be smaller than contralateral |
| **Pain** | Dull, aching pain; worse with prolonged standing or activity; improved with supine position |
| **Hypogonadism** | Large varicoceles may impair Leydig cell function and reduce testosterone |

### Treatment

**Indications for varicocelectomy or embolization:**
- Palpable varicocele with abnormal semen analysis and infertility
- Recurrent or persistent pain (after excluding other causes)
- Testicular growth arrest in adolescents (affected testis >2 mL smaller or 20% volume loss compared to contralateral)
- Symptomatic varicocele with significant bother

**Treatment options:**
- **Microsurgical varicocelectomy (inguinal or subinguinal):** Gold standard; highest success rates; lowest recurrence and complication rates
- **Laparoscopic varicocelectomy:** Good results; risk of arterial injury
- **Radiographic embolization:** Interventional radiology; minimally invasive; good for recurrence after surgery; radiation exposure
- **Retroperitoneal (Palomo):** Higher recurrence and hydrocele rates; less commonly performed

**Outcomes:** Semen parameters improve in 60-80% of men; pregnancy rates 30-50% within 1-2 years after repair.

## 9.5 Hydrocele

### Overview

Hydrocele is a collection of fluid between the parietal and visceral layers of the tunica vaginalis surrounding the testis.

**Types:**
- **Communicating hydrocele:** Patent processus vaginalis allows peritoneal fluid to enter scrotum; common in infants; often resolves spontaneously by age 1-2 years
- **Non-communicating hydrocele:** Acquired; no connection to peritoneum; often idiopathic in adults; may develop after infection, trauma, or as a result of testicular pathology
- **Hydrocele of the cord:** Isolated fluid collection along the spermatic cord

### Evaluation
- Physical examination: Transilluminates (glows red with penlight shining through scrotum)
- Scrotal ultrasound: Confirms diagnosis; rules out underlying testicular pathology
- **Critical:** Evaluate for underlying testicular pathology (tumor, infection) in adult-onset hydrocele

### Treatment
- **Infants:** Observation until age 1-2 years; surgical repair (hydrocelectomy + hernia repair) if persists
- **Adults:** Surgical repair (hydrocelectomy) if symptomatic, cosmetically concerning, or large; aspiration with sclerotherapy is an alternative but higher recurrence rate

# Chapter 10: Male Infertility

---

## 10.1 Clinical Overview

Infertility affects approximately 15% of couples globally. Male factor infertility contributes to approximately 40-50% of infertility cases, either as sole cause or combined with female factors. Male infertility is defined as the inability of a couple to achieve pregnancy after 12 months of unprotected intercourse (or 6 months if female partner >35 years old).

## 10.2 Evaluation

### 10.2.1 Initial Assessment

**Comprehensive History:**

| Category | Key Questions |
|----------|--------------|
| **Medical** | Chronic illnesses (diabetes, thyroid, celiac, CRF, liver disease), infections (mumps orchitis, STIs), genetic conditions |
| **Surgical** | Hernia repair, orchiopexy, varicocelectomy, vasectomy, pelvic/abdominal surgery, prostate surgery |
| **Developmental** | Cryptorchidism history, delayed puberty, testicular trauma/torsion |
| **Sexual** | Erectile function, ejaculatory function, timing/frequency of intercourse, use of lubricants (many are spermicidal) |
| **Family** | Infertility, genetic conditions, birth defects |
| **Environmental/Occupational** | Heat exposure (saunas, hot tubs, occupational), pesticides, solvents, heavy metals, radiation |
| **Medications/Substances** | Testosterone, anabolic steroids, finasteride, SSRIs, alcohol, tobacco, marijuana, opioids |

**Physical Examination:**
- Testicular volume: Use Prader orchidometer (normal adult >=15 mL, or length >=4 cm)
- Testicular consistency: Normal testes are firm and resilient; soft testes suggest impaired spermatogenesis
- Varicocele: Palpate in standing and supine positions, with and without Valsalva
- Secondary sexual characteristics: Body hair distribution, gynecomastia, facial hair
- Penile examination: Peyronie plaques, hypospadias, phimosis
- Digital rectal exam if indicated
- Assess for signs of hypogonadism

### 10.2.2 Semen Analysis (WHO 6th Edition, 2021) [^421^]

**Collection requirements:**
- 2-7 days of sexual abstinence before collection
- Two separate samples collected 2-3 weeks apart (high variability between samples)
- Complete collection in clean container; note any spillage
- Deliver to lab within 30-60 minutes; keep at body temperature during transport

**WHO 2021 Lower Reference Limits (5th percentile):**

| Parameter | Lower Reference Limit |
|-----------|----------------------|
| **Semen volume** | >=1.4 mL |
| **Sperm concentration** | >=16 million/mL |
| **Total sperm count** | >=39 million per ejaculate |
| **Total motility** (progressive + non-progressive) | >=42% |
| **Progressive motility** | >=30% |
| **Normal morphology** (strict/Kruger criteria) | >=4% |
| **Vitality** (live sperm) | >=54% |
| **pH** | >=7.2 |

**Additional tests (when indicated):**
- **Post-ejaculate urinalysis:** To diagnose retrograde ejaculation (sperm present in urine)
- **Antisperm antibodies:** Post-vasectomy, trauma, infection, varicocele; sperm agglutination on analysis
- **Semen culture:** If infection suspected
- **Fructose:** Absent fructose suggests ejaculatory duct obstruction or seminal vesicle agenesis

### 10.2.3 Hormonal Testing

| Hormone | Interpretation | Clinical Significance |
|---------|---------------|---------------------|
| **FSH** | Elevated | Primary testicular failure (Sertoli cell damage) |
| **FSH** | Low/normal | Secondary (hypogonadotropic) hypogonadism |
| **LH + Testosterone** | Both low | Hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (hypothalamic/pituitary) |
| **LH high + Testosterone low** | Hypergonadotropic hypogonadism (primary testicular failure) |
| **Prolactin** | Elevated | Prolactinoma; suppresses GnRH; check if secondary hypogonadism |
| **Estradiol** | Elevated | Obesity (peripheral aromatization); may suppress HPG axis |

**Indications for hormonal testing:**
- Abnormal semen analysis (especially severe oligospermia or azoospermia)
- Signs of hypogonadism
- Suspected endocrine disorder
- Prior to initiating hormonal therapy for fertility

### 10.2.4 Genetic Testing

| Test | Indication | What It Detects |
|------|-----------|----------------|
| **Karyotype** | Severe oligospermia (<5 million/mL) or non-obstructive azoospermia | Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY); sex chromosome abnormalities |
| **Y-chromosome microdeletion** | Severe oligospermia or NOA | Microdeletions in AZF (azoospermia factor) regions a-d; guides TESE prognosis |
| **CFTR mutation testing** | Azoospermia with absent vas deferens (CBAVD) | Cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator mutations |
| **Karyotype + microarray** | Recurrent pregnancy loss | Balanced translocations |

### 10.2.5 Additional Testing

| Test | Indication |
|------|-----------|
| **Scrotal ultrasound** | Varicocele, testicular masses, microlithiasis, small testes, absence of vas deferens |
| **Transrectal ultrasound (TRUS)** | Ejaculatory duct obstruction (dilated seminal vesicles), prostate pathology |
| **Testicular biopsy** | Part of TESE procedure for NOA; diagnostic if endocrine workup unrevealing |
| **MRI sella** | Elevated prolactin or other pituitary signs |

## 10.3 Causes and Treatment

### Classification by Anatomic/Physiologic Level

| Category | Causes | Treatment |
|----------|--------|-----------|
| **Pretesticular (endocrine/hypothalamic-pituitary)** | Hypogonadotropic hypogonadism, hyperprolactinemia, thyroid disease, hemochromatosis, Cushing syndrome, exogenous steroids | Hormonal therapy (hCG + FSH or pulsatile GnRH); treat underlying endocrinopathy |
| **Testicular** | Varicocele, cryptorchidism, orchitis, testicular cancer, genetic (Klinefelter, Y-microdeletion), chemotherapy/radiation, anabolic steroid use | Varicocelectomy; sperm retrieval + ART; avoid gonadotoxins |
| **Post-testicular (obstructive)** | Vasectomy, CBAVD, ejaculatory duct obstruction, epididymal obstruction (infection/inflammation) | Vasectomy reversal; transurethral resection of ejaculatory ducts; epididymovasostomy; sperm retrieval + ICSI |
| **Sexual dysfunction** | ED, retrograde ejaculation, anejaculation, premature ejaculation | Treat underlying sexual dysfunction; IUI if retrograde ejaculation |
| **Idiopathic** | No identifiable cause (~25-30%) | Empiric treatment or ART |

### 10.3.1 Specific Treatment Approaches

**Varicocele Treatment:**
- Indication: Palpable varicocele + abnormal semen analysis + infertility OR testicular growth arrest in adolescent
- Microsurgical varicocelectomy: Gold standard; semen parameters improve in 60-80%; natural pregnancy rates 30-50%
- Consider repair even if only mildly abnormal SA if female partner has normal fertility

**Hypogonadotropic Hypogonadism:**
- hCG 1,000-2,000 IU 2-3x/week (stimulates Leydig cells)
- Add FSH or recombinant FSH 75-150 IU 3x/week if spermatogenesis not achieved in 6 months (stimulates Sertoli cells)
- Response may take 6-12 months or longer
- If treatment started before puberty, higher doses and longer time needed

**Ejaculatory Duct Obstruction:**
- TRUS-guided transurethral resection of ejaculatory ducts (TURED)
- Postoperative semen analysis to confirm patency

**Azoospermia Workup and Treatment:**

| Type | Diagnosis | Treatment |
|------|-----------|-----------|
| **Obstructive azoospermia (OA)** | Normal FSH, normal testicular volume, normal testosterone; dilated epididymis or absent vas; previous vasectomy | Vasectomy reversal; epididymovasostomy; MESA/TESE + ICSI |
| **Non-obstructive azoospermia (NOA)** | Elevated FSH, small testes, low/normal testosterone; testicular failure | Micro-TESE (microsurgical testicular sperm extraction); sperm found in 40-60% of cases; ICSI required |

### 10.3.2 Assisted Reproductive Technology (ART)

| Procedure | Indication | Description |
|-----------|-----------|-------------|
| **Intrauterine insemination (IUI)** | Mild male factor (mild oligospermia, motility issues); cervical factor; unexplained infertility | Washed, concentrated sperm placed directly into uterus at time of ovulation |
| **In-vitro fertilization (IVF)** | Moderate male factor; tubal factor; endometriosis; unexplained infertility | Ovarian stimulation, egg retrieval, fertilization in laboratory, embryo transfer |
| **Intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI)** | Severe male factor (severe oligospermia, teratozoospermia); prior fertilization failure; sperm retrieved surgically | Single sperm injected directly into oocyte cytoplasm; enables fertilization with very low sperm counts |
| **Testicular sperm extraction (TESE)** | Azoospermia | Open surgical sperm retrieval from testis |
| **Micro-TESE** | Non-obstructive azoospermia | Microsurgical dissection under operating microscope to identify sperm-producing tubules |
| **Microsurgical epididymal sperm aspiration (MESA)** | Obstructive azoospermia | Sperm aspirated from epididymis under microsurgical visualization |
| **Percutaneous epididymal sperm aspiration (PESA)** | Obstructive azoospermia | Needle aspiration of epididymis (less invasive than MESA) |

### 10.3.3 Empiric Medical Therapies

| Therapy | Evidence | Notes |
|---------|----------|-------|
| **Clomiphene citrate** | Moderate | For men with low-normal testosterone and idiopathic oligospermia; off-label |
| **Antioxidants** (CoQ10, vitamin E, zinc, folic acid, L-carnitine, selenium) | Limited/uncertain | May improve sperm parameters in some men; generally safe |
| **Human growth hormone** | Limited | Not routinely recommended |
| **Anti-estrogens** (tamoxifen) | Limited | May improve sperm count in some men with elevated estradiol |

## 10.4 Counseling and Prevention

### General Recommendations to Optimize Fertility

| Recommendation | Rationale |
|---------------|-----------|
| Maintain healthy weight (BMI 18.5-25) | Obesity impairs sperm quality via hormonal and thermal mechanisms |
| Avoid tobacco | Reduces sperm count, motility, and morphology |
| Limit alcohol | Heavy use reduces testosterone and sperm quality |
| Avoid recreational drugs (marijuana, cocaine, anabolic steroids) | All impair spermatogenesis; anabolic steroids cause profound suppression |
| Avoid excessive heat (saunas, hot tubs, tight underwear) | Elevated scrotal temperature impairs sperm production |
| Manage medical conditions (diabetes, thyroid, varicocele) | Underlying conditions can impair fertility |
| Review medications with physician | Some medications impair fertility (testosterone, finasteride, SSRIs, calcium channel blockers) |
| Consider antioxidant supplementation | May provide modest benefit; generally safe |
| Regular exercise | Improves overall health and may improve sperm quality |

### When to Refer

- Azoospermia or severe oligospermia (<5 million/mL)
- Abnormal hormonal profile
- Suspected obstructive cause
- Need for surgical intervention (varicocelectomy, vasectomy reversal)
- Need for assisted reproductive technology
- Complex genetic findings
- Couple infertility >1 year with identified male factor

## Women's Health Emergencies

| Condition | Red Flags | Immediate Action |
|-----------|-----------|-----------------|
| **Ectopic pregnancy** | Amenorrhea + abdominal pain + vaginal bleeding + hemodynamic instability; shoulder pain (referred); syncope | IV access, type & screen, quantitative hCG, stat pelvic ultrasound; surgical/emergency OB consult |
| **Ovarian torsion** | Sudden severe unilateral pelvic pain with nausea/vomiting; adnexal mass | Emergent surgical consult; ultrasound with Doppler; do not delay surgical exploration |
| **Ruptured hemorrhagic cyst/ectopic** | Acute pelvic pain with hemodynamic instability; syncope; acute abdomen | Fluid resuscitation; emergent surgical management |
| **Septic abortion/PID with sepsis** | Fever >38.5 C, severe pelvic pain, purulent discharge, tachycardia, hypotension | Broad-spectrum IV antibiotics; hospital admission; source control |
| **Eclampsia** | Seizure in pregnant/postpartum woman with preeclampsia | Airway protection; magnesium sulfate 4-6 g IV load; BP control; emergent delivery |
| **HELLP syndrome** | RUQ/epigastric pain, thrombocytopenia, elevated LFTs in pregnancy | Hospital admission; magnesium sulfate; BP control; emergent delivery if >=34 weeks or deteriorating |
| **Postpartum hemorrhage** | Blood loss >500 mL (vaginal) or >1000 mL (cesarean); signs of shock | Uterine massage; uterotonics (oxytocin, methylergonovine, carboprost, misoprostol); surgical intervention |
| **Postpartum psychosis** | Delusions, hallucinations, extreme confusion, homicidal/suicidal ideation within first 2 weeks postpartum | Emergency psychiatric hospitalization; medical emergency |

## Men's Health Emergencies

| Condition | Red Flags | Immediate Action |
|-----------|-----------|-----------------|
| **Testicular torsion** | Acute severe scrotal pain, absent cremasteric reflex, elevated/h horizontally lying testis, nausea/vomiting | Emergent urology consult; surgical exploration within 6 hours for best salvage |
| **Acute urinary retention** | Painful bladder distension, inability to void, suprapubic tenderness | Bladder catheterization (urethral or suprapubic); evaluate cause; alpha-blocker if BPH |
| **Fournier gangrene** | Perineal/genital pain, swelling, crepitus, fever, necrosis, sepsis; disproportionate pain | Emergency surgical debridement; broad-spectrum IV antibiotics; ICU admission; high mortality |
| **Priapism** | Painful erection >4 hours duration; ischemic (low-flow) vs. non-ischemic (high-flow) | Ischemic priapism: Emergency - corpus cavernosum aspiration + irrigation with phenylephrine; urology consult |
| **Acute bacterial prostatitis with sepsis** | Fever, rigors, dysuria, perineal pain, hypotension, confusion | IV antibiotics (ceftriaxone + fluoroquinolone); hospital admission; suprapubic catheter if retention |
| **Paraphimosis** | Retracted foreskin proximal to glans causing painful glans swelling, edema, discoloration | Manual reduction (compress glans to reduce edema, then advance foreskin); dorsal slit or circumcision if unable |
| **Penile fracture** | Sudden cracking/popping sound during intercourse, immediate detumescence, swelling, ecchymosis ("eggplant deformity"), pain | Surgical repair within 24 hours; urology emergency |

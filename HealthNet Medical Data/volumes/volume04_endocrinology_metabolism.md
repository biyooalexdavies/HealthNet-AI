# Chapter 1: Diabetes Mellitus — Type 1

## 1.1 Overview

Type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) is an autoimmune disease characterized by T-cell-mediated destruction of pancreatic beta cells, resulting in absolute insulin deficiency. It accounts for approximately 5-10% of all diabetes cases worldwide [^1^]. The pathophysiology involves a complex interplay between genetic susceptibility (HLA-DR3/DR4, DQ2/DQ8 haplotypes) and environmental triggers (viral infections, dietary factors, gut microbiome alterations) in predisposed individuals.

**Key pathophysiologic features:**
- Progressive loss of insulin-producing beta cells
- Presence of pancreatic autoantibodies months to years before clinical onset
- Complete dependence on exogenous insulin for survival
- Risk of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) if insulin is withheld

**Epidemiology:**
- Incidence varies by geography: highest in Finland and Sardinia; lowest in Asia
- Bimodal age distribution: first peak at ages 10-14; second peak in adults aged 30-50 (LADA)
- Latent Autoimmune Diabetes in Adults (LADA): slower onset; may initially be misclassified as T2DM; positive GAD65 antibodies distinguish from T2DM

---

## 1.2 Clinical Presentation

### Classic Triad
- **Polyuria:** Osmotic diuresis from glucosuria
- **Polydipsia:** Compensatory response to hyperosmolality
- **Unintentional weight loss:** Catabolic state from insulin deficiency

### Additional Symptoms
- Polyphagia (often paradoxically present with weight loss)
- Fatigue, weakness
- Blurred vision (osmotic lens changes)
- Recurrent infections (candidal skin infections, urinary tract infections)
- Nausea, abdominal pain (especially if DKA developing)

### Physical Examination Findings
- Signs of dehydration (dry mucous membranes, reduced skin turgor, orthostatic hypotension)
- Kussmaul respirations (deep, rapid breathing) if DKA present
- Fruity/acetone breath odor in DKA
- Signs of insulin injection sites (lipohypertrophy if site rotation inadequate)

---

## 1.3 Diagnosis

### Diagnostic Criteria [^1^]
Any ONE of the following is diagnostic (confirm on repeat testing unless unequivocal hyperglycemia with classic symptoms present):

| Test | Diagnostic Threshold |
|------|---------------------|
| HbA1c | >=6.5% (48 mmol/mol) |
| Fasting plasma glucose | >=126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L) after >=8 hours |
| 2-hour OGTT (75g glucose) | >=200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) |
| Random plasma glucose | >=200 mg/dL with classic symptoms |

### Differentiating T1DM from T2DM

| Feature | Type 1 DM | Type 2 DM |
|---------|-----------|-----------|
| Age of onset | Any; peaks in childhood and ~30-50 | Typically >40 (increasingly younger) |
| BMI | Normal or underweight | Overweight or obese |
| Onset | Acute (days to weeks) | Insidious (months to years) |
| Ketosis/DKA | Common at presentation | Uncommon (except with severe stress) |
| C-peptide | Low or absent | Normal or elevated |
| Autoantibodies | Positive (GAD65, IA-2, ZnT8) | Negative |
| Insulin requirement | Absolute (required for survival) | Relative (may not need initially) |
| Family history | Less common | Common |

### Confirmatory Testing for T1DM
- **C-peptide:** Low or undetectable fasting C-peptide supports T1DM; may be preserved early in disease
- **Diabetes-associated autoantibodies:**
  - GAD65 (anti-glutamic acid decarboxylase): Most sensitive; present in 70-80%
  - IA-2 (anti-tyrosine phosphatase): Present in ~60%
  - ZnT8 (anti-zinc transporter 8): Present in 60-80%; most specific for T1DM
  - Insulin autoantibodies (IAA): More common in children; less specific

> **Clinical Pearl:** Testing positive for two or more autoantibodies defines Stage 1 T1DM (asymptomatic autoimmunity). Progression to symptomatic disease (Stage 3) is highly likely over months to years.

## 1.4 Insulin Therapy

### Basal-Bolus Regimen (Standard of Care)

All patients with T1DM require basal (background) insulin and bolus (mealtime/prandial) insulin [^1^].

#### Basal Insulins (Long-Acting)
| Insulin | Onset | Peak | Duration | Dosing |
|---------|-------|------|----------|--------|
| Insulin glargine (U-100, U-300) | 1-2 hours | Minimal | 24 hours | Once daily |
| Insulin detemir | 1-2 hours | Minimal | 12-24 hours | Once or twice daily |
| Insulin degludec (U-100, U-200) | 30-90 minutes | Minimal | >42 hours | Once daily (flexible timing) |
| NPH | 1-2 hours | 4-8 hours | 12-18 hours | Twice daily |

#### Bolus Insulins (Rapid-Acting)
| Insulin | Onset | Peak | Duration | Notes |
|---------|-------|------|----------|-------|
| Insulin lispro | 15 minutes | 30-90 min | 3-5 hours | First rapid analog |
| Insulin aspart | 10-20 minutes | 1-3 hours | 3-5 hours | |
| Insulin glulisine | 15 minutes | 1-2 hours | 3-5 hours | |
| Faster aspart | 2.5 minutes | 1-2 hours | 3-5 hours | Faster onset |
| Ultra-rapid lispro | 1 minute | Similar | Similar | Fastest onset |
| Regular insulin (R) | 30-60 minutes | 2-4 hours | 5-8 hours | Less expensive; used IV |

### Starting Insulin Doses
- **Total daily insulin (TDI):** 0.4-0.6 units/kg/day for adults
- **Adolescents during puberty:** May require 0.8-1.0 units/kg/day (insulin resistance of puberty)
- **Basal:Bolus ratio:** Typically 40-50% basal, 50-60% bolus
- **Insulin-to-carbohydrate ratio (ICR):** Typically 1 unit per 10-15g carbohydrate (individualized)
- **Correction factor (insulin sensitivity factor):** Typically 1 unit lowers glucose 50 mg/dL (individualized)

### Insulin Pump Therapy (CSII) [^2^]
Continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion (CSII) offers advantages over multiple daily injections:
- Programmable basal rates (multiple profiles for exercise, illness)
- Precise bolus dosing (to 0.025-0.05 unit increments)
- Integration with CGM for sensor-augmented pump therapy
- **2024 ADA recommendation:** CSII and/or automated insulin delivery should be offered early, even at time of diagnosis (Grade C evidence) [^2^]

## 1.5 Continuous Glucose Monitoring (CGM)

CGM has transformed T1DM management. The 2024 ADA Standards of Care strongly endorse CGM for all T1DM patients capable of using devices safely [^2^].

### CGM Systems
| Type | Examples | Data Delivery | Key Features |
|------|----------|--------------|--------------|
| rtCGM (real-time) | Dexcom G6/G7, Medtronic Guardian | Continuous transmission | Alerts for highs/lows; predictive alerts |
| isCGM (intermittently scanned) | FreeStyle Libre 2/3 | Scan to read | Smaller size; Libre 2/3 has optional alarms |

### CGM Metrics (2024 ADA Targets) [^2^]
| Metric | Target Range | Goal |
|--------|-------------|------|
| Time in Range (TIR) | 70-180 mg/dL | >70% of time |
| Time below Range (TBR) | <70 mg/dL | <4% of time |
| Time below Range Level 2 | <54 mg/dL | <1% of time |
| Time above Range (TAR) | >180 mg/dL | <25% of time |
| Time above Range Level 2 | >250 mg/dL | <5% of time |
| Glycemic Variability (CV) | — | <36% |
| GMI (Glucose Management Indicator) | — | Target individualized |

> **Clinical Pearl:** The Ambulatory Glucose Profile (AGP) report provides a standardized "picture" of glucose patterns. Review the AGP report with patients to identify patterns: consistent fasting highs (adjust basal), post-meal spikes (adjust ICR), nocturnal lows (reduce overnight basal).

## 1.6 Automated Insulin Delivery (AID) Systems [^2^]

Hybrid closed-loop systems (artificial pancreas) combine CGM, insulin pump, and control algorithm to automatically adjust insulin delivery.

**2024 ADA recommendation (Grade A):** AID should be offered to all youth and adults with T1DM who can use the device safely.

### Benefits of AID
- Increased Time in Range (TIR)
- Reduced hypoglycemia (especially overnight)
- Reduced glycemic variability
- Reduced cognitive burden of diabetes management
- Improved sleep quality

## 1.7 Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA) — EMERGENCY PROTOCOL

### Definition and Severity Classification [^3^]

| Severity | pH | Bicarbonate (mEq/L) | beta-hydroxybutyrate | Mental Status |
|----------|-----|---------------------|----------------------|---------------|
| Mild | 7.25-7.30 | 15-18 | <=6 mmol/L | Alert |
| Moderate | 7.00-7.24 | 10 to <15 | <=6 mmol/L | Alert/drowsy |
| Severe | <7.00 | <10 | >6 mmol/L | Stupor/coma |

### EMERGENCY — DKA Management Protocol [^3^][^4^]

> **RED FLAG:** DKA is a medical emergency requiring hospital admission. Mortality is 0.5-5% even with treatment. Cerebral edema is the leading cause of death in children.

#### Step 1: Fluid Resuscitation
- **0.9% saline:** 500-1000 mL/hour for first 2-4 hours (if no cardiac/renal compromise)
- **Transition to 0.45% saline** when hemodynamically stable, if serum sodium is high/normal
- **Total fluid deficit:** Typically 3-6 liters in adults

#### Step 2: Potassium Assessment (CRITICAL — BEFORE INSULIN)
- **If K <3.3 mEq/L:** HOLD insulin. Give KCl 20-40 mEq/hour until K >3.3
- **If K 3.3-5.3 mEq/L:** Give KCl 20-40 mEq/L in IV fluids
- **If K >5.3 mEq/L:** Hold potassium; check every 2 hours

#### Step 3: Insulin Therapy
- **IV regular insulin:** 0.1 units/kg/hour continuous infusion (or 0.14 units/kg/hour)
- **Alternative:** 0.1 units/kg IV bolus, then 0.1 units/kg/hour
- **Subcutaneous option:** Mild/moderate uncomplicated DKA may be treated with SC rapid-acting insulin q1-2h (emerging evidence, clinical judgment required)

#### Step 4: Glucose Monitoring
- Check capillary glucose every 1-2 hours
- **When glucose <200-250 mg/dL:** Add D5W (dextrose 5%) to IV fluids to permit continued insulin administration until ketosis resolves
- Continue insulin at reduced dose (0.02-0.05 units/kg/hour) until DKA resolves

#### Step 5: DKA Resolution Criteria [^3^]
- Blood beta-hydroxybutyrate <0.6 mmol/L **AND**
- Venous pH >=7.3 **AND**
- Bicarbonate >=18 mEq/L **AND**
- Anion gap closed (<=12)

#### Step 6: Transition to Subcutaneous Insulin
- Give first dose of basal (SC) insulin 2-4 hours **before** stopping IV insulin
- This overlap prevents rebound hyperglycemia and ketosis
- Continue basal insulin even when NPO (never omit basal insulin)

#### Monitoring During DKA Treatment
| Parameter | Frequency |
|-----------|-----------|
| Capillary glucose | Every 1-2 hours |
| Electrolytes (Na, K, Cl, CO2) | Every 2-4 hours |
| Venous blood gas (pH, bicarbonate) | Every 4 hours |
| Beta-hydroxybutyrate | Every 4 hours |
| Serum creatinine, BUN | Every 4 hours |
| Urine output | Hourly (Foley catheter) |
| ECG monitoring | Continuous if K abnormal |

#### Special Considerations
- **Bicarbonate therapy:** NOT routinely recommended. Consider only if pH <6.9 with hemodynamic compromise — give 100 mmol in 400 mL sterile water with 20 mEq KCl over 2 hours
- **Phosphate:** Routine replacement not recommended; consider if <1.0 mg/dL or cardiac/respiratory dysfunction
- **Cerebral edema:** Most common in children/adolescents; rare >28 years. Signs: headache, altered mental status, bradycardia, hypertension. Treat with mannitol 1 g/kg or hypertonic saline (3%) 5-10 mL/kg.
- **Euglycemic DKA:** Glucose <250 mg/dL with ketosis/acidosis; associated with SGLT2 inhibitor use, pregnancy, fasting, alcohol use

## 1.8 Hypoglycemia

### Classification [^1^]

| Level | Glucose | Clinical Significance |
|-------|---------|----------------------|
| Level 1 | <70 mg/dL | Threshold for counterregulatory response; warning |
| Level 2 | <54 mg/dL | Neuroglycopenic threshold; requires immediate action |
| Level 3 | Any value | Severe event requiring third-party assistance |

### Treatment — Rule of 15
1. Consume 15g fast-acting carbohydrate (glucose tablets, juice, regular soda, honey)
2. Recheck glucose in 15 minutes
3. Repeat if still <70 mg/dL
4. Once glucose normalized, eat a snack with protein/fat to prevent recurrence

### Severe Hypoglycemia Treatment
- **Glucagon administration:**
  - **Nasal glucagon (Baqsimi):** 3 mg single dose into one nostril — easiest for bystanders
  - **Autoinjector (Gvoke):** 0.5 mg (children) or 1 mg (adults) IM/subQ
  - **Traditional glucagon kit:** 1 mg IM/subQ (requires reconstitution)
- Turn patient on side (prevent aspiration)
- Call emergency services if no response within 15 minutes or if glucagon unavailable
- Recheck glucose every 15 minutes; give IV dextrose if available in clinical setting

### Risk Factors for Hypoglycemia
- Excess insulin dosing, missed or delayed meals
- Exercise (especially unplanned or prolonged)
- Alcohol consumption (blocks hepatic gluconeogenesis)
- Renal impairment (reduces insulin clearance)
- Prior episode of severe hypoglycemia (6- to 12-fold increased risk of recurrence)
- Hypoglycemia unawareness (counterregulatory failure from recurrent lows)

### Hypoglycemia Unawareness
- Defined as absence of warning symptoms before neuroglycopenia
- Caused by recurrent hypoglycemia blunting counterregulatory responses
- **Management:** Strict avoidance of hypoglycemia for 2-3 weeks (relax glycemic targets, increase CGM alerts) often restores awareness
- CGM with low glucose alerts is essential

## 1.9 Sick Day Rules (Patient Education)

1. **NEVER stop basal insulin** — insulin requirements often increase during illness
2. Check blood glucose every 2-4 hours (or monitor CGM closely)
3. Check blood/urine ketones when glucose >250 mg/dL or if ill
4. Drink plenty of fluids (sugar-free if glucose >180 mg/dL; sugary if glucose <100 mg/dL)
5. Continue carbohydrate intake (even if not eating regular meals — 15g carbs every 3-4 hours)
6. Contact healthcare provider if: moderate/large ketones, vomiting >4 hours, unable to keep fluids down, glucose persistently >300 mg/dL, signs of DKA
7. Have emergency glucagon available

## 1.10 Special Populations

### Pediatric T1DM
- **Glycemic targets:** HbA1c <7.0% (individualize based on age, hypoglycemia risk)
- **CGM:** Strongly recommended — rtCGM (Grade A), isCGM (Grade E) [^2^]
- **AID:** Recommended (Grade A)
- **Insulin requirements:** Often 0.8-1.0 units/kg/day during puberty (insulin resistance)
- **Psychosocial support:** Essential — depression, anxiety, eating disorders more common
- **Transition to adult care:** Planned, structured process beginning in mid-adolescence

### Pregnancy
- **Tight glycemic targets:** Fasting <95 mg/dL; 1-hour postprandial <140 mg/dL; 2-hour <120 mg/dL
- **Insulin** is the only approved pharmacotherapy for T1DM in pregnancy
- **CGM use:** Recommended; reduces risk of large-for-gestational-age infants
- **Preconception counseling:** Essential — optimize glucose control (HbA1c <6.5% if achievable safely) before conception; start folic acid 5 mg daily

### Older Adults
- **Relaxed targets:** HbA1c <8.0-8.5% may be appropriate if hypoglycemia risk, significant comorbidities, or limited life expectancy
- **Primary goal:** Hypoglycemia prevention
- **Technology:** CGM with alerts can reduce hypoglycemia burden
- **Simplify regimen:** Reduce injection complexity; fixed-dose basal insulin may be appropriate

## 1.12 When to Seek Emergency Care

- Signs of DKA: vomiting, abdominal pain, rapid/deep breathing, fruity breath, confusion
- Severe hypoglycemia with loss of consciousness or seizure
- Persistent glucose >400 mg/dL despite correction doses
- Moderate to large ketones with illness
- Signs of infection (fever, confusion) with poor glucose control

# Chapter 2: Diabetes Mellitus — Type 2

## 2.1 Overview

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a progressive metabolic disorder characterized by insulin resistance and relative insulin deficiency. It represents 90-95% of all diabetes cases and is closely linked to obesity, sedentary lifestyle, genetic predisposition, and aging [^5^]. The natural history involves progressive beta-cell dysfunction over time, necessitating escalation of therapy.

**Pathophysiologic defects (the "Ominous Octet"):**
1. Decreased incretin effect (GLP-1, GIP)
2. Increased lipolysis and reduced adipokines
3. Increased hepatic glucose production
4. Neurotransmitter dysfunction (brain insulin resistance)
5. Increased glucose reabsorption by kidneys (SGLT2)
6. Beta-cell dysfunction (progressive)
7. Alpha-cell dysfunction (inappropriate glucagon secretion)
8. Insulin resistance in muscle and liver

**Epidemiology:**
- Global prevalence: ~10% of adults; increasing dramatically
- Higher prevalence in certain ethnic groups (South Asian, Hispanic/Latino, African American, Native American, Pacific Islander)
- Strong heritability; polygenic inheritance with environmental interaction

---

## 2.2 Diagnosis

### Diagnostic Criteria (ADA 2024) [^5^]
Any ONE of the following (confirm on repeat testing unless unequivocal hyperglycemia with classic symptoms):

| Test | Diagnostic Threshold | Prediabetes Range |
|------|---------------------|-------------------|
| HbA1c | >=6.5% (48 mmol/mol) | 5.7-6.4% |
| Fasting plasma glucose | >=126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L) | 100-125 mg/dL |
| 2-hour OGTT (75g) | >=200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) | 140-199 mg/dL |
| Random glucose | >=200 mg/dL with symptoms | — |

### Screening Recommendations [^5^]
- **All adults >=35 years:** Screen every 3 years (USPSTF/ADA)
- **Adults <35 years:** Screen if overweight/obese (BMI >=25; >=23 in Asian Americans) AND one or more risk factors:
  - First-degree relative with diabetes
  - High-risk race/ethnicity
  - History of gestational diabetes (GDM)
  - Physical inactivity
  - Hypertension (>=130/80)
  - Dyslipidemia (HDL <35 mg/dL or TG >=250 mg/dL)
  - Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS)
  - History of cardiovascular disease
- **Women with prior GDM:** Screen every 3 years lifelong
- **Annual screening:** For prediabetes

## 2.3 Glycemic Targets (2022 ADA/EASD Consensus) [^5^][^6^]

| Patient Category | HbA1c Target | Fasting Glucose | Postprandial Glucose |
|------------------|-------------|-----------------|---------------------|
| Most non-pregnant adults | <7% (53 mmol/mol) | 80-130 mg/dL | <180 mg/dL at 1-2h |
| Stringent (young, new diagnosis, long life expectancy) | <6.5% if achievable safely | Individualized | Individualized |
| Relaxed (older, comorbidities, hypoglycemia risk, limited life expectancy) | <8.0-8.5% | Higher targets acceptable | Higher targets acceptable |

**Key principle:** Targets must be individualized based on patient characteristics, preferences, comorbidities, hypoglycemia risk, and life expectancy.

---

## 2.4 Management — Lifestyle Foundation

All patients with T2DM should receive comprehensive diabetes self-management education and support (DSMES) [^6^].

### Medical Nutrition Therapy
- No single "diabetic diet" — individualized approach
- Emphasize non-starchy vegetables, whole grains, lean protein, healthy fats
- Limit refined carbohydrates, sugar-sweetened beverages, saturated/trans fats
- Mediterranean, DASH, and low-carbohydrate diets have evidence for glycemic improvement
- Weight loss of 5-10% improves glycemic control, blood pressure, and lipids

### Physical Activity
- At least 150 minutes/week moderate-intensity aerobic activity (spread over >=3 days)
- Resistance training 2-3 times per week
- Reduce sedentary time (prolonged sitting)
- Supervised exercise program for those with complications

### Weight Management
- Modest weight loss (5-10%) improves HbA1c, BP, lipids
- Greater weight loss (10-15%) may achieve diabetes remission (DiRECT trial)
- Consider anti-obesity medications or metabolic/bariatric surgery for eligible patients

## 2.5 Pharmacologic Therapy

### First-Line: Metformin [^5^][^6^]

Metformin remains the cornerstone of initial pharmacotherapy for T2DM unless contraindicated or not tolerated.

| Parameter | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| **Mechanism** | Decreases hepatic glucose production; improves peripheral insulin sensitivity |
| **HbA1c reduction** | 1.0-1.5% |
| **Weight effect** | Neutral to slight loss |
| **Hypoglycemia risk** | Low (when used as monotherapy) |
| **Starting dose** | 500 mg with largest meal, daily or BID |
| **Titration** | Increase by 500 mg weekly to target 1500-2000 mg/day |
| **Maximum dose** | 2550 mg/day (immediate-release); 2000 mg/day (extended-release) |
| **Contraindications** | eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73m2 (discontinue); hold if eGFR 30-45 |
| **Hold before contrast** | If eGFR <60, hold 48 hours before and after iodinated contrast |
| **Common side effects** | GI upset (nausea, diarrhea, metallic taste) — improve with slow titration, extended-release formulation, taking with food |
| **Rare risk** | Lactic acidosis (very rare; contraindications minimize risk) |
| **Benefits** | Cardiovascular safety established (UKPDS); low cost; extensive experience |

### Escalation Algorithm — ADD Therapy Based on Comorbidities [^6^]

> **CRITICAL DECISION POINT:** After metformin (or at diagnosis if HbA1c >=1.5% above target), select add-on therapy based on the patient's comorbidity profile.

#### With ASCVD or High ASCVD Risk
- **GLP-1 RA with proven CV benefit:** Liraglutide (LEADER trial), semaglutide (SUSTAIN-6), dulaglutide (REWIND)
- **SGLT2-i with proven CV benefit:** Empagliflozin (EMPA-REG), canagliflozin (CANVAS), dapagliflozin (DECLARE)
- Can use both classes together for additive benefit

#### With Heart Failure (especially HFrEF)
- **SGLT2-i (dapagliflozin, empagliflozin):** Class I indication for HF — reduces hospitalization and cardiovascular death
- SGLT2-i should be continued even when eGFR falls below glucose-lowering thresholds

#### With Chronic Kidney Disease (UACR >=30 mg/g or eGFR decline)
- **SGLT2-i:** First-line for kidney protection (CREDENCE, DAPA-CKD, EMPA-KIDNEY trials)
- **GLP-1 RA:** Add if additional glycemic lowering needed or if SGLT2-i not tolerated
- **Finerenone:** Nonsteroidal MRA for T2DM with CKD and albuminuria (FIDELIO-DKD)

#### With Obesity / Need for Weight Loss
- **GLP-1 RA:** Semaglutide (significant weight loss)
- **GIP/GLP-1 dual agonist:** Tirzepatide (greatest HbA1c lowering and weight loss)
- **SGLT2-i:** Modest weight benefit (~2-4 kg)

#### With Predominant Hyperglycemia (HbA1c >10% or symptomatic)
- Consider early insulin therapy
- If HbA1c >=1.5% above target at diagnosis, consider dual therapy from start

- Active pancreatitis history
- Pregnancy/breastfeeding

### Tirzepatide (Mounjaro for T2DM; Zepbound for Obesity)
- **Dosing:** 2.5 mg weekly initially, titrate every 4 weeks to 5, 7.5, 10, 12.5, 15 mg
- **SURPASS program:** HbA1c reduction up to 2.4%; weight loss up to 12-15 kg at maximum dose
- **SURMOUNT program (obesity):** Weight loss up to 20-22% at 15 mg
- **SURMOUNT-MC trial:** Cardiovascular outcomes data ongoing

### Insulin Initiation in T2DM [^6^]

**When to initiate insulin:**
- HbA1c persistently >10% despite dual/triple oral therapy
- Symptomatic hyperglycemia (polyuria, polydipsia, weight loss)
- Catabolic features (unintentional weight loss, ketosis)
- Acute illness or surgery requiring rapid glycemic control

**Basal insulin initiation:**
- Start: 10 units/day OR 0.1-0.2 units/kg/day
- Options: NPH (less expensive), glargine, detemir, degludec
- Titration: Increase by 2 units every 3 days (or 10-15% weekly) until fasting target reached
- **DO NOT discontinue** organ-protective medications (SGLT2-i, GLP-1 RA) when starting insulin

**Intensification to basal-bolus:**
- If basal insulin >0.5 units/kg/day and HbA1c still above target
- Add prandial insulin to largest meal first, then additional meals as needed
- Fixed-ratio combinations (iDegLira, iGlarLixi) simplify intensification

## 2.7 Complications Screening

### Microvascular Complications

| Complication | Screening Method | Frequency |
|-------------|-----------------|-----------|
| **Diabetic retinopathy** | Dilated/comprehensive eye exam by ophthalmologist/optometrist | At diagnosis (T2DM), then annually; every 2 years if normal with well-controlled |
| **Diabetic nephropathy** | Urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR); serum creatinine/eGFR | Annually; more frequently if abnormal |
| **Diabetic neuropathy** | 10g monofilament testing; vibration perception (128 Hz tuning fork); pinprick sensation | Annually |
| **Autonomic neuropathy** | Symptoms (gastroparesis, orthostatic hypotension, erectile dysfunction, resting tachycardia) | As clinically indicated |

### Macrovascular Risk Management
- **Blood pressure:** Target <130/80 mmHg if tolerated (ACE inhibitor or ARB preferred)
- **Lipids:** Moderate- to high-intensity statin for most adults with T2DM (see Chapter 7)
- **Aspirin:** Consider for secondary prevention; primary prevention individualized based on bleeding vs. ASCVD risk
- **Smoking cessation:** Absolute requirement

### Foot Care
- Annual comprehensive foot examination
- Risk stratification: low (normal sensation/pulses), moderate (one risk factor), high (neuropathy, PAD, deformity, prior ulcer)
- Patient education on daily foot inspection, appropriate footwear
- Refer to podiatry for high-risk feet

## 2.8 Red Flags / Emergency Features

> **EMERGENCY:** New diagnosis with HbA1c >10% or symptomatic — consider insulin and evaluate for T1DM (check autoantibodies, C-peptide)

- **Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA):** More common in T2DM with severe stress, SGLT2-i use, or acute illness; check ketones
- **Hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state (HHS):** Glucose >600 mg/dL, effective serum osmolality >320 mOsm/kg, minimal ketosis; mortality 10-20%
- **Foot ulcer with infection signs:** Erythema, warmth, purulent drainage, malodor — requires urgent evaluation
- **Sudden vision changes:** Could indicate vitreous hemorrhage, retinal detachment, or macular edema
- **Chest pain:** Evaluate for acute coronary syndrome (diabetes is a coronary artery disease equivalent)

---

## 2.9 Special Populations

### Older Adults
- Avoid tight glycemic control; prioritize hypoglycemia prevention
- Targets: HbA1c <8.0-8.5% may be appropriate
- Simplify medication regimens
- Avoid glyburide (high hypoglycemia risk)
- Metformin safe if eGFR adequate

### Chronic Kidney Disease
- Metformin: contraindicated if eGFR <30; reduce dose and monitor if 30-45
- SGLT2-i: continue for kidney/heart protection even when eGFR falls below glucose-lowering threshold
- GLP-1 RA: generally safe; reduce dose for some agents if severe CKD
- DPP-4i: linagliptin requires no renal dose adjustment
- Insulin: reduced clearance; lower doses needed

### Pregnancy
- Insulin is the preferred treatment for T2DM in pregnancy
- Targets: fasting <95 mg/dL; 1h <140 mg/dL; 2h <120 mg/dL
- Preconception counseling essential; optimize control before conception
- Metformin and glyburide may be used but insulin preferred
- Stop statins, ACE inhibitors, ARBs before conception

# Chapter 3: Thyroid Disorders

## 3.1 Hypothyroidism

### Overview
Hypothyroidism is the clinical syndrome resulting from insufficient thyroid hormone production. In iodine-sufficient regions, chronic autoimmune thyroiditis (Hashimoto's thyroiditis) is the most common cause, affecting approximately 5% of the population, with a female-to-male ratio of 5-10:1 [^7^].

### Etiology

| Category | Causes |
|----------|--------|
| **Primary (95%)** | Hashimoto's thyroiditis (autoimmune), post-ablative (radioactive iodine, surgery), postpartum thyroiditis, drug-induced (amiodarone, lithium, interferon, immune checkpoint inhibitors), iodine deficiency/excess, congenital |
| **Central (5%)** | Pituitary adenoma, pituitary surgery/radiation, hypothalamic disease (TRH deficiency), infiltrative (hemochromatosis) |
| **Peripheral (rare)** | Thyroid hormone resistance (RTH), thyroid hormone transport defects |

### Clinical Presentation

| System | Symptoms |
|--------|----------|
| Constitutional | Fatigue, cold intolerance, weight gain, lethargy |
| Dermatologic | Dry skin, coarse hair, hair loss, non-pitting edema (myxedema), brittle nails |
| Cardiovascular | Bradycardia, diastolic hypertension, hyperlipidemia, pericardial effusion |
| GI | Constipation, decreased appetite, weight gain |
| Neuromuscular | Muscle cramps, weakness, delayed reflexes (relaxation phase), carpal tunnel syndrome |
| Psychiatric | Depression, cognitive slowing, apathy |
| Reproductive | Menorrhagia, infertility, galactorrhea (from TRH-stimulated prolactin) |
| Metabolic | Elevated cholesterol, hyponatremia (SIADH-like), anemia (normocytic or macrocytic) |

### Physical Examination
- Bradycardia, delayed relaxation of deep tendon reflexes
- Dry, coarse skin; periorbital puffiness
- Goiter (firm, rubbery, sometimes nodular in Hashimoto's)
- Myxedema (non-pitting edema of hands, face)
- Hoarse voice

### Diagnosis

| Test | Overt Hypothyroidism | Subclinical Hypothyroidism |
|------|---------------------|---------------------------|
| TSH | Elevated (>4.5-10 mIU/L) | Elevated (typically 4.5-10) |
| Free T4 | Low (<0.8 ng/dL) | Normal |
| Anti-TPO antibodies | Positive in 90-95% of Hashimoto's | Often positive |
| Anti-thyroglobulin antibodies | Positive in 60-80% | May be positive |

> **IMPORTANT:** Always check free T4 with TSH. TSH alone cannot distinguish overt from subclinical hypothyroidism. Anti-TPO antibodies help identify autoimmune etiology.

### Treatment — Levothyroxine (L-T4) [^7^]

| Parameter | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| **Mechanism** | Synthetic T4; converted to active T3 peripherally |
| **Dosing (young, healthy)** | 1.6 mcg/kg/day (full replacement) |
| **Dosing (elderly, cardiac disease)** | Start 25-50 mcg/day; increase every 4-6 weeks |
| **Dosing (elderly >65, CAD)** | Start 12.5-25 mcg/day; titrate slowly |
| **Administration** | On empty stomach, 30-60 minutes before breakfast |
| **Drug interactions** | Separate from calcium, iron, PPIs, soy, coffee by at least 4 hours |
| **Monitoring** | TSH 6-8 weeks after initiation or dose change; then every 6-12 months when stable |
| **Target** | TSH within reference range (typically 0.5-4.0 mIU/L) |

**Key considerations:**
- Same brand/generic should be maintained (bioequivalence varies between formulations)
- Pregnancy: increase dose by 20-30% as soon as pregnancy confirmed; target TSH <2.5 first trimester, <3.0 second/third
- Many drugs reduce levothyroxine absorption (calcium, iron, cholestyramine, PPIs, sucralfate, aluminum-containing antacids)
- Some drugs increase T4 metabolism (carbamazepine, phenytoin, rifampin, sertraline, estrogen)

### Subclinical Hypothyroidism
- Defined as elevated TSH with normal free T4
- Present in 4-10% of the population; increases with age

**Treatment considerations:**
- **Treat if:** TSH >10 mIU/L, positive anti-TPO antibodies, symptoms, pregnancy plans, goiter, dyslipidemia, prior treatment for thyroid cancer
- **Consider trial if:** TSH 4.5-10 with symptoms (reassess in 3-6 months)
- **Monitor if untreated:** TSH every 6-12 months (progression to overt hypothyroidism ~2-5% per year if antibody-positive)

### Myxedema Coma — EMERGENCY PROTOCOL

> **RED FLAG:** Myxedema coma is a life-threatening emergency with mortality >30-60% even with treatment. Precipitants include infection, cold exposure, surgery, trauma, CNS depressants.

**Clinical features:** Hypothermia, altered mental status (coma), bradycardia, hypotension, hypoventilation, hyponatremia, hypoglycemia.

**Emergency treatment:**
1. **Levothyroxine IV:** 200-400 mcg loading dose, then 50-100 mcg daily
2. **Hydrocortisone IV:** 50-100 mg q8h (until adrenal insufficiency excluded — ACTH stimulation test)
3. **Supportive care:** Mechanical ventilation if needed, passive rewarming (avoid rapid rewarming), IV fluids (careful — risk of water intoxication)
4. **Consider liothyronine (T3):** 5-20 mcg IV every 8 hours if no response to T4 alone (controversial)
5. **Treat precipitating cause** (antibiotics if infection suspected)

## 3.2 Hyperthyroidism

### Overview
Hyperthyroidism results from excessive thyroid hormone production. Graves' disease (autoimmune stimulation of TSH receptor) accounts for 60-80% of cases, followed by toxic multinodular goiter (5-15%) and toxic adenoma (2-10%) [^7^][^8^].

### Etiology

| Cause | Mechanism | Key Features |
|-------|-----------|--------------|
| **Graves' disease** | TSH receptor stimulating antibodies (TRAb/TSI) | Diffuse goiter, ophthalmopathy, dermopathy; onset usually <40 |
| **Toxic multinodular goiter** | Autonomous hormone production in nodules | Older patients; long-standing goiter; no eye disease |
| **Toxic adenoma** | Single autonomous functioning nodule | Single "hot" nodule on scan |
| **Thyroiditis** | Inflammation with hormone release | Painful (subacute/de Quervain's) or painless (silent/postpartum) |
| **Iodine-induced (Jod-Basedow)** | Iodine load triggers hormone production | After contrast, amiodarone, kelp supplements |
| **Exogenous thyroid hormone** | Excessive intake | Low thyroid uptake; suppressed thyroglobulin |

### Clinical Presentation

| System | Symptoms |
|--------|----------|
| Constitutional | Heat intolerance, weight loss despite increased appetite, fatigue |
| Cardiovascular | Palpitations, tachycardia, atrial fibrillation (especially >60 years), widened pulse pressure |
| Neuromuscular | Tremor, anxiety, irritability, insomnia, proximal muscle weakness, periodic paralysis (Asian males) |
| GI | Increased bowel movements/diarrhea |
| Reproductive | Oligomenorrhea/amenorrhea, infertility, gynecomastia (men), decreased libido |
| Dermatologic | Warm, moist skin; fine hair; onycholysis (Plummer's nails); pretibial myxedema (Graves') |
| Ocular (Graves') | Exophthalmos, periorbital edema, diplopia, chemosis, optic neuropathy (rare) |

### Physical Examination
- Tachycardia or irregularly irregular rhythm (AF)
- Fine tremor, hyperreflexia, warm moist skin
- Lid lag, lid retraction (stare)
- Diffuse goiter (smooth, may have bruit in Graves')
- Signs specific to Graves': ophthalmopathy, pretibial myxedema, thyroid acropachy

### Diagnosis

| Test | Result |
|------|--------|
| TSH | Suppressed (<0.01 mIU/L typically) |
| Free T4 | Elevated (may be normal in T3 toxicosis) |
| Free T3 | Elevated (may be disproportionately elevated) |
| TSH receptor antibodies (TRAb/TSI) | Positive in Graves' disease |
| Radioactive iodine uptake (RAIU) | High/diffuse (Graves'); patchy (toxic MNG); focal (adenoma); low (thyroiditis) |
| Thyroid ultrasound | Diffuse enlargement (Graves'); nodules (toxic MNG, adenoma) |

### Treatment of Graves' Disease

#### 1. Antithyroid Drugs (ATDs) — First-Line in Most Cases [^8^]

| Parameter | Methimazole (MMI) | Propylthiouracil (PTU) |
|-----------|-------------------|----------------------|
| **Dosing** | 10-40 mg once daily | 100-150 mg q8h |
| **Mechanism** | Inhibits thyroid hormone synthesis | Inhibits synthesis AND peripheral T4-to-T3 conversion |
| **Potency** | ~10x more potent | Less potent |
| **First trimester safety** | Teratogenic risk (aplasia cutis, choanal atresia) | Preferred in first trimester |
| **Major side effects** | Agranulocytosis (0.2-0.5%), hepatotoxicity (cholestatic) | Agranulocytosis, hepatotoxicity (severe/fatal hepatitis) |
| **Monitoring** | Baseline CBC, LFTs; check if fever/sore throat | Baseline CBC, LFTs; check if fever/sore throat, jaundice |
| **Duration** | 12-18 months, then assess for remission | 12-18 months |
| **Remission rate** | 40-50% | Similar |

> **CRITICAL SAFETY ALERT:** Agranulocytosis (neutrophils <500/mcL) is a medical emergency. Patients must be instructed to stop ATD and seek immediate medical attention if they develop fever or sore throat. CBC should be checked immediately.

**Factors favoring remission with ATDs:**
- Mild disease, small goiter
- Negative TRAb at diagnosis
- TSH normalized within 8-12 weeks
- Male gender
- Compliance with treatment

#### 2. Radioactive Iodine (RAI) Therapy
- Definitive treatment — destroys thyroid tissue
- Contraindicated in pregnancy and breastfeeding
- Requires pre-treatment with ATDs if severe hyperthyroidism (to prevent thyroid storm from radiation-induced hormone release)
- Results in permanent hypothyroidism (requires lifelong levothyroxine)
- May worsen Graves' ophthalmopathy (give prednisone if moderate-severe eye disease)

#### 3. Surgery (Thyroidectomy)
- Indications: Very large goiter, suspicious nodules, failed ATD/RAI, patient preference, pregnancy (second trimester), moderate-severe ophthalmopathy
- Requires preoperative preparation with ATDs (achieve euthyroid state)
- Risks: Recurrent laryngeal nerve injury (1-2%), hypoparathyroidism (temporary 10-30%, permanent 1-5%), bleeding, infection
- Total thyroidectomy → lifelong levothyroxine

#### 4. Graves' Ophthalmopathy Management
- Smoking cessation (strong risk factor for progression)
- Artificial tears, sunglasses, head-of-bed elevation
- Selenium supplementation (100 mcg BID for 6 months) may benefit mild active disease
- Active moderate-severe disease: High-dose IV glucocorticoids (first-line), orbital radiotherapy, teprotumumab (IGF-1R monoclonal antibody)
- Sight-threatening (dysthyroid optic neuropathy): Urgent surgical decompression

### Toxic Nodular Goiter and Toxic Adenoma
- RAI or surgery preferred (ATDs only for pre-treatment)
- Lower remission rate with ATDs alone (autonomous tissue not autoimmune)
- RAI highly effective for toxic adenoma

## 3.3 Thyroid Storm — EMERGENCY PROTOCOL

> **RED FLAG:** Thyroid storm is a life-threatening medical emergency with mortality 10-30% even with treatment. Burch-Wartofsky score >45 indicates thyroid storm. Precipitants include infection, surgery, trauma, RAI therapy, parturition, iodine load, ATD withdrawal [^8^].

### Clinical Features
- Fever >38.5 C (101.3 F), often >40 C
- Tachycardia (>140 bpm), atrial fibrillation, heart failure
- CNS dysfunction (agitation, delirium, psychosis, seizures, coma)
- GI-hepatic symptoms (nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, abdominal pain, jaundice)
- Cardiovascular collapse

### Burch-Wartofsky Point Scale
| Parameter | Points |
|-----------|--------|
| Temperature | 37.2-37.7 (5), 37.8-38.2 (10), 38.3-38.8 (15), 38.9-39.4 (20), 39.5-39.9 (25), >=40 (30) |
| Cardiovascular | Tachycardia 90-109 (5), 110-119 (10), 120-139 (15), >=140 (25); AF (10), CHF (5-15) |
| CNS | Mild agitation (10), delirium/psychosis (20), seizure/coma (30) |
| GI-hepatic | Diarrhea/nausea/vomiting/abdominal pain (10-20), jaundice (30) |
| Precipitant | Present (10) |

**Interpretation:** >=45: highly suggestive; 25-44: suggestive/imminent; <25: unlikely

### Emergency Treatment Protocol [^8^]

| Step | Treatment | Rationale |
|------|-----------|-----------|
| 1 | **PTU 600-1000 mg loading, then 200-250 mg q4h** (OR MMI 60-80 mg load, then 20-25 mg q4h) | Block new hormone synthesis; PTU preferred (blocks T4-to-T3 conversion) |
| 2 | **SSKI/Lugol's iodine:** 5 drops (0.25 mL) q6h — give **1 hour AFTER** ATD | Block hormone release (Wolff-Chaikoff effect); ATD must be given first to prevent iodine being used for synthesis |
| 3 | **Propranolol 60-80 mg PO q4h** (or IV esmolol 50-100 mcg/kg/min) | Block adrenergic effects; reduce T4-to-T3 conversion |
| 4 | **Hydrocortisone 100 mg IV q8h** | Treat possible adrenal insufficiency; inhibit T4-to-T3 conversion |
| 5 | **Supportive care:** IV fluids, cooling blankets (NOT aspirin — displaces T4 from TBG), acetaminophen, oxygen, treat precipitating cause | |
| 6 | **Cholestyramine:** 4 g PO q6h | Binds thyroid hormone in gut, increases fecal excretion |
| 7 | **Definitive therapy:** RAI or surgery once stable | |

## 3.4 Subclinical Thyroid Disease

### Subclinical Hypothyroidism
- TSH elevated (typically 4.5-10), free T4 normal
- Anti-TPO positive: 4.3% annual progression to overt disease
- Anti-TPO negative: 2.6% annual progression

**Treatment approach:**
- TSH >10: Generally treat (cardiovascular risk, symptom benefit)
- TSH 4.5-10 with symptoms, positive antibodies, pregnancy plans, or goiter: Consider trial of levothyroxine
- TSH 4.5-10, asymptomatic, negative antibodies: Monitor TSH every 6-12 months

### Subclinical Hyperthyroidism
- TSH suppressed (<0.1), free T4 and T3 normal
- Associated with atrial fibrillation, osteoporosis, cardiovascular mortality

**Treatment approach:**
- TSH <0.1: Generally treat, especially if >65 years, cardiac disease, osteoporosis
- TSH 0.1-0.4: Consider treatment based on risk factors; monitor if untreated
- Evaluate cause: Exogenous thyroid hormone, early Graves', nodular disease

## 3.5 Thyroid Nodules and Cancer

### Overview
Thyroid nodules are extremely common, detected in 50-60% of adults on ultrasound. Only 5-10% are malignant. The goal of evaluation is to identify the minority of nodules that require intervention [^9^].

### Diagnostic Approach (ATA 2015 Guidelines) [^9^]

1. **TSH first:** If suppressed, radionuclide scan to identify "hot" (functioning) nodule (rarely malignant)
2. **Thyroid ultrasound:** All nodules >=1 cm or suspicious features
3. **FNA based on ultrasound risk stratification:**

| Sonographic Pattern | Malignancy Risk | FNA Threshold | Features |
|--------------------|----------------|---------------|----------|
| **High suspicion** | >70-90% | >=1 cm | Solid hypoechoic, irregular margins, microcalcifications, taller-than-wide, extrathyroidal extension |
| **Intermediate suspicion** | 10-20% | >=1 cm | Hypoechoic solid, smooth margins, no microcalcifications |
| **Low suspicion** | 5-10% | >=1.5 cm | Isoechoic/hyperechoic solid, partially cystic with eccentric solid area |
| **Very low suspicion** | <3% | >=2 cm | Spongiform, partially cystic without suspicious features |
| **Benign** | <1% | No biopsy | Pure cyst |

### Bethesda System for Cytopathology
| Category | Risk of Malignancy | Management |
|----------|-------------------|------------|
| I: Non-diagnostic | — | Repeat FNA with ultrasound guidance |
| II: Benign | 0-3% | Clinical/ultrasound follow-up |
| III: AUS/FLUS | 5-15% | Repeat FNA, molecular testing, or diagnostic lobectomy |
| IV: Follicular neoplasm | 15-30% | Diagnostic lobectomy |
| V: Suspicious for malignancy | 60-75% | Near-total thyroidectomy or lobectomy |
| VI: Malignant | 97-99% | Near-total thyroidectomy |

### Differentiated Thyroid Cancer (Papillary/Follicular)
- **Treatment:** Total thyroidectomy +/- radioactive iodine ablation + TSH suppression
- **TSH suppression goals:** Low-risk: 0.1-0.5 mIU/L; High-risk: <0.1 mIU/L
- **Follow-up:** Thyroglobulin levels (marker of residual/recurrent disease), neck ultrasound, whole-body scan if indicated

## 3.6 TSH Interpretation Guide

| TSH | Free T4 | Free T3 | Interpretation | Next Steps |
|-----|---------|---------|----------------|------------|
| High | Low | Low/Normal | Primary hypothyroidism | Start levothyroxine; check anti-TPO |
| High | Normal | Normal | Subclinical hypothyroidism | Assess for treatment; check anti-TPO |
| Low | High | High | Primary hyperthyroidism | Check TRAb, RAIU; determine etiology |
| Low | Normal | Normal | Subclinical hyperthyroidism | Evaluate cause; consider treatment |
| Low | Low | Low | Central (secondary) hypothyroidism | Evaluate pituitary; MRI; do NOT treat based on TSH |
| Normal | High | High | Thyroid hormone resistance (rare) | Genetic testing if suspected |
| Low | High | Normal | T4 toxicosis (rare) | Check TBG, drug effects |
| Low | Normal | High | T3 toxicosis | Early/mild hyperthyroidism; check T3-predominant causes |

### Important Laboratory Considerations
- **Biotin interference:** High-dose biotin (>5 mg/day) causes falsely LOW TSH and falsely HIGH T3/T4. Stop biotin 48 hours before thyroid function testing.
- **Acute illness (euthyroid sick syndrome):** TSH may be transiently low or high; free T4 may be low; do NOT treat based on abnormal thyroid tests during acute illness unless clear thyroid dysfunction.
- **Pregnancy:** Trimester-specific TSH reference ranges required; TSH typically lower in first trimester.
- **Amiodarone:** Complex effects — can cause hypo- or hyperthyroidism; check thyroid function before starting and every 6 months during therapy.
- **Glucocorticoids, dopamine agonists:** Suppress TSH
- **Lithium:** Can cause hypothyroidism (check TSH periodically)

## 3.7 When to Seek Emergency Care

- **Thyroid storm:** High fever, severe tachycardia, agitation/confusion, vomiting/diarrhea
- **Myxedema coma:** Severe hypothermia, altered mental status, bradycardia, hypoventilation
- **Rapidly enlarging neck mass** with breathing or swallowing difficulty
- **New-onset atrial fibrillation** (evaluate for hyperthyroidism)
- **Severe eye pain/vision changes** in Graves' disease (optic neuropathy emergency)

# Chapter 4: Adrenal Disorders

## 4.1 Cushing's Syndrome

### Overview
Cushing's syndrome results from chronic exposure to excess glucocorticoids. Endogenous causes are categorized as ACTH-dependent (pituitary adenoma [Cushing's disease], ectopic ACTH syndrome) and ACTH-independent (adrenal adenoma, adrenal carcinoma, macronodular hyperplasia) [^10^]. Exogenous glucocorticoid use (iatrogenic Cushing's) is actually the most common cause.

### Etiology

| Category | Cause | Approximate Frequency |
|----------|-------|---------------------|
| **Iatrogenic (exogenous)** | Glucocorticoid therapy | Most common overall |
| **ACTH-dependent** | | ~80% of endogenous |
| — Pituitary adenoma (Cushing's disease) | | 70% |
| — Ectopic ACTH | Small cell lung cancer, carcinoid, medullary thyroid cancer, pheochromocytoma | 10-15% |
| **ACTH-independent** | | ~20% of endogenous |
| — Adrenal adenoma | | 10-15% |
| — Adrenal carcinoma | | Rare but aggressive |
| — Bilateral macronodular hyperplasia | | Rare |
| — Primary pigmented nodular adrenocortical disease (PPNAD) | Genetic (Carney complex) | Rare |

### Clinical Features

| System | Features |
|--------|----------|
| **Central obesity** | Truncal obesity, "moon facies," "buffalo hump" (dorsocervical fat pad), supraclavicular fat pads |
| **Skin** | Purple striae (>1 cm wide), thin skin, easy bruising, acne, hirsutism, hyperpigmentation (if ACTH elevated) |
| **Musculoskeletal** | Proximal muscle weakness, osteopenia/osteoporosis, vertebral fractures |
| **Cardiovascular** | Hypertension, edema |
| **Metabolic** | Impaired glucose tolerance/diabetes, dyslipidemia, weight gain |
| **Psychiatric** | Depression, anxiety, irritability, cognitive impairment, psychosis |
| **Reproductive** | Menstrual irregularities, decreased libido, infertility |
| **Infections** | Increased susceptibility (impaired immunity) |

### Diagnosis [^10^]

**Step 1: Screening (at least one abnormal test required)**

| Screening Test | Procedure | Positive Result | Notes |
|---------------|-----------|----------------|-------|
| 24-hour urinary free cortisol (UFC) | Collect 24h urine x2-3 | >3x upper limit of normal | False positives: stress, alcohol, depression, obesity, pregnancy |
| Late-night salivary cortisol | Collect at 11 PM x2 | Elevated | Loses circadian rhythm in Cushing's |
| 1 mg overnight dexamethasone suppression test (DST) | 1 mg dexamethasone at 11 PM; check 8 AM cortisol | Cortisol >1.8 mcg/dL | Simple screening test |

**Step 2: Confirmatory testing** if one screening test positive

**Step 3: Determine ACTH-dependent vs. independent**
- ACTH level: ACTH-dependent (>10 pg/mL), ACTH-independent (<5 pg/mL)

**Step 4: Localization**

| ACTH Level | Next Test | Interpretation |
|------------|-----------|----------------|
| ACTH-dependent | Pituitary MRI | If pituitary adenoma visible: likely Cushing's disease |
| ACTH-dependent + equivocal MRI | Inferior petrosal sinus sampling (IPSS) | Gold standard; ACTH gradient central:peripheral >=2 baseline, >=3 after CRH |
| ACTH-independent | CT adrenal | Adrenal mass/macronodular hyperplasia |
| High ACTH + no pituitary lesion | CT chest/abdomen | Search for ectopic ACTH source |

### Treatment [^10^]

**First-line: Surgical resection**

| Cause | Treatment | Notes |
|-------|-----------|-------|
| Cushing's disease (pituitary) | Transsphenoidal selective adenomectomy (TSS) | Experienced pituitary surgeon essential; remission 70-80% for microadenomas |
| Adrenal adenoma | Unilateral laparoscopic adrenalectomy | |
| Adrenal carcinoma | Open adrenalectomy +/- chemotherapy (mitotane) | Poor prognosis; high recurrence rate |
| Ectopic ACTH | Resection if source identified | Often occult or metastatic; medical management common |

**Medical therapy (if surgery contraindicated/unsuccessful):**

| Drug | Mechanism | Dosing | Notes |
|------|-----------|--------|-------|
| Ketoconazole | Steroidogenesis inhibitor | 400-1200 mg/day | Hepatotoxicity risk; QT prolongation; drug interactions |
| Metyrapone | 11-beta hydroxylase inhibitor | 500 mg TID to 4.5 g/day | Rapid onset; hirsutism from androgen accumulation |
| Osilodrostat | 11-beta hydroylase inhibitor | 2-30 mg BID | FDA approved; LFT monitoring |
| Mitotane | Adrenolytic | Start 500 mg BID | For adrenal carcinoma; also used for Cushing's |
| Mifepristone | Glucocorticoid receptor antagonist | 300-1200 mg/day | No need to measure cortisol (will be high); monitor for hypokalemia, adrenal insufficiency |

**Bilateral adrenalectomy:** For severe/recurrent Cushing's disease when pituitary surgery and medical therapy fail

**Postoperative management:**
- Glucocorticoid replacement required (HPA axis recovery takes 6-18 months)
- Hydrocortisone 10-12 mg/m2/day in divided doses
- Taper slowly; reassess axis with morning cortisol and ACTH
- Lifelong surveillance for recurrence (except adrenal adenoma with HU <10)

## 4.2 Addison's Disease (Primary Adrenal Insufficiency)

### Overview
Primary adrenal insufficiency results from destruction of the adrenal cortex, leading to deficiency of cortisol and aldosterone. Autoimmune adrenalitis accounts for 70-90% of cases in developed countries [^11^][^12^]. It affects approximately 1 in 10,000 people. Without treatment, it is fatal; with proper replacement therapy, life expectancy is normal.

### Etiology

| Cause | Details |
|-------|---------|
| **Autoimmune adrenalitis** | 70-90% of cases in developed countries; isolated or part of APS type 1 or 2 |
| **Infections** | TB (most common worldwide), HIV, histoplasmosis, CMV, fungal |
| **Metastatic disease** | Lung, breast, melanoma, lymphoma |
| **Adrenal hemorrhage** | Waterhouse-Friderichsen (meningococcemia), anticoagulation, sepsis |
| **Genetic** | Congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH), X-linked adrenoleukodystrophy (ALD) |
| **Drugs** | Ketoconazole, etomidate (even single dose), mitotane |
| **Infiltrative** | Amyloidosis, sarcoidosis, hemochromatosis |

### Clinical Features

| System | Features | Explanation |
|--------|----------|-------------|
| Constitutional | Fatigue, weakness, anorexia, weight loss | Cortisol deficiency |
| GI | Nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, diarrhea | Cortisol deficiency |
| Cardiovascular | Hypotension (orthostatic), syncope, shock in crisis | Aldosterone deficiency (volume depletion) |
| Skin/mucous membranes | Hyperpigmentation (creases, scars, oral mucosa) | Elevated ACTH stimulates melanocytes |
| Metabolic | Hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, hypercalcemia (mild) | Aldosterone deficiency |
| Psychiatric | Irritability, depression, psychosis | Cortisol deficiency |
| Reproductive | Amenorrhea, decreased libido, loss of axillary/pubic hair (women) | Adrenal androgen deficiency |

### Diagnosis

| Test | Result | Interpretation |
|------|--------|----------------|
| **Morning cortisol (8 AM)** | <3 mcg/dL | Strongly suggestive |
| **Morning cortisol** | >15 mcg/dL | Excludes adrenal insufficiency |
| **ACTH** | Elevated (>2-3x upper limit) | Consistent with PRIMARY adrenal insufficiency |
| **ACTH stimulation test** | Cortisol <18 mcg/dL at 30 or 60 min after 250 mcg cosyntropin | Gold standard for diagnosis |
| **Renin and aldosterone** | Elevated renin, low aldosterone | Confirms mineralocorticoid deficiency |
| **21-hydroxylase autoantibodies** | Positive | Confirms autoimmune etiology |
| **Electrolytes** | Hyponatremia, hyperkalemia | Aldosterone deficiency |
| **CT adrenals** | Atrophic adrenals (autoimmune); calcified/hyperplastic (TB); enlarged (hemorrhage, metastases) | Helpful for non-autoimmune causes |

### Treatment [^11^][^12^]

#### Glucocorticoid Replacement

| Drug | Dosing | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|------|--------|-----------|--------------|
| **Hydrocortisone** | 15-25 mg/day in 2-3 divided doses (largest dose in morning, smaller at lunch, optional small evening dose) | Physiologic; short half-life allows normal diurnal variation; easy to adjust for stress | Multiple daily doses; peaks and troughs |
| **Cortisone acetate** | 20-35 mg/day in 2-3 doses | Oral prodrug of cortisol | Requires hepatic conversion |
| **Prednisolone** | 3-5 mg once daily | Single daily dosing; convenient | Longer half-life; may cause more bone/metabolic effects |
| **Modified-release hydrocortisone (Plenadren)** | 20 mg once daily morning | Mimics circadian rhythm | Not universally available |

**Key principles:**
- Morning dose should be largest (simulates normal cortisol peak)
- Dose must be increased during stress (illness, surgery, trauma)
- No reliable laboratory test for monitoring — clinical assessment (energy, weight, BP) is primary guide
- **Never abruptly discontinue** — risk of adrenal crisis

#### Mineralocorticoid Replacement
- **Fludrocortisone:** 50-300 mcg/day (start 100 mcg)
- Titrate based on blood pressure, electrolytes, and plasma renin activity
- Not needed in secondary/tertiary adrenal insufficiency (aldosterone preserved via RAAS)

#### Patient Education — Critical for Crisis Prevention
- **Carry steroid emergency card** and medical alert bracelet
- **Sick day rules:** Double or triple hydrocortisone dose during febrile illness, vomiting, significant injury
- **Emergency injection kit:** 100 mg hydrocortisone for IM injection for patients who cannot take oral medications
- **Know when to seek emergency care:** Persistent vomiting, inability to take oral steroids, severe weakness, confusion

## 4.3 Adrenal Crisis — EMERGENCY PROTOCOL

> **RED FLAG:** Adrenal crisis is a life-threatening emergency. Mortality is 0.5-1% per crisis episode. Precipitants include infection, trauma, surgery, abrupt glucocorticoid withdrawal, gastroenteritis [^11^].

### Clinical Features
- Severe weakness, fatigue, confusion, altered mental status
- Abdominal pain, vomiting (may mimic acute abdomen)
- Hypotension (refractory to fluids), shock, cardiovascular collapse
- Hypoglycemia, hyponatremia, hyperkalemia
- Fever (if precipitated by infection)
- Coma (untreated)

### Emergency Treatment [^11^][^12^]

| Step | Treatment | Details |
|------|-----------|---------|
| 1 | **Hydrocortisone IV 100 mg immediately** | Bolus — do NOT delay for lab results |
| 2 | **0.9% saline IV** | 1 liter over first hour, then adjust to hemodynamic status |
| 3 | **Hydrocortisone 200 mg/24 hours** | Continuous infusion or 50 mg IV q6h |
| 4 | **Glucose management** | Treat hypoglycemia if present |
| 5 | **Treat precipitating cause** | Broad-spectrum antibiotics if infection suspected |
| 6 | **Supportive care** | Vasopressors if needed after fluids and hydrocortisone |

**Transition to oral:** Once stable and tolerating oral intake, switch to oral hydrocortisone at usual replacement doses with stress dosing until illness resolves.

## 4.4 Adrenal Incidentaloma

### Overview
An adrenal mass discovered incidentally on imaging performed for non-adrenal indications. Found in 1-5% of abdominal CT scans, with prevalence increasing with age [^10^].

### Key Clinical Question: Is it hormonally active or malignant?

### Evaluation

**Step 1: Hormonal workup (ALL patients with adrenal incidentaloma)**

| Test | Screens For | Abnormal Result |
|------|------------|----------------|
| Overnight 1 mg DST or late-night salivary cortisol | Cushing's syndrome | Cortisol >1.8 mcg/dL post-DST or elevated salivary cortisol |
| Plasma free metanephrines | Pheochromocytoma | >4x upper limit of normal highly specific |
| Aldosterone/renin ratio (if hypertensive or hypokalemic) | Primary hyperaldosteronism | ARR >20-40 |

**Step 2: Imaging characteristics**

| Feature | Suggests Benign (Adenoma) | Suggests Malignant |
|---------|--------------------------|-------------------|
| CT attenuation (HU) | <=10 | >10-20 |
| Washout (absolute) | >60% | <60% |
| Washout (relative) | >40% | <40% |
| Size | <4 cm | >4-6 cm |
| Growth on follow-up | Stable | >20% increase in size |

**Step 3: Biopsy**
- **ONLY** if metastatic disease suspected AND pheochromocytoma excluded (risk of hypertensive crisis)
- Never biopsy without excluding pheochromocytoma first

### Management

| Scenario | Management |
|----------|------------|
| **Hormonally active** | Surgical resection regardless of size |
| **Non-functional, benign imaging (HU <=10), <4 cm** | Repeat imaging in 6-12 months, then annually for 1-2 years; stop if stable |
| **Non-functional, indeterminate imaging, or >4 cm** | Consider adrenalectomy or close follow-up |
| **Malignant features** | Surgical resection with oncologic approach |

## 4.5 Primary Hyperaldosteronism (Conn's Syndrome)

### Overview
Excess aldosterone production from the adrenal glands, independent of renin stimulation. Causes hypertension, hypokalemia (though may be absent in up to 40%), and metabolic alkalosis. Most common cause of secondary hypertension.

### Clinical Features
- Resistant hypertension (failing 3 drugs including diuretic)
- Hypokalemia (spontaneous or diuretic-induced)
- Metabolic alkalosis
- Muscle weakness, fatigue, polyuria/polydipsia (hypokalemia-related)

### Diagnosis

**Step 1: Screening (aldosterone-to-renin ratio, ARR)**
- Screen: All patients with resistant hypertension, hypertension + hypokalemia, hypertension + adrenal incidentaloma, family history of early-onset hypertension or stroke (<40 years)
- **ARR >20-40 ng/dL per ng/mL/hr** is suggestive
- Correct for potassium (hypokalemia suppresses aldosterone), medications (mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, ACE-I, ARBs affect results)

**Step 2: Confirmatory testing**
- Saline suppression test (primary method)
- Oral sodium loading test
- Fludrocortisone suppression test

**Step 3: Subtype diagnosis**
- CT adrenal: Identify unilateral vs. bilateral disease
- **Adrenal venous sampling (AVS):** Gold standard for lateralization; essential if surgical candidate

### Treatment

| Type | Treatment | Outcomes |
|------|-----------|----------|
| **Unilateral aldosterone-producing adenoma** | Laparoscopic adrenalectomy | Cure of hypokalemia (~100%); hypertension cure 30-60%; improvement in 90% |
| **Bilateral adrenal hyperplasia** | Mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist | |

**Medical therapy for bilateral disease:**

| Drug | Dosing | Side Effects |
|------|--------|-------------|
| **Spironolactone** | 25-400 mg/day | Gynecomastia, breast tenderness, sexual dysfunction, menstrual irregularities |
| **Eplerenone** | 50-200 mg/day (divided BID) | More selective; fewer sexual side effects; shorter half-life |
| **Amiloride** | 5-10 mg BID | Potassium-sparing diuretic; alternative if intolerant of MRAs |

## 4.6 Pheochromocytoma

### Overview
Catecholamine-secreting tumor of adrenal medulla (pheochromocytoma) or extra-adrenal paraganglia (paraganglioma). The "rule of 10s" is a useful teaching tool: approximately 10% bilateral, 10% malignant, 10% extra-adrenal, 10% familial, 10% pediatric [^12^].

### Clinical Features (Paroxysmal or Sustained)
- **Classic triad:** Episodic headache, sweating, palpitations/tachycardia
- Hypertension (sustained or paroxysmal; may be resistant)
- Anxiety, panic attacks, tremor
- Pallor (not flushing — catecholamine-mediated vasoconstriction)
- Orthostatic hypotension (volume depletion + impaired baroreflex)
- Weight loss, hyperglycemia
- Cardiomyopathy (catecholamine-induced)

### Diagnosis

| Test | Result | Notes |
|------|--------|-------|
| **Plasma free metanephrines** | Elevated | High sensitivity; best screening test |
| **24-hour urinary fractionated metanephrines** | Elevated | Confirmatory; high specificity |
| **CT abdomen/pelvis** | Adrenal mass >1 cm or extra-adrenal mass | Sensitivity >90% |
| **MRI** | "Light bulb bright" on T2-weighted imaging | Useful for extra-adrenal/localization |
| **MIBG scan** | Uptake in tumor | Specific but less sensitive; useful for metastatic disease |
| **DOTATATE PET/CT** | Uptake in tumor | More sensitive than MIBG for detection |
| **Genetic testing** | SDHx, VHL, RET, NF1 | Consider for ALL patients with pheochromocytoma |

### Treatment

**Preoperative preparation (ESSENTIAL — inadequate blockade can be fatal):**

| Step | Treatment | Duration | Notes |
|------|-----------|----------|-------|
| 1 | **Alpha-blockade first** — phenoxybenzamine 10 mg BID, titrate to orthostasis; or doxazosin 1-2 mg daily | 7-14 days minimum | Must achieve before beta-blockade |
| 2 | Liberal salt and fluid intake | Throughout alpha-blockade | Restore intravascular volume |
| 3 | **Beta-blockade ONLY after alpha-blockade** — propranolol 10-20 mg q6-8h | Add 2-3 days before surgery | Never give beta-blocker alone (unopposed alpha = hypertensive crisis) |

**Surgery:** Laparoscopic adrenalectomy (standard); open if large or malignant

**Malignant pheochromocytoma:** 131I-MIBG therapy, chemotherapy (CVD: cyclophosphamide, vincristine, dacarbazine), targeted therapy (sunitinib), locoregional therapy

---

## 4.7 When to Seek Emergency Care

- **Adrenal crisis:** Severe vomiting, confusion, extreme weakness, hypotension/shock, especially in patient with known adrenal insufficiency or on chronic steroids
- **Hypertensive crisis:** Blood pressure >180/120 with headache, chest pain, or neurological symptoms (possible pheochromocytoma)
- **Signs of Cushing's with rapid progression** (possible adrenal carcinoma)
- **Severe hypokalemia** with muscle weakness or cardiac arrhythmias

# Chapter 5: Pituitary Disorders

## 5.1 Prolactinoma

### Overview
Prolactin-secreting pituitary adenomas are the most common hormonally active pituitary tumors. Microprolactinomas (<1 cm) predominate in women; macroprolactinomas (>=1 cm) are more common in men [^13^][^14^]. The female-to-male ratio is approximately 10:1 for microadenomas, but equalizes for macroadenomas.

### Pathophysiology
- Prolactinomas secrete excess prolactin autonomously
- Mass effect from macroadenomas can compress pituitary stalk, causing "stalk effect" (disinhibition of dopamine suppression)
- Dopamine normally suppresses prolactin; dopamine agonists are the therapeutic mainstay

### Clinical Features

| Population | Symptoms |
|------------|----------|
| **Women** | Galactorrhea (50-80%), amenorrhea/oligomenorrhea, infertility, decreased libido, dyspareunia, hirsutism (if associated hyperandrogenism) |
| **Men** | Decreased libido, erectile dysfunction, infertility, reduced muscle mass, gynecomastia (rarely galactorrhea) |
| **Mass effects (macroadenoma)** | Headache, visual field defects (bitemporal hemianopsia from optic chiasm compression), cranial nerve palsies (cavernous sinus invasion), hypopituitarism |

### Diagnosis

| Test | Finding | Notes |
|------|---------|-------|
| **Prolactin level** | Elevated | Level correlates roughly with tumor size (micro <200 ng/mL; macro >200 ng/mL typical) |
| **"Hook effect"** | Falsely normal/low prolactin | Large macroadenomas can cause assay artifact; request 1:100 dilution if large tumor with unexpectedly low prolactin |
| **Macroprolactin** | Consider if asymptomatic with elevated prolactin | Inactive prolactin polymers; no clinical significance; eliminates need for treatment |
| **MRI pituitary (with contrast)** | Hypoenhancing lesion | Gold standard for visualization; assess size and extension |
| **Pituitary function panel** | Evaluate other axes | Assess for hypopituitarism from mass effect |
| **Visual field testing** | Required for macroadenomas | Formal perimetry if suprasellar extension |

### Differential Diagnosis of Hyperprolactinemia

| Cause | Mechanism | Key Distinguishing Features |
|-------|-----------|----------------------------|
| **Physiologic** | Pregnancy, lactation, sleep, stress, exercise, nipple stimulation | Clinical context; very high levels in pregnancy |
| **Medications** | Antipsychotics (risperidone, haloperidol), metoclopramide, domperidone, verapamil, methyldopa, SSRIs, opioids, estrogen | Medication history; usually <100 ng/mL |
| **Hypothyroidism** | TRH stimulates prolactin | Check TSH; resolves with levothyroxine |
| **CKD** | Reduced clearance | Elevated creatinine; moderate elevation |
| **Stalk effect** | Any sellar mass compressing stalk | MRI shows other lesion |
| **Chest wall lesions** | Reflex stimulation | History of surgery, herpes zoster |
| **Seizures** | Post-ictal | Temporal relationship |

### Treatment (Pituitary Society Consensus 2023) [^14^]

#### First-Line: Dopamine Agonists

| Parameter | Cabergoline | Bromocriptine |
|-----------|-------------|---------------|
| **Dosing** | 0.25-0.5 mg/week initially; titrate to 0.5-3.5 mg/week | 1.25-2.5 mg/day initially; titrate to 10-15 mg/day |
| **Frequency** | 1-2 times/week | Daily (BID-TID) |
| **Efficacy** | Normalizes prolactin in 80-90%; tumor shrinkage 70-90% | Normalizes prolactin in 70-80% |
| **Side effects** | Nausea, dizziness, headache, constipation, valvular heart disease (rare, dose-related at very high doses) | More GI side effects, nasal congestion, orthostatic hypotension |
| **Pregnancy safety** | Generally considered safe; preferred for macroprolactinoma | Historical first-line for pregnancy |
| **Cost** | More expensive | Generic; less expensive |

**Cabergoline is preferred first-line** due to superior efficacy and tolerability.

**Dopamine agonist side effects to monitor:**
- Nausea, vomiting, dizziness, headache (usually transient)
- Orthostatic hypotension (especially with first dose)
- Psychiatric symptoms (impulse control disorders — gambling, shopping, hypersexuality; rare but serious)
- Cardiac valvulopathy: Rare at standard doses; monitor with echocardiogram if doses >2 mg/week

#### Surgery
- Transsphenoidal resection if DA intolerance/resistance
- Higher success rate with microadenomas (60-80%) than macroadenomas (30-50%)
- Consider for women seeking fertility with microadenoma (avoid DA exposure)

#### Radiation
- Reserved for surgical failures or aggressive tumors
- Risk of hypopituitarism (50-60% at 10 years)

#### Withdrawal of Dopamine Agonists
- 40-50% of patients with normalized prolactin and tumor shrinkage can successfully taper after 2+ years of therapy
- Criteria for trial withdrawal: Normal prolactin for >2 years, no visible tumor or significant reduction
- Monitor prolactin every 3-6 months after discontinuation; MRI at 6-12 months

### Pregnancy Management [^14^]

| Tumor Type | Management |
|------------|-----------|
| **Microprolactinoma** | Stop DA when pregnancy confirmed; <1-2% risk of symptomatic enlargement; monitor clinically (visual field checks each trimester) |
| **Macroprolactinoma** | Higher risk of tumor expansion (25-35%); stop DA if prior surgery or visual fields normal; may continue cabergoline with informed consent; monthly visual field checks; MRI without gadolinium if symptoms develop |
| **Both** | Prolactin levels NOT useful for monitoring during pregnancy (normally elevated); rely on clinical assessment and visual fields |

## 5.2 Acromegaly

### Overview
Acromegaly results from excess growth hormone (GH) secretion, almost always from a pituitary somatotroph adenoma. If occurs before epiphyseal fusion, causes gigantism. Prevalence is approximately 40-70 per million. Associated with significant morbidity and 2-3x increased mortality if untreated [^15^].

### Clinical Features

| System | Features |
|--------|----------|
| **Skeletal** | Progressive acral enlargement (hands, feet, jaw, forehead), increased shoe/ring/glove size, prognathism, malocclusion, frontal bossing |
| **Soft tissue** | Macroglossia, thickened lips, coarse facial features, oily skin, skin tags, acanthosis nigricans |
| **Cardiovascular** | Hypertension, cardiomyopathy (biventricular hypertrophy), arrhythmias, aortic root dilatation |
| **Respiratory** | Sleep apnea (obstructive and central), respiratory insufficiency |
| **Metabolic** | Insulin resistance, impaired glucose tolerance/diabetes, dyslipidemia |
| **Musculoskeletal** | Arthropathy (degenerative), carpal tunnel syndrome, back pain, proximal muscle weakness |
| **Reproductive** | Menstrual irregularities, erectile dysfunction |
| **Other** | Colonic polyps (increased cancer risk), thyroid nodules, headache, sweating, organomegaly |

### Diagnosis

| Test | Finding | Notes |
|------|---------|-------|
| **IGF-1** | Elevated for age-matched reference | Best screening test; not affected by acute factors |
| **OGTT with GH suppression** | GH nadir >0.4 mcg/L (or >1.0 mcg/L depending on assay) after 75g glucose | Confirms diagnosis; gold standard |
| **MRI pituitary** | Macroadenoma in ~70% | Assess size, extension, cavernous sinus invasion |
| **Pituitary function panel** | Variable | Assess for hypopituitarism from mass effect |
| **Visual fields** | Required if suprasellar extension | Bitemporal hemianopsia |
| **Colonic evaluation** | Required at diagnosis | Colonoscopy (increased polyp/cancer risk) |
| **ECG/echo** | Baseline cardiac assessment | Hypertrophy, arrhythmias |
| **Sleep study** | If symptoms suggestive | Sleep apnea very common |

### Treatment

**Goal:** Normalize IGF-1 and age/sex-matched GH levels; control tumor size; manage comorbidities.

#### Surgery
- **Transsphenoidal resection** is first-line for most patients
- High cure rate with microadenomas (>80%); lower with macroadenomas (40-60%)
- Experienced pituitary surgeon essential
- Best outcomes: Non-invasive microadenomas with low preoperative IGF-1

#### Medical Therapy (for surgical failure or contraindication)

| Class | Agent | Dosing | Notes |
|-------|-------|--------|-------|
| **Somatostatin analogues (SSA)** | Octreotide LAR | 20-30 mg IM monthly | First-line medical; controls GH/IGF-1 in ~60%; tumor shrinkage ~30% |
| | Lanreotide autogel | 60-120 mg SC monthly | Similar efficacy; deep SC injection |
| | Pasireotide | 40-60 mg IM monthly | Potent; higher risk of hyperglycemia |
| **GH receptor antagonist** | Pegvisomant | 10-30 mg SC daily | Blocks GH action; normalizes IGF-1 in >90%; does not shrink tumor |
| **Dopamine agonist** | Cabergoline | 0.5-3.5 mg/week | Monotherapy effective in ~30% (mild cases); adjuvant |

#### Radiation Therapy
- For surgical failures not controlled by medical therapy
- Stereotactic radiosurgery (Gamma Knife, CyberKnife) preferred
- Slow onset of action (months to years); risk of hypopituitarism

#### Long-Term Monitoring
- IGF-1 and GH every 3-6 months
- MRI annually or as indicated
- Annual colonoscopy (increased polyp/cancer risk)
- Echocardiogram, sleep study as needed
- Monitor for hypopituitarism
- Joint and bone health assessment

## 5.3 Hypopituitarism

### Overview
Deficiency of one or more anterior pituitary hormones (ACTH, TSH, LH/FSH, GH, prolactin) or posterior pituitary hormones (ADH/oxytocin). Can be congenital or acquired [^16^].

### Etiology

| Category | Causes |
|----------|--------|
| **Pituitary adenoma or treatment** | Macroadenoma compression, surgery, radiation therapy |
| **Pituitary apoplexy** | Hemorrhagic infarction of pituitary (often into existing adenoma) |
| **Traumatic brain injury** | Shearing of pituitary stalk |
| **Subarachnoid hemorrhage** | Vasospasm affecting blood supply |
| **Infiltrative** | Hemochromatosis, sarcoidosis, Langerhans cell histiocytosis |
| **Infectious** | TB, fungal, syphilis |
| **Autoimmune** | Lymphocytic hypophysitis (often postpartum or associated with checkpoint inhibitors) |
| **Genetic** | Mutations in transcription factors, PROP1, POU1F1 |
| **Empty sella** | CSF herniation into sella turcica |
| **Medications** | High-dose glucocorticoids (suppress ACTH) |

### Clinical Features by Hormone Deficiency

| Hormone | Clinical Features | Laboratory Findings |
|---------|------------------|---------------------|
| **ACTH (adrenal insufficiency)** | Fatigue, weakness, hypoglycemia, hypotension, pallor (no hyperpigmentation — ACTH not elevated) | Low morning cortisol, low/normal ACTH; failed ACTH stimulation test |
| **TSH (secondary hypothyroidism)** | Similar to primary but usually milder; cold intolerance, fatigue, weight gain | Low free T4 with inappropriately normal/low TSH |
| **LH/FSH (hypogonadism)** | Women: amenorrhea, infertility, hot flashes, vaginal dryness; Men: decreased libido, ED, infertility, decreased body hair, anemia | Low estrogen/testosterone with low/normal LH/FSH |
| **GH** | Decreased muscle mass, increased visceral fat, dyslipidemia, reduced bone density, fatigue, poor quality of life | Low IGF-1 (not reliable in adults); GH stimulation testing |
| **Prolactin** | Failure to lactate postpartum | Inability to lactate |
| **ADH (diabetes insipidus)** | Polyuria, polydipsia, nocturia | See DI section below |

### Treatment [^16^]

| Deficiency | Treatment | Monitoring |
|------------|-----------|------------|
| **ACTH** | Hydrocortisone 15-25 mg/day in 2-3 doses | Clinical; stress-dose education; emergency card |
| **TSH** | Levothyroxine (use free T4 target — upper-normal range; NOT TSH-guided) | Free T4 (not TSH) |
| **LH/FSH (men)** | Testosterone replacement (gel, injection, patch) | Hematocrit, PSA, lipids |
| **LH/FSH (women)** | Estrogen/progestin replacement (if premenopausal) | BP, lipids, breast exam |
| **GH** | GH replacement 0.2-0.6 mg/day SC | IGF-1, body composition, QoL |
| **ADH** | Desmopressin (see DI section) | Electrolytes, urine output |

> **CRITICAL:** In secondary hypothyroidism, levothyroxine dosing must be guided by free T4 levels (target upper-normal third of reference range), NOT TSH. The pituitary cannot mount an appropriate TSH response.

### Order of Replacement
1. Glucocorticoids (ACTH) FIRST — thyroxine alone can precipitate adrenal crisis
2. Then thyroid hormone (TSH)
3. Then sex steroids (LH/FSH)
4. GH replacement last (after euthyroid and glucocorticoid-replete)

## 5.4 Diabetes Insipidus (DI)

### Overview
DI is characterized by hypotonic polyuria (>3 liters/day) and polydipsia due to impaired antidiuretic hormone (ADH/AVP) secretion (central DI) or renal resistance to ADH (nephrogenic DI) [^17^][^18^].

### Classification and Causes

| Type | Mechanism | Common Causes |
|------|-----------|--------------|
| **Central DI** | Inadequate ADH secretion from posterior pituitary | Pituitary surgery (most common), tumors (craniopharyngioma, germinoma), trauma, infiltrative (LCH, sarcoid), idiopathic, genetic (AVP mutations) |
| **Nephrogenic DI** | Renal resistance to ADH | Lithium (most common drug cause), congenital (AVPR2/AQP2 mutations), hypercalcemia, hypokalemia, post-obstructive uropathy, amyloidosis |
| **Primary polydipsia** | Excessive fluid intake suppressing ADH | Psychiatric disorders, medications, hypothalamic lesions affecting thirst |
| **Gestational DI** | Placental vasopressinase degrades ADH | Usually in second/third trimester; resolves postpartum |

### Diagnosis

**Water Deprivation Test (Gold Standard):**

| Phase | Procedure | Interpretation |
|-------|-----------|----------------|
| **Deprivation phase** | Withhold all fluids; measure urine and plasma osmolality hourly; weight every 1-2 hours; stop if >3% weight loss, orthostatic hypotension, or urine osmolality plateaus | |
| **Central DI** | Urine osmolality remains <300 mOsm/kg despite dehydration | Inability to concentrate urine |
| **Nephrogenic DI** | Urine osmolality remains <300 mOsm/kg despite dehydration | Same as central |
| **Primary polydipsia** | Urine osmolality >500 mOsm/kg after deprivation | Ability to concentrate urine |
| **Desmopressin phase** | After max concentration reached, give desmopressin 2 mcg SC; measure urine osmolality at 1-2 hours | |
| **Central DI** | Urine osmolality increases >50% after desmopressin | Responsive to ADH replacement |
| **Nephrogenic DI** | Minimal response (<50% increase) | Renal resistance to ADH |
| **Primary polydipsia** | Minimal response (but already concentrated) | Already maximally concentrated |

**Emerging biomarker:** Plasma copeptin (stable C-terminal fragment of pro-AVP) — levels are low in central DI, elevated in nephrogenic DI.

**MRI:** Assess pituitary/hypothalamus; absent posterior pituitary "bright spot" on T1-weighted images suggests central DI.

### Treatment [^17^][^18^]

#### Central DI

| Agent | Dosing | Notes |
|-------|--------|-------|
| **Desmopressin (DDAVP)** | Oral: 0.1-0.2 mg BID-TID; Intranasal: 10-40 mcg/day; Sublingual: 60-240 mcg BID | Synthetic ADH analogue; longer half-life than natural ADH |
| | | Monitor for hyponatremia — allow periodic "breakthrough" urination by skipping dose 1-2x/week |
| | | Maintain free access to water |
| **Chlorpropamide** | 125-250 mg daily | Potentiates ADH effect; risk of hypoglycemia; rarely used |
| **Carbamazepine** | 200-600 mg/day | Stimulates ADH release; use if partial DI |

#### Nephrogenic DI

| Treatment | Mechanism | Notes |
|-----------|-----------|-------|
| **Stop offending drug** (e.g., lithium) | | If possible; often not feasible in bipolar disorder |
| **Thiazide diuretics** | Paradoxical antidiuresis (mild volume contraction enhances proximal Na/water reabsorption) | Hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg BID; most effective treatment |
| **Low-solute diet** | Reduce protein and sodium intake | Decreases obligatory urine output |
| **Amiloride** | Blocks lithium entry into collecting duct cells | For lithium-induced DI; lithium-sparing |
| **NSAIDs (indomethacin)** | Reduces prostaglandin-mediated antagonism of ADH | Moderate efficacy; GI side effects |
| **High-dose desmopressin** | Partial response in partial NDI | May help some patients |

## 5.5 SIADH (Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone)

### Overview
SIADH is the most common cause of euvolemic hyponatremia, characterized by inappropriate ADH secretion leading to water retention and dilutional hyponatremia [^19^]. It accounts for approximately 30% of hyponatremia cases in hospitalized patients.

### Diagnostic Criteria
All criteria must be met:
1. Hyponatremia (serum Na <135 mEq/L)
2. Inappropriately concentrated urine (urine osmolality >100 mOsm/kg, typically >300)
3. Euvolemic clinical status (no edema, normal skin turgor, no orthostasis)
4. Normal thyroid function and adrenal function (exclude hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency)
5. Normal effective arterial blood volume (no edema-forming states)
6. Urine sodium >30 mEq/L (on normal salt intake)
7. No recent diuretic use

### Causes

| Category | Causes |
|----------|--------|
| **Malignancy** | Small cell lung cancer (most common), head/neck cancer, lymphoma, thymoma, mesothelioma |
| **CNS disorders** | Stroke (subarachnoid hemorrhage, SAH most classic), infection (meningitis, encephalitis), trauma, tumors, Guillain-Barre syndrome |
| **Pulmonary disease** | Pneumonia (bacterial, viral), TB, positive pressure ventilation, asthma, COPD |
| **Medications** | SSRIs, antipsychotics, carbamazepine, MDMA (ecstasy), NSAIDs, vincristine, cyclophosphamide, desmopressin |
| **Pain, nausea, stress** | Postoperative state (most common hospital-associated cause) |
| **Idiopathic** | More common in elderly |

### Treatment [^19^][^20^]

**General principles:**
- Rate of correction matters: Max 8-10 mEq/L in first 24 hours; 18 mEq/L in 48 hours
- Risk of osmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS) if corrected too rapidly
- Risk factors for ODS: Na <120, malnutrition, alcoholism, liver disease, hypokalemia

#### By Severity

| Severity | Sodium | Treatment |
|----------|--------|-----------|
| **Mild/asymptomatic** | >125 mEq/L | Fluid restriction 500-1000 mL/day (first-line); treat underlying cause |
| **Moderate** | 120-125 mEq/L | Fluid restriction + oral urea 15-30 g/day OR tolvaptan 7.5-15 mg daily |
| **Severe/symptomatic** | <120 mEq/L OR symptoms | 3% hypertonic saline 100-150 mL bolus (may repeat 1-2x); goal: increase Na by 4-6 mEq/L acutely to stop symptoms; then slow correction |

#### Specific Treatments

| Treatment | Mechanism | Dosing | Notes |
|-----------|-----------|--------|-------|
| **Fluid restriction** | Reduces free water intake | 500-1000 mL/day all fluids | First-line for mild SIADH; often poorly tolerated |
| **Oral urea** | Osmotic diuresis | 15-30 g/day | Highly effective, safe, inexpensive; may be preferred first-line [^20^] |
| **Tolvaptan** | V2 receptor antagonist | 7.5-15 mg daily; titrate to 60 mg | Onset 2-4 hours; monitor for overly rapid correction; expensive; hepatotoxicity risk (monitor LFTs) |
| **Loop diuretic + salt tablets** | Free water clearance + solute load | Furosemide 20-40 mg + NaCl 1-2 g TID | Alternative if other options fail |
| **Demeclocycline** | Inhibits ADH action on collecting duct | 600-1200 mg/day | Less commonly used due to toxicity, photosensitivity, variable response |

#### Overcorrection Management
If sodium corrects >8-10 mEq/L in 24 hours:
- Give D5W to re-lower sodium gradually
- Consider desmopressin 2 mcg SC to prevent further water loss
- Do NOT give free water alone if urine output very high

### Osmotic Demyelination Syndrome (ODS)

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Pathophysiology** | Rapid correction of chronic hyponatremia causes osmotic stress on brain cells; demyelination of pons (central pontine myelinolysis) and extrapontine areas |
| **Risk factors** | Na <105, malnutrition, alcoholism, liver disease, hypokalemia, rapid correction >10-12 mEq/L/24h |
| **Clinical features** | Altered mental status, dysarthria, dysphagia, spastic quadriparesis, pseudobulbar palsy, locked-in syndrome |
| **Onset** | 2-7 days after rapid correction (delay is characteristic) |
| **Prevention** | Slow correction: max 8 mEq/L/24h, 18 mEq/L/48h |
| **Treatment** | Re-lowering serum sodium with D5W + desmopressin may help if caught early; otherwise supportive; may improve over months |

# Chapter 6: Metabolic Syndrome and Obesity

## 6.1 Metabolic Syndrome — Overview

Metabolic syndrome is a cluster of interrelated metabolic risk factors that increase the risk of cardiovascular disease and type 2 diabetes. Insulin resistance and central obesity are the central pathophysiologic features [^21^]. It affects approximately 20-25% of adults worldwide.

### Diagnostic Criteria

#### NCEP ATP III (Revised 2005) — Most Widely Used [^21^]
**Requires >=3 of 5 criteria:**

| Component | Cutoff Value |
|-----------|-------------|
| Elevated waist circumference | Men: >102 cm (40 in); Women: >88 cm (35 in) |
| Elevated triglycerides | >=150 mg/dL or on drug therapy |
| Reduced HDL cholesterol | Men: <40 mg/dL; Women: <50 mg/dL or on drug therapy |
| Elevated blood pressure | >=130/85 mmHg or on antihypertensive therapy |
| Elevated fasting glucose | >=100 mg/dL or on drug therapy |

#### IDF Criteria [^21^]
- **Central obesity required** (ethnicity-specific waist circumference)
- **Plus >=2 of 4:** Elevated TG, reduced HDL, elevated BP, elevated glucose

| Ethnicity | Male WC Cutoff | Female WC Cutoff |
|-----------|---------------|-----------------|
| Europid | >=94 cm | >=80 cm |
| South Asian, Chinese | >=90 cm | >=80 cm |
| Japanese | >=85 cm | >=90 cm |
| African, Middle Eastern | >=94 cm | >=80 cm |

#### AACE Diagnostic Criteria (2020)
- Diagnoses "insulin resistance syndrome" rather than metabolic syndrome
- Emphasizes detection and management of insulin resistance

### Cardiovascular Risk Associated with Metabolic Syndrome [^21^]
- **2-fold increased risk** of cardiovascular disease
- **5-fold increased risk** of type 2 diabetes
- CVD risk is greater than the sum of individual components
- Associated with pro-inflammatory state (elevated CRP, IL-6, TNF-alpha)
- Associated with pro-thrombotic state (elevated fibrinogen, PAI-1)
- Associated with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)

---

## 6.2 Management of Metabolic Syndrome

### Lifestyle Modification (First-Line for All Components)

| Intervention | Target | Expected Benefit |
|-------------|--------|-----------------|
| **Weight loss** | 7-10% of body weight | Improves all components |
| **Physical activity** | >=150 min/week moderate-intensity aerobic | Improves insulin sensitivity, BP, lipids |
| **Resistance training** | 2-3x/week | Improves insulin sensitivity |
| **Dietary changes** | Mediterranean, DASH, or low-carbohydrate; limit refined carbs, sugar-sweetened beverages | Improves weight, glucose, TG, HDL |
| **Smoking cessation** | Complete | Reduces CVD risk independent of metabolic syndrome |
| **Alcohol moderation** | <=1 drink/day (women), <=2 (men) | Reduces TG, BP, weight |

### Pharmacologic Treatment of Components

| Component | Target | Pharmacotherapy |
|-----------|--------|----------------|
| **Hypertension** | <130/80 mmHg | ACE-I or ARB preferred (insulin-sensitizing); add thiazide, CCB as needed |
| **Dyslipidemia** | Statin primary | Moderate-high intensity statin; add ezetimibe if needed; consider fibrate if TG >500 |
| **Hyperglycemia** | HbA1c <7% if diabetic | Metformin first-line; SGLT2-i, GLP-1 RA for CV/weight benefit |
| **Hypertriglyceridemia** | TG <150 mg/dL | Icosapent ethyl (EPA) 4 g/day if ASCVD or diabetes with risk factors (REDUCE-IT) |
| **Aspirin prophylaxis** | Consider in high 10-year ASCVD risk | 81 mg daily (weigh bleeding risk) |

### Monitoring
- Annual reassessment of all metabolic syndrome components
- ASCVD risk calculation using PREVENT calculator (2024)
- Periodic HbA1c if prediabetic (annually)
- Weight and waist circumference at each visit

- **Waist circumference:** Visceral adiposity better predicts metabolic risk than BMI alone
- **Waist-to-height ratio:** Target <0.5 (simpler than BMI; works across ethnicities)
- **Body composition analysis:** DEXA, bioelectrical impedance
- **Obesity-related complications:** T2DM, hypertension, dyslipidemia, OSA, NAFLD, osteoarthritis, depression, certain cancers

## 6.4 Obesity Treatment — Comprehensive Approach

### Lifestyle Intervention (Foundation)
- **Caloric deficit:** 500-750 kcal/day deficit; 1-2 lb/week loss
- **Physical activity:** 150-300 min/week moderate intensity aerobic + resistance training
- **Behavioral strategies:** Self-monitoring, goal setting, cognitive behavioral therapy, motivational interviewing
- **Expected outcome:** 5-10% weight loss at 6 months; maintenance is the primary challenge
- **Intensive behavioral therapy (IBT):** >=14 sessions in 6 months; more effective than brief counseling

### Anti-Obesity Pharmacotherapy [^23^][^24^]

#### Criteria for Pharmacotherapy
- BMI >=30 kg/m2, OR
- BMI >=27 kg/m2 with obesity-related comorbidity (hypertension, T2DM, dyslipidemia, OSA, NAFLD)

#### FDA-Approved Anti-Obesity Medications

| Drug | Mechanism | Dosing | Avg Weight Loss | Key Side Effects | Contraindications |
|------|-----------|--------|----------------|-----------------|-------------------|
| **Orlistat** | Lipase inhibitor | 120 mg TID with meals | 3-5% at 1 year | GI (steatorrhea, fecal urgency, oily spotting), fat-soluble vitamin malabsorption | Chronic malabsorption, cholestasis |
| **Naltrexone/Bupropion** | Opioid antagonist / dopamine-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor | 8/90 mg to 32/360 mg (titrated) | 5-8% | Nausea, constipation, headache, insomnia, BP elevation | Uncontrolled HTN, seizure disorder, chronic opioid use, eating disorder, MAOIs |
| **Phentermine-Topiramate ER** | Sympathomimetic / anticonvulsant | 3.75/23 to 15/92 mg daily | 8-10% | Paresthesia, dry mouth, constipation, insomnia, dysgeusia, cognitive effects, teratogenicity | Pregnancy, hyperthyroidism, glaucoma, MAOIs |
| **Liraglutide 3.0 mg** | GLP-1 receptor agonist | 0.6 mg daily, titrate to 3.0 mg | 10-12% | Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, constipation | MEN2, medullary thyroid CA, pregnancy |
| **Semaglutide 2.4 mg** | GLP-1 receptor agonist | 0.25 mg weekly, titrate to 2.4 mg | 15-17% (STEP trials) | Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, constipation | MEN2, medullary thyroid CA, pregnancy |
| **Tirzepatide** | GIP/GLP-1 dual agonist | 2.5 mg weekly, titrate to 5, 10, 15 mg | 20-22% (SURMOUNT trials) | Nausea, diarrhea, vomiting, constipation | MEN2, medullary thyroid CA, pregnancy |

#### Comparison of GLP-1 RA and Tirzepatide for Obesity [^24^]

| Feature | Semaglutide 2.4 mg | Tirzepatide 15 mg |
|---------|-------------------|-------------------|
| **Avg weight loss** | 15-17% | 20-22% |
| **HbA1c lowering** | 1.5-2.0% | 2.0-2.4% |
| **CV outcomes** | SELECT: 20% MACE reduction in overweight/obese with CVD | SURMOUNT-MC: ongoing |
| **GI side effects** | Nausea ~45%, vomiting ~25%, diarrhea ~30% | Similar; dose-dependent |
| **Injection frequency** | Weekly | Weekly |
| **Titration** | 4-week intervals | 4-week intervals |
| **FDA approval** | Wegovy (obesity) + Ozempic (T2DM) | Zepbound (obesity) + Mounjaro (T2DM) |

**Common class side effects (GLP-1 RA, tirzepatide):**
- GI effects: nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, constipation (dose-dependent; usually improve over weeks)
- Gallbladder disease: cholelithiasis (rapid weight loss contributes)
- Injection site reactions
- Pancreatitis: Rare; discontinue if suspected
- **Thyroid C-cell tumors:** Observed in rodents; contraindicated in personal/family history of medullary thyroid carcinoma or MEN2

**Titration strategy for GLP-1 RA/tirzepatide to minimize GI side effects:**
- Slow titration (every 4 weeks) is essential
- Eat smaller meals, stop when full
- Avoid high-fat meals if nausea prominent
- Consider antiemetics temporarily if needed
- Temporary dose reduction if intolerable side effects

#### Selection Guide for Anti-Obesity Medications

| Patient Profile | Preferred Agent | Rationale |
|----------------|----------------|-----------|
| **T2DM + obesity** | Semaglutide or tirzepatide | Dual glycemic/weight benefit; CV protection |
| **Obesity + high CVD risk** | Semaglutide 2.4 mg | SELECT trial: 20% MACE reduction |
| **Maximal weight loss desired** | Tirzepatide 15 mg | Greatest weight loss in trials |
| **Cost-sensitive** | Orlistat or phentermine-topiramate | Generic options available |
| **Binge eating disorder** | Naltrexone/bupropion | May reduce cravings |
| **Cannot tolerate injections** | Phentermine-topiramate, naltrexone/bupropion, or orlistat | Oral options |
| **Seizure disorder** | Avoid bupropion | Lowers seizure threshold |

## 6.5 Bariatric/Metabolic Surgery [^22^][^25^]

### Indications (ASMBS/IFSO 2022 Updated Guidelines)

| BMI Category | Recommendation |
|-------------|----------------|
| **>=35 kg/m2** | Recommended regardless of comorbidities |
| **30-34.9 kg/m2** | Considered for metabolic disease (especially T2DM) or failure of nonsurgical methods |
| **>=27.5 kg/m2 (Asian)** | Should be offered metabolic/bariatric surgery |
| **Adolescents** | BMI >120% of 95th percentile with major comorbidity OR >140% of 95th percentile |

### Procedure Types

| Procedure | Mechanism | % of Procedures | Avg Weight Loss | T2DM Remission | Notes |
|-----------|-----------|----------------|----------------|---------------|-------|
| **Sleeve gastrectomy (SG)** | Restrictive; removes ~80% of stomach (fundus) | ~60% | 25-30% | 60-70% | Most common; irreversible; simpler technically |
| **Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB)** | Restrictive + malabsorptive | ~20% | 30-35% | 70-80% | Gold standard for metabolic disease;Dumping risk |
| **One-anastomosis gastric bypass (OAGB)** | Similar to RYGB, single anastomosis | Increasing | 30-35% | 70-80% | Simpler than RYGB; bile reflux concern |
| **BPD/DS** | Most malabsorptive | ~2% | 35-40% | 80-90% | Most effective for severe obesity; highest nutritional risk |

### Outcomes
- **Weight loss:** 25-35% total body weight loss at 1-2 years; 20-30% maintained at 10 years
- **T2DM remission:** 60-80% at 1-2 years; durability varies with diabetes duration (better if <5 years)
- **Mortality:** Perioperative <0.1-0.5%; 30-day mortality ~0.03-0.2%
- **Comorbidity improvement:** Hypertension resolves in ~50%, dyslipidemia in ~60%, OSA in ~75%
- **Mortality reduction:** 30-40% reduction in all-cause mortality at 10 years compared to matched non-surgical controls

### Risks and Complications

| Early Complications | Late Complications |
|--------------------|-------------------|
| Anastomotic leak (<1-2%) | Nutritional deficiencies (B12, iron, calcium, vitamin D, folate, thiamine) |
| Bleeding (<2%) | Dumping syndrome (RYGB) |
| Pulmonary embolism (<1%) | Marginal ulcers (RYGB) |
| Infection | Strictures, bowel obstruction (internal hernia) |
| | Weight regain (10-20% at 10 years) |
| | Gallstones (rapid weight loss) |

### Long-Term Follow-Up [^22^]
- **Lifelong vitamin/mineral supplementation:**
  - Vitamin B12: 1000 mcg/day (oral) or monthly injections
  - Iron: Ferrous sulfate 325 mg + vitamin C (especially menstruating women)
  - Calcium citrate 1200-1500 mg/day + vitamin D 3000 IU/day
  - Folate, thiamine as indicated
  - Consider fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) for malabsorptive procedures
- **Annual metabolic monitoring:** CBC, CMP, iron studies, vitamin B12, folate, 25(OH)D, bone density
- **Dumping syndrome management:** Small frequent meals, avoid simple sugars, fiber supplementation
- **Alcohol/drug use counseling:** Transfer addiction risk ("addiction transfer")
- **Pregnancy:** Delay 12-18 months post-surgery; contraception essential; increased risk of micronutrient deficiencies

### Emerging: Endoscopic Bariatric Procedures
- Intragastric balloons (temporary, 6 months)
- Endoscopic sleeve gastroplasty (ESG)
- Lower efficacy than surgery but less invasive

## 6.6 Red Flags Requiring Immediate Referral

- BMI >40 with comorbidities
- Rapidly progressive obesity in childhood (evaluate for genetic/syndromic causes)
- Suspicion of secondary obesity (Cushing's syndrome, hypothyroidism, hypothalamic obesity)
- Severe OSA, obesity hypoventilation syndrome (Pickwickian syndrome)
- New-onset diabetes with severe obesity
- Complications requiring urgent management: acute pancreatitis from hypertriglyceridemia, acute cholecystitis, deep vein thrombosis

# Chapter 7: Dyslipidemia

## 7.1 Overview

Dyslipidemia encompasses abnormal lipid levels including elevated LDL-C, elevated triglycerides, low HDL-C, and elevated non-HDL-C or apolipoprotein B. It is a major modifiable risk factor for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), which remains the leading cause of death worldwide [^26^]. Effective lipid management reduces cardiovascular events by 20-40%.

### Lipoprotein Classification and Function

| Lipoprotein | Source | Function | Atherogenic |
|-------------|--------|----------|-------------|
| **Chylomicrons** | Intestine | Transport dietary TG | No (too large to penetrate endothelium) |
| **VLDL** | Liver | Transport endogenous TG | Mild |
| **IDL** | VLDL remnant | Intermediate | Yes |
| **LDL** | VLDL metabolism | Deliver cholesterol to peripheral tissues | **Primary atherogenic particle** |
| **HDL** | Liver/intestine | Reverse cholesterol transport | Protective ("good cholesterol") |
| **Lp(a)** | Liver | Modified LDL; pro-inflammatory, pro-thrombotic | Yes; largely genetically determined |

---

## 7.2 ASCVD Risk Assessment

### PREVENT Calculator (2024) [^26^]
The PREVENT equations have replaced the Pooled Cohort Equations (PCE) in the 2026 ACC/AHA Guidelines. Key improvements:
- Includes chronic kidney disease, BMI, HbA1c, social determinants of health
- Estimates 10-year and 30-year risk
- Separates risk for ASCVD, heart failure, and total CVD
- Race-free (does not use race as a variable)

### Risk Categories

| Category | 10-Year ASCVD Risk | Management Approach |
|----------|-------------------|--------------------|
| **Low risk** | <5% | Lifestyle modification; statin not generally indicated |
| **Borderline risk** | 5-7.5% | Risk discussion; consider statin if risk-enhancing factors present |
| **Intermediate risk** | >7.5-20% | Statin indicated (moderate intensity); risk-enhancing factors guide intensity |
| **High risk** | >20% | High-intensity statin indicated; consider combination therapy |

### Risk-Enhancing Factors (Favor Statin Therapy)
- Family history of premature ASCVD (male <55, female <65)
- LDL-C >=160 mg/dL (persistent)
- Lp(a) >=50 mg/dL (or >=125 nmol/L)
- ApoB >=130 mg/dL
- Metabolic syndrome
- Chronic kidney disease (eGFR 15-59)
- History of preeclampsia or premature menopause (<40 years)
- High-risk ethnicity (South Asian ancestry)
- Inflammatory diseases (HIV, psoriasis, RA, SLE)
- Elevated hs-CRP >=2 mg/L
- ABI <0.9

---

## 7.3 LDL-C Treatment by Clinical Category [^26^]

### Severe Hypercholesterolemia (LDL-C >=190 mg/dL)
- **High-intensity statin** regardless of 10-year risk
- **Goal:** LDL-C <100 mg/dL (no ASCVD); <70 mg/dL (with ASCVD or confirmed FH)
- Add ezetimibe, PCSK9 mAb, bempedoic acid, or inclisiran as needed
- **Evaluate for familial hypercholesterolemia** (see Section 7.6)

### Diabetes (Age 40-75 Years)
- Without ASCVD or multiple risk factors: **Moderate-intensity statin**; goal LDL-C <100, non-HDL-C <130
- With multiple risk factors (long duration, albuminuria, retinopathy, neuropathy, eGFR <60): **High-intensity statin**; goal LDL-C <70, non-HDL-C <100
- With ASCVD: Very high-risk; goal LDL-C <55

### Secondary Prevention (Established ASCVD)
- **High-intensity statin:** Goal >=50% reduction, LDL-C <70 mg/dL
- If LDL-C >=70 on statin: Add ezetimibe
- If LDL-C >=70 on statin + ezetimibe: Add PCSK9 mAb or bempedoic acid
- **Very high risk** (recurrent events, polyvascular disease): LDL-C <55 mg/dL

### Age >75 Years
- Continue statin if tolerating
- May initiate moderate-intensity after shared decision-making if life expectancy >2.5 years
- Consider life expectancy, frailty, comorbidities, patient preferences

### LDL-C Thresholds and Goals (Comparison with ESC/EAS 2019)

| Risk Category | ACC/AHA 2026 LDL-C Goal | ESC/EAS 2019 LDL-C Goal |
|--------------|------------------------|------------------------|
| Very high risk | <70 mg/dL (consider <55) | <55 mg/dL (<1.4 mmol/L) |
| High risk | <100 mg/dL | <70 mg/dL (<1.8 mmol/L) |
| Moderate risk | <130 mg/dL (consider <100) | <100 mg/dL (<2.6 mmol/L) |
| Low risk | Lifestyle focus | <116 mg/dL (<3.0 mmol/L) |

## 7.4 Statin Therapy

### High-Intensity Statins (Reduce LDL-C >=50%)

| Drug | Dose | Notes |
|------|------|-------|
| **Atorvastatin** | 40-80 mg daily | Most widely used; generic; CYP3A4 substrate |
| **Rosuvastatin** | 20-40 mg daily | More potent mg-for-mg; less CYP metabolism; Asian patients need lower starting dose |

### Moderate-Intensity Statins (Reduce LDL-C 30-49%)

| Drug | Dose | Notes |
|------|------|-------|
| **Atorvastatin** | 10-20 mg | |
| **Rosuvastatin** | 5-10 mg | |
| **Simvastatin** | 20-40 mg | Many drug interactions; FDA limits 80 mg dose |
| **Pravastatin** | 40-80 mg | Hydrophilic; fewer muscle side effects; fewer drug interactions |
| **Pitavastatin** | 2-4 mg | Minimal CYP metabolism; good for complex medication regimens |
| **Fluvastatin XL** | 80 mg | Less commonly used |

### Statin Administration and Monitoring

| Parameter | Recommendation |
|-----------|---------------|
| **Timing** | Any time of day (atorvastatin, rosuvastatin have long half-lives); evening for simvastatin, pravastatin |
| **Baseline labs** | Lipid panel, ALT, creatine kinase (if muscle symptoms), TSH (hypothyroidism can cause myopathy) |
| **Follow-up lipid panel** | 4-12 weeks after initiation or dose change; then every 3-12 months |
| **Liver enzymes** | Check at baseline; repeat if symptoms; routine monitoring not necessary (2016 FDA change) |
| **CK** | Only if muscle symptoms; not routinely monitored |

### Statin Side Effects and Management

| Side Effect | Frequency | Management |
|-------------|-----------|------------|
| **Myalgia** (muscle aches without CK elevation) | 5-10% | Check vitamin D, TSH; trial of alternate-day dosing; switch to hydrophilic statin (pravastatin, rosuvastatin); add non-statin |
| **Myopathy** (muscle symptoms + CK >10x ULN) | <0.1% | Discontinue statin; rechallenge with lower dose or different statin once resolved |
| **Rhabdomyolysis** | <0.01% | Emergency; discontinue statin; IV fluids; monitor renal function, CK |
| **Elevated transaminases** (>3x ULN) | 0.5-2% | Reduce dose or discontinue if persistent; usually transient |
| **New-onset diabetes** | Small absolute increase | Benefits far outweigh risks; glucose monitoring; lifestyle counseling |

### Statin Drug Interactions
- **CYP3A4 substrates (atorvastatin, simvastatin):** Avoid strong CYP3A4 inhibitors (clarithromycin, itraconazole, ketoconazole, HIV protease inhibitors, cobicistat, grapefruit juice >1 quart/day)
- **Cyclosporine:** Increases all statin levels (avoid simvastatin; limit atorvastatin to 10 mg)
- **Fibrates:** Gemfibrozil increases myopathy risk significantly (avoid with simvastatin); fenofibrate safer
- **Warfarin:** Variable interaction; monitor INR closely

### Statin Intolerance Management
- Confirm true intolerance (vs. non-adherence, nocebo effect)
- Rule out secondary causes: hypothyroidism, vitamin D deficiency, drug interactions, alcohol, excessive exercise
- Try different statin: 70-90% of patients can tolerate a different statin
- Hydrophilic statins (pravastatin, rosuvastatin) may have fewer muscle symptoms
- Lower dose or alternate-day dosing (rosuvastatin and atorvastatin have long half-lives)
- Non-daily dosing can maintain ~70% of daily-dose LDL reduction
- Add non-statin therapy (ezetimibe, bempedoic acid) to lower statin dose

## 7.5 Non-Statin LDL-Lowering Therapies

### Ezetimibe

| Parameter | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| **Mechanism** | Inhibits NPC1L1 transporter in intestinal brush border; blocks cholesterol absorption |
| **LDL-C reduction** | Additional ~18-25% |
| **Dosing** | 10 mg once daily |
| **Key evidence** | IMPROVE-IT: Added to simvastatin post-ACS, reduced CV events by 6.4% |
| **Side effects** | Generally well tolerated; myalgia (rare), elevated LFTs (rare) |
| **Notes** | First-line add-on to statin; inexpensive generic |

### PCSK9 Inhibitors (Monoclonal Antibodies)

| Parameter | Alirocumab | Evolocumab |
|-----------|-----------|-----------|
| **Mechanism** | Monoclonal antibody against PCSK9; prevents PCSK9-LDL receptor degradation | Same |
| **Dosing** | 75-150 mg SC every 2 weeks | 140 mg every 2 weeks OR 420 mg monthly |
| **LDL-C reduction** | 50-60% additional | 50-60% additional |
| **Key evidence** | ODYSSEY: 15% reduction in MACE | FOURIER: 20% reduction in MACE |
| **Administration** | Pen injector; patient self-injection | Same |
| **Side effects** | Injection site reactions (7%), nasopharyngitis | Similar |
| **Cost** | High; prior authorization required; patient assistance programs available | High |
| **Indications** | FH, ASCVD on maximally tolerated statin + ezetimibe with LDL >=70 | Same |

### Inclisiran (siRNA)

| Parameter | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| **Mechanism** | Small interfering RNA targeting PCSK9 mRNA in hepatocytes; reduces PCSK9 synthesis |
| **Dosing** | 284 mg SC on day 1, day 90, then every 6 months |
| **LDL-C reduction** | ~50% |
| **Advantage** | Infrequent dosing (biannual) — may improve adherence |
| **Evidence** | ORION trials; CV outcomes trials ongoing |
| **Administration** | Healthcare provider administered |

### Bempedoic Acid

| Parameter | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| **Mechanism** | ACL (ATP citrate lyase) inhibitor — upstream from HMG-CoA reductase |
| **Dosing** | 180 mg once daily |
| **LDL-C reduction** | ~15-20% |
| **Key evidence** | CLEAR Outcomes: Reduced MACE by 13% in statin-intolerant patients |
| **Side effects** | Hyperuricemia (gout risk), tendon rupture risk, elevated LFTs |
| **Advantage** | Oral; does not cause myopathy (not activated in muscle); ideal for statin-intolerant patients |

### Combination Therapy Strategies

| Scenario | Combination |
|----------|-------------|
| ASCVD, LDL >=70 on high-intensity statin | Add ezetimibe |
| ASCVD, LDL >=70 on statin + ezetimibe | Add PCSK9 mAb or inclisiran |
| Statin-intolerant, needs moderate LDL reduction | Bempedoic acid +/- ezetimibe |
| Statin-intolerant, needs significant LDL reduction | Bempedoic acid + ezetimibe |
| Maximal oral therapy, LDL still elevated | PCSK9 mAb + statin + ezetimibe |

## 7.6 Familial Hypercholesterolemia (FH) [^26^][^27^]

### Definition
A genetic disorder of LDL metabolism resulting in lifelong elevated LDL-C and premature ASCVD.

| Type | Characteristics | Genetic Basis |
|------|----------------|---------------|
| **Heterozygous FH (HeFH)** | LDL-C >190 mg/dL (untreated adults); tendon xanthomas; premature ASCVD (men <55, women <65) | One pathogenic variant in LDLR, APOB, or PCSK9 |
| **Homozygous FH (HoFH)** | LDL-C >500 mg/dL (untreated); xanthomas by age 10; ASCVD in childhood/adolescence | Biallelic pathogenic variants; very rare (~1 in 300,000) |

### Diagnosis — Simon Broome Criteria

| Definite FH | Possible FH |
|-------------|-------------|
| 1. TC >290 mg/dL or LDL-C >190 in adult (TC >260 or LDL >155 in child) | 1. TC >290 or LDL >190 in adult |
| 2. Tendon xanthomas in patient or first-degree relative | 2. Family history of premature ASCVD |
| **Both required** | **Both required** |

- **DNA-based evidence** of an FH mutation in LDLR, APOB, PCSK9, or LDLRAP1 confirms diagnosis

### Physical Examination Findings
- **Tendon xanthomas:** Achilles tendon, extensor tendons of hands, patellar tendon (pathognomonic)
- **Corneal arcus:** In patients <45 years (arcus juvenilis)
- **Xanthelasma:** Less specific; can occur without FH

### Treatment

| Type | Treatment | Goals |
|------|-----------|-------|
| **HeFH** | High-intensity statin + ezetimibe; add PCSK9 mAb if needed; consider bempedoic acid | LDL-C <70-100 depending on risk; >=50% reduction |
| **HoFH** | Maximal oral therapy + PCSK9 mAb + evinacumab (ANGPTL3 mAb); lomitapide (MTP inhibitor); lipoprotein apheresis | LDL-C <70; aggressive reduction |

**Cascade screening:** Once FH identified, test all first-degree relatives (genetic testing preferred; if mutation known, cascade testing is highly specific and cost-effective).

## 7.7 Hypertriglyceridemia Management [^26^]

### Classification

| Severity | Triglyceride Level | Clinical Significance |
|----------|-------------------|----------------------|
| Normal | <150 mg/dL | |
| Mild | 150-199 mg/dL | Lifestyle modification |
| Moderate | 200-499 mg/dL | Consider pharmacotherapy if ASCVD risk |
| Severe | 500-999 mg/dL | Pancreatitis risk; pharmacotherapy indicated |
| Very severe | >=1000 mg/dL | High pancreatitis risk; urgent management |

### Management by Severity

| Level | Management |
|-------|-----------|
| **150-499 mg/dL** | Lifestyle modification (weight loss, exercise, limit alcohol, reduce refined carbs); statin if ASCVD risk; consider icosapent ethyl 4 g/day if ASCVD or diabetes with risk factors (REDUCE-IT trial) |
| **500-999 mg/dL** | Very low-fat diet (<15% calories from fat); eliminate alcohol; fibrate or omega-3 fatty acids; goal: reduce TG <500 |
| **>=1000 mg/dL** | Fibrate + omega-3 fatty acids; evaluate for familial chylomicronemia syndrome (LPL deficiency); urgent management to prevent pancreatitis |

### Pharmacotherapy for Hypertriglyceridemia

| Drug | Mechanism | TG Reduction | Notes |
|------|-----------|-------------|-------|
| **Fibrates** (fenofibrate, gemfibrozil) | PPAR-alpha agonist | 30-50% | Fenofibrate preferred (fewer drug interactions); gemfibrozil increases myopathy risk with statins |
| **Icosapent ethyl (EPA)** | High-purity EPA omega-3 | 20-30% | 4 g/day; REDUCE-IT: 25% CV event reduction in statin-treated patients with TG 135-499 |
| **Omega-3 carboxylic acids** | EPA + DHA | 20-30% | 4 g/day; STRENGTH trial did not show CV benefit |
| **Omega-3 ethyl esters** | EPA + DHA | 20-30% | Variable quality; prescription vs OTC |

> **Important note:** For patients with TG 135-499 mg/dL on statins with ASCVD or diabetes plus risk factors, icosapent ethyl 4 g/day is recommended (REDUCE-IT trial showed 25% relative risk reduction in ischemic events).

| HbA1c | If diabetic; screen if prediabetic |

| Vitamin D | If on statin with muscle symptoms |

### Medication Adherence Strategies
- Simplify regimen (once-daily dosing preferred)
- Use combination products if multiple medications needed
- Address cost concerns (generic statins are inexpensive; patient assistance for PCSK9 mAbs)
- Discuss benefits in absolute terms (number needed to treat)
- Address side effect concerns proactively
- Reinforce that statins are lifelong therapy (discontinuation leads to rebound in LDL-C and event rates)

- **Acute pancreatitis:** Severe epigastric pain radiating to back with TG >1000 mg/dL
- **Muscle pain with dark urine:** Possible rhabdomyolysis from statin (rare but emergency)
- **Chest pain, acute neurological symptoms:** Possible ASCVD event (dyslipidemia is a major risk factor)

# Chapter 8: Osteoporosis

## 8.1 Overview

Osteoporosis is a skeletal disorder characterized by compromised bone strength and microarchitectural deterioration of bone tissue, leading to increased fracture risk. It is defined by bone mineral density (BMD) T-score <=-2.5 at the lumbar spine, total hip, or femoral neck on DEXA scanning [^28^][^29^].

**Clinical significance:**
- Fragility fractures (hip, spine, wrist) are the main morbidity
- 1 in 3 women and 1 in 5 men over age 50 will experience an osteoporotic fracture
- Hip fracture mortality: 20-25% within 1 year
- Vertebral fractures: chronic pain, height loss, kyphosis, reduced pulmonary function
- After a first fragility fracture, risk of second fracture increases 2-5 fold

### Risk Factors

| Non-Modifiable | Modifiable |
|----------------|-----------|
| Advanced age | Low body weight/BMI <20 |
| Female sex | Smoking |
| Caucasian/Asian race | Excessive alcohol (>3 units/day) |
| Family history of hip fracture | Vitamin D deficiency |
| Prior fragility fracture | Calcium deficiency |
| Early menopause (<45 years) | Sedentary lifestyle |
| Genetic factors | Glucocorticoid use |
| Rheumatoid arthritis | Other medications (aromatase inhibitors, ADT, phenytoin, PPIs, SSRIs) |

---

## 8.2 Diagnosis

### DEXA Scanning (Dual-Energy X-ray Absorptiometry)

#### WHO Diagnostic Criteria (T-Scores)

| Category | T-Score | Clinical Significance |
|----------|---------|----------------------|
| Normal | >= -1.0 | No pharmacotherapy indicated |
| Osteopenia (low bone mass) | -1.0 to -2.5 | Consider treatment based on FRAX |
| Osteoporosis | <= -2.5 | Pharmacotherapy indicated |
| Severe osteoporosis | <= -2.5 plus fragility fracture | Aggressive treatment indicated |

> **T-score definition:** Number of standard deviations above or below the mean BMD of a young adult reference population (30-year-old, sex-matched).

#### Indications for DEXA Scanning
- All women >=65 years and men >=70 years
- Younger postmenopausal women (age 50-69) and men (50-69) with risk factors
- Adults with fragility fracture
- Adults with conditions or medications associated with bone loss (glucocorticoids >=7.5 mg prednisone equivalent for >=3 months)
- Anyone being considered for pharmacologic therapy
- Anyone receiving long-term glucocorticoid therapy
- Anyone with evidence of bone loss on radiographs

### FRAX Score [^28^]

The WHO Fracture Risk Assessment Tool estimates 10-year probability of major osteoporotic fracture (hip, spine, wrist, proximal humerus) and hip fracture specifically.

**Inputs:**
- Age, sex, weight, height
- Prior fracture (after age 50)
- Parent with hip fracture
- Current smoking
- Alcohol intake (>=3 units/day)
- Rheumatoid arthritis
- Secondary osteoporosis
- Femoral neck BMD (optional but recommended)

**Treatment thresholds (US-adapted):**
- 10-year major osteoporotic fracture risk >=20%: Treatment indicated
- 10-year hip fracture risk >=3%: Treatment indicated

**Important limitations:**
- Does not include all risk factors (falls, vitamin D status, bone turnover markers)
- Does not account for dose/duration of glucocorticoids
- Does not account for number of prior fractures
- Does not include lumbar spine BMD

### Vertebral Fracture Assessment (VFA)
- DEXA-based imaging of thoracic and lumbar spine
- Detects moderate-severe vertebral fractures
- Indicated when T-score <-1.0 with height loss, kyphosis, or glucocorticoid use

### Laboratory Workup for Secondary Causes

| Test | Rationale |
|------|-----------|
| CBC | Anemia (multiple myeloma, malabsorption) |
| CMP | Calcium, renal function, liver function |
| 25-hydroxyvitamin D | Vitamin D deficiency (extremely common) |
| TSH | Hyperthyroidism |
| PTH | Hyperparathyroidism |
| Testosterone (men) | Hypogonadism |
| Estradiol/FSH (women) | Hypoestrogenism |
| Celiac screening (tTG-IgA) | Celiac disease |
| SPEP/UPEP | Multiple myeloma |
| 24-hour urine calcium | Hypercalciuria, malabsorption |

### Secondary Causes to Evaluate
- Glucocorticoid use (most common medication cause)
- Hypogonadism (men and women)
- Hyperthyroidism/overtreatment with levothyroxine
- Hyperparathyroidism
- Celiac disease and other malabsorption
- Multiple myeloma
- Alcoholism
- Vitamin D deficiency
- Chronic kidney disease
- Rheumatoid arthritis

---

## 8.3 Treatment Guidelines (2024 UK / 2023 ACP) [^28^][^29^]

### Indications for Pharmacologic Treatment

| Indication | Evidence |
|-----------|----------|
| T-score <= -2.5 (osteoporosis) | Established indication |
| T-score -1.0 to -2.5 with FRAX above intervention threshold | Based on 10-year fracture risk |
| Prior fragility fracture of hip or spine | Very high risk; treat regardless of BMD |
| Long-term glucocorticoids (>=7.5 mg prednisone >=3 months) | Protect during glucocorticoid therapy |
| Multiple risk factors with osteopenia | Clinical judgment |

### First-Line Therapy: Bisphosphonates [^28^]

Bisphosphonates are the most extensively studied and cost-effective osteoporosis treatments. They inhibit osteoclast-mediated bone resorption.

| Drug | Dosing | Route | Key Notes |
|------|--------|-------|-----------|
| **Alendronate** | 70 mg once weekly | Oral | Most widely used; generic; take on empty stomach with full glass plain water; remain upright 30 min |
| **Risedronate** | 35 mg once weekly or 150 mg monthly | Oral | May have lower atypical fracture and ONJ risk than alendronate; remain upright 30 min |
| **Ibandronate** | 150 mg monthly or 3 mg IV q3 months | Oral or IV | Less robust hip fracture data; remain upright 60 min |
| **Zoledronic acid** | 5 mg IV once yearly | IV | Most potent; preferred if GI contraindication, compliance concern, or very high fracture risk |

#### Duration of Therapy

| Agent | Minimum Duration | Continue Beyond Minimum If: |
|-------|-----------------|---------------------------|
| Oral bisphosphonates | 5 years | Age >=70, prior hip/vertebral fracture, glucocorticoids >=7.5 mg/day, fracture on therapy, very low T-score |
| IV zoledronic acid | 3 years | Same high-risk criteria; continue >=6 years |

**Drug holiday:** After 5 years oral (or 3 years IV), reassess risk. If no longer high risk, can consider drug holiday with monitoring. If fracture occurs during therapy or risk remains high, continue.

#### Administration Instructions (Critical for Oral Agents)
1. Take first thing in the morning on empty stomach
2. Swallow with full glass (8 oz) of plain water only
3. Remain upright (sitting or standing) — alendronate/risedronate 30 minutes; ibandronate 60 minutes
4. Do not eat, drink, or take other medications during this period
5. Do not lie down

#### Side Effects

| Side Effect | Frequency | Notes |
|-------------|-----------|-------|
| GI upset, esophagitis | 5-10% | Worse if not taken properly; less with weekly dosing vs daily |
| Acute phase reaction (IV zoledronate) | 10-20% | Fever, myalgia, flu-like symptoms for 1-3 days; pre-treat with acetaminophen |
| Atypical femur fracture | Very rare | Subtrochanteric/diaphyseal; increased with long-term use; bilateral in ~50% |
| Osteonecrosis of jaw (ONJ) | ~1 in 10,000-100,000 patient-years | Associated with dental procedures, poor oral hygiene; rare with osteoporosis doses |
| Hypocalcemia | 1-2% (IV) | Ensure vitamin D repletion before administration |

#### Contraindications
- eGFR <30-35 mL/min (specific thresholds vary by agent)
- Esophageal disorders (stricture, achalasia, inability to stand upright — for oral agents)
- Hypocalcemia (correct before starting)
- Pregnancy/breastfeeding
- Inability to follow administration instructions

### Second-Line and Alternative Therapies

#### Denosumab [^28^]

| Parameter | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| **Mechanism** | Monoclonal antibody against RANKL; inhibits osteoclast activation |
| **Dosing** | 60 mg SC every 6 months |
| **Efficacy** | Reduces vertebral fractures by 68%, hip by 40%, non-vertebral by 20% |
| **Advantages** | No renal dose adjustment (unlike bisphosphonates); no GI side effects; can be given in CKD; more potent BMD increases than bisphosphonates |
| **Disadvantages** | More expensive; requires injection; no oral option |
| **CRITICAL WARNING** | Must NOT stop without transition plan — multiple vertebral fractures can occur within months of discontinuation due to rebound bone turnover |

**Transitioning from denosumab:** If stopping, give bisphosphonate (zoledronic acid 5 mg IV) 1-2 months before or at the time of the missed dose, then continue bisphosphonate therapy.

#### Teriparatide (PTH 1-34)

| Parameter | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| **Mechanism** | Anabolic agent — stimulates osteoblastic bone formation |
| **Dosing** | 20 mcg SC once daily for maximum 2 years |
| **Indications** | Very high fracture risk: T-score <-3.5, multiple fractures, fractures occurring on bisphosphonate therapy, glucocorticoid-induced osteoporosis |
| **Administration** | Pen device; self-injection; refrigerate |
| **Contraindications** | Paget disease, prior skeletal radiation, open epiphyses, bone metastases, hypercalcemia |
| **Boxed warning** | Osteosarcoma observed in rats at high doses; theoretical risk in humans |
| **Duration** | Maximum 2 years lifetime (due to osteosarcoma concern in rodent studies) |
| **Side effects** | Nausea, dizziness, leg cramps, transient orthostatic hypotension, hypercalcemia |
| **Follow-up** | Must follow with antiresorptive therapy (bisphosphonate or denosumab) after 2 years to maintain gains |

#### Abaloparatide
- Similar mechanism to teriparatide (PTHrP analog)
- 80 mcg SC daily; also limited to 2 years
- May have lower hypercalcemia risk than teriparatide
- Must follow with antiresorptive

#### Romosozumab

| Parameter | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| **Mechanism** | Anti-sclerostin antibody — dual action: increases bone formation AND decreases resorption |
| **Dosing** | 210 mg SC monthly; maximum 12 months |
| **Efficacy** | Rapid, large BMD increases; reduces vertebral and clinical fractures |
| **Advantage** | Most rapid BMD increase of all osteoporosis agents |
| **Cardiovascular warning** | Avoid in patients with MI or stroke within preceding year (possible CV signal in trial) |
| **Follow-up** | Must follow with antiresorptive therapy after 12 months |

### Sequential Therapy Strategy

| Scenario | First-Line | Next Step | Rationale |
|----------|-----------|-----------|-----------|
| Standard osteoporosis | Oral bisphosphonate | Zoledronic acid if GI issues or non-adherent | Cost-effective, well-studied |
| Very high fracture risk | Teriparatide or romosozumab | Bisphosphonate or denosumab | Build bone first, then maintain |
| Renal impairment (eGFR 15-30) | Denosumab | Teriparatide if very high risk | No renal clearance needed |
| Cannot tolerate oral/IV bisphosphonate | Denosumab | Teriparatide if fractures continue | Injection options |
| On denosumab, stopping | Zoledronic acid transition | Continue bisphosphonate | Prevent rebound fractures |

## 8.4 Calcium and Vitamin D Supplementation

### Calcium
- **Recommended intake:** 1000-1200 mg/day total (diet + supplements)
- **Supplement dose:** Only supplement the difference between dietary intake and target
- **Food sources:** Dairy products, fortified foods, leafy greens, sardines with bones
- **Supplement form:** Calcium carbonate (with meals, cheaper) or calcium citrate (absorbed without food, better for PPI users or achlorhydria)
- **Divide doses:** Maximum 500-600 mg elemental calcium per dose for optimal absorption
- **Caution:** Excessive calcium supplementation (>1200-1500 mg/day) may increase kidney stone risk and cardiovascular risk (data controversial)

### Vitamin D
- **Target level:** 25(OH)D >=30 ng/mL (75 nmol/L)
- **Maintenance dose:** 800-2000 IU/day (cholecalciferol/D3 preferred over ergocalciferol/D2)
- **Repletion for deficiency:** 50,000 IU weekly x 6-8 weeks, then maintenance
- **Obesity:** May need higher doses (2-3x) due to sequestration in adipose tissue
- **Malabsorption:** May need very high doses or active vitamin D (calcitriol)
- **Monitoring:** Check 25(OH)D level after 3 months of supplementation

## 8.5 Glucocorticoid-Induced Osteoporosis [^28^]

### Risk Assessment
- Dose-dependent risk: >=7.5 mg prednisone equivalent daily for >=3 months confers high risk
- Bone loss is most rapid in first 3-6 months of therapy
- Risk assessment: FRAX with glucocorticoid adjustment (multiply risk by 1.15 for major fracture, 1.2 for hip fracture)

### Prevention and Treatment
- **Start bone protection at same time as glucocorticoids** if:
  - Prior fragility fracture, OR
  - Age >=70 (women) or >=50 (men), OR
  - High-dose glucocorticoids (>=7.5 mg prednisone for >=3 months), OR
  - FRAX above treatment threshold

| Agent | Dosing | Notes |
|-------|--------|-------|
| **Alendronate** | 70 mg weekly | First-line; well-studied in GIO |
| **Risedronate** | 35 mg weekly or 150 mg monthly | Alternative |
| **Zoledronic acid** | 5 mg IV yearly | If oral not feasible |
| **Teriparatide** | 20 mcg SC daily | If very high fracture risk or fractures on bisphosphonate |

- Duration of bone protection should match glucocorticoid use
- Reassess on glucocorticoid discontinuation

## 8.6 Fall Prevention

Falls are the precipitating event for most hip fractures and many wrist fractures. Fall prevention is as important as bone protection.

### Multifactorial Fall Risk Assessment
| Risk Factor | Intervention |
|-------------|-------------|
| Gait/balance impairment | Physical therapy; tai chi; strength and balance exercises |
| Visual impairment | Correct refractive errors; cataract surgery; treat glaucoma |
| Environmental hazards | Remove rugs, cords; improve lighting; install grab bars; nonslip surfaces |
| Medications | Deprescribe sedatives, hypnotics, anticholinergics, opioids; minimize antihypertensives |
| Orthostatic hypotension | Review medications; compression stockings; hydration; slow position changes |
| Foot problems | Proper footwear; podiatry; treat foot pain |
| Sensory neuropathy | Visual cues; assistive devices; home safety evaluation |
| Cognitive impairment | Supervision; alarm systems; home modifications |
| Previous falls | Comprehensive geriatric assessment |

### Vitamin D and Falls
- Vitamin D deficiency is associated with increased fall risk and muscle weakness
- Repletion to 25(OH)D >30 ng/mL improves muscle function
- Doses of 800-2000 IU/day are associated with reduced fall risk
- Very high intermittent bolus doses (e.g., 500,000 IU annually) may paradoxically increase fall risk

| Parameter | Frequency |
|-----------|-----------|
| DEXA scan | Every 1-2 years until stable on therapy; then every 2 years |
| 25(OH)D level | After 3 months of supplementation; then annually |
| Calcium level | Baseline; periodically (especially on teriparatide) |
| Renal function | Baseline; periodically (especially with denosumab) |
| Medication adherence | Every visit |
| Height | Annually (loss >2 cm may indicate vertebral fracture) |
| New fractures | At every visit |
| Fall history | At every visit |

### When to Refer to Endocrinology or Specialist
- Unusual or young-onset osteoporosis (men <50, premenopausal women)
- Fractures despite appropriate therapy
- Complex secondary causes requiring evaluation
- Very high fracture risk requiring anabolic therapy
- Consideration of drug holiday after long-term bisphosphonate use

## 8.8 When to Seek Emergency Care

- **Acute severe back pain** after minimal trauma (possible acute vertebral compression fracture)
- **Hip fracture** after fall: inability to bear weight, shortened externally rotated leg
- **Falling with head strike** while on anticoagulation

# Chapter 9: Electrolyte Disorders

## 9.1 Hyponatremia [^19^][^20^]

### Overview
Hyponatremia (serum sodium <135 mEq/L) is the most common electrolyte disorder in hospitalized patients, affecting up to 15-30% of inpatients. It is associated with increased mortality, length of stay, and healthcare costs. Proper classification by volume status and symptom severity guides treatment.

### Classification by Volume Status

| Type | Clinical Clues | Common Causes | Treatment Approach |
|------|---------------|---------------|-------------------|
| **Hypovolemic** | Orthostatic hypotension, dry mucous membranes, decreased skin turgor, tachycardia | Diuretics, vomiting, diarrhea, adrenal insufficiency, third spacing, osmotic diuresis | Isotonic saline; address underlying cause |
| **Euvolemic (SIADH)** | No edema, normal skin turgor, may have mild hypervolemia | Malignancy, CNS disorders, medications, pain/nausea, postoperative (see Chapter 5.5) | Fluid restriction; oral urea; tolvaptan |
| **Hypervolemic** | Peripheral edema, ascites, pulmonary edema | CHF, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome | Fluid restriction; diuretics; treat underlying |

### Clinical Manifestations
Symptoms depend on both severity AND rate of decline:

| Sodium Level | Acute (<48h) | Chronic (>48h) |
|-------------|-------------|----------------|
| 130-135 mEq/L | Usually asymptomatic | Usually asymptomatic |
| 125-129 mEq/L | Headache, nausea, malaise | May be asymptomatic or mild symptoms |
| 120-124 mEq/L | Vomiting, abdominal cramps, confusion | Fatigue, headache, mild confusion |
| <120 mEq/L | Seizures, coma, respiratory arrest | Severe confusion, gait disturbance, falls |

> **Critical principle:** Brain cells adapt to chronic hyponatremia by extruding intracellular solutes. This protects against cerebral edema but makes the brain vulnerable to osmotic demyelination if sodium is corrected too rapidly.

### Diagnostic Workup
1. **Confirm true hyponatremia:** Check serum osmolality (should be <275 mOsm/kg in true hyponatremia)
   - **Pseudohyponatremia:** Hyperlipidemia or hyperproteinemia (lab artifact with indirect ISE)
   - **Hypertonic hyponatremia:** Hyperglycemia, mannitol (correct sodium for glucose: add 1.6 mEq/L per 100 mg/dL glucose above 100)
2. **Assess volume status:** Physical exam (orthostatic vitals, skin turgor, mucous membranes, JVP, edema)
3. **Urine osmolality:** >100 mOsm/kg suggests ADH-mediated; <100 suggests psychogenic polydipsia or low solute intake
4. **Urine sodium:** >30 mEq/L suggests SIADH or adrenal insufficiency; <20 suggests volume depletion or hypervolemic states
5. **Check TSH, cortisol:** Exclude hypothyroidism and adrenal insufficiency
6. **Medication review:** Diuretics, SSRIs, antipsychotics, carbamazepine

### Treatment Algorithm

#### Acute Symptomatic Hyponatremia (Seizures, Severe Altered Mental Status)

> **EMERGENCY:** Goal is rapid increase of 4-6 mEq/L to stop cerebral edema and seizures. NOT to correct sodium completely.

| Step | Treatment |
|------|-----------|
| 1 | 3% hypertonic saline: 100-150 mL IV bolus over 10-15 minutes |
| 2 | Recheck serum sodium after 20 minutes |
| 3 | Repeat bolus up to 2 more times if symptoms persist |
| 4 | Goal: 4-6 mEq/L increase in first 1-6 hours; then STOP rapid correction |
| 5 | Switch to slower correction (0.5-1 mEq/L/hour) or fluid restriction |

#### Chronic Symptomatic Hyponatremia
- 3% hypertonic saline more cautiously
- Max correction rate: 8-10 mEq/L in 24 hours; 18 mEq/L in 48 hours
- Consider desmopressin + hypertonic saline to control correction rate

#### SIADH (Euvolemic) — See Chapter 5.5
| Severity | Treatment |
|----------|-----------|
| Mild (Na >125) | Fluid restriction 500-1000 mL/day; treat underlying cause |
| Moderate | Fluid restriction + oral urea 15-30 g/day |
| Severe/symptomatic | 3% saline (controlled) + fluid restriction + urea |

#### Hypovolemic Hyponatremia
- **Isotonic saline (0.9%):** 500-1000 mL over 1-2 hours initially
- Monitor sodium frequently (every 2-4 hours initially)
- Watch for overly rapid correction as volume status normalizes
- Once euvolemic, switch to fluid restriction if SIADH component

#### Hypervolemic Hyponatremia
- **Fluid restriction:** 1000 mL/day (all sources including IV meds)
- **Diuretics:** Loop diuretics (furosemide) to promote free water excretion
- Treat underlying condition (optimize HF therapy, manage cirrhosis)
- V2 receptor antagonists (tolvaptan) — caution in cirrhosis; short-term only

### Osmotic Demyelination Syndrome (ODS) Prevention and Management

| Risk Factor | Prevention Strategy |
|-------------|-------------------|
| Na <105 mEq/L | Max correction 8 mEq/L/24h |
| Malnutrition/alcoholism | Even slower correction (6 mEq/L/24h) |
| Liver disease | Max 6 mEq/L/24h |
| Hypokalemia | Correct K concurrently |

**If overcorrection occurs (>8-10 mEq/L in 24h):**
- Give D5W to re-lower sodium gradually
- Consider desmopressin 2 mcg SC to prevent further free water excretion
- Consult nephrology

## 9.2 Hypernatremia [^18^]

### Overview
Hypernatremia (serum sodium >145 mEq/L) is less common than hyponatremia but carries significant mortality, especially in hospitalized and elderly patients. It almost always indicates inadequate water intake relative to water loss — either impaired thirst mechanism or lack of access to water.

### Causes

| Category | Mechanism | Examples |
|----------|-----------|----------|
| **Hypotonic fluid loss (most common)** | Water loss > sodium loss | Diabetes insipidus (central or nephrogenic), osmotic diuresis (hyperglycemia, mannitol), diarrhea, profuse sweating |
| **Impaired thirst** | Inadequate water intake | Altered mental status, elderly, hypothalamic lesions |
| **Sodium gain** | Excess sodium intake | Hypertonic saline, sodium bicarbonate administration, mineralocorticoid excess |

### Clinical Features
- Thirst (most sensitive defense mechanism; absent if hypothalamic lesion)
- Lethargy, weakness, irritability
- Confusion, seizures, coma (severe)
- Signs of volume depletion (if hypotonic fluid loss)

### Free Water Deficit Calculation

**Formula:**
- Men: FWD = 0.6 x weight (kg) x [(Na/140) - 1]
- Women: FWD = 0.5 x weight (kg) x [(Na/140) - 1]

Example: 70 kg man with Na 160 mEq/L: FWD = 0.6 x 70 x [(160/140) - 1] = 6 liters

### Treatment

| Type | Approach |
|------|----------|
| **Acute hypernatremia (<48h)** | D5W at 3-6 mL/kg/hour; can correct rapidly (1-2 mEq/L/hour) |
| **Chronic hypernatremia (>48h)** | Correct slowly: 0.5 mEq/L/hour (max 10-12 mEq/L/24h); use D5W or enteral free water; risk of cerebral edema if corrected too rapidly |
| **Central DI** | Desmopressin (DDAVP) — see Chapter 5.4 |
| **Nephrogenic DI** | Thiazide diuretics, low-solute diet, treat underlying cause |
| **Hypovolemic hypernatremia** | First give isotonic saline to restore volume, then switch to hypotonic fluids |

**Monitoring:** Check sodium every 2-4 hours during correction.

## 9.3 Hypokalemia [^30^][^31^]

### Overview
Hypokalemia (serum potassium <3.5 mEq/L) is one of the most common electrolyte abnormalities. Potassium is critical for maintaining cardiac, neuromuscular, and renal function. Severe hypokalemia (<2.5 mEq/L) is life-threatening.

### Causes

| Mechanism | Examples |
|-----------|----------|
| **Decreased intake** | Malnutrition, eating disorders, clay ingestion (binge eating disorder) |
| **GI losses** | Vomiting, diarrhea, laxative abuse, villous adenoma, fistula |
| **Renal losses** | Diuretics (thiazides, loops), hyperaldosteronism, hypomagnesemia, RTA (Type 1, 2), Bartter syndrome, Gitelman syndrome |
| **Intracellular shifts** | Insulin, beta-agonists (albuterol), alkalosis, hypokalemic periodic paralysis |

### Diagnostic Approach
1. **Check magnesium:** Hypomagnesemia causes refractory hypokalemia (must replete Mg first)
2. **Spot urine potassium:**
   - UK <20 mEq/L: Extrarenal loss (GI, poor intake)
   - UK >20 mEq/L: Renal loss
3. **Check blood pressure:**
   - Hypertension: Hyperaldosteronism, Cushing's, renal artery stenosis
   - Hypotension: Volume depletion, diuretics
4. **Check acid-base status:**
   - Metabolic alkalosis + hypertension: Hyperaldosteronism
   - Metabolic alkalosis + diuretic use: Diuretic-induced
   - Metabolic acidosis: RTA

### Clinical Features

| System | Manifestations |
|--------|---------------|
| **Cardiac** | Arrhythmias (PACs, PVCs, atrial fibrillation), U waves on ECG, flattened T waves, ST depression, prolonged QT |
| **Neuromuscular** | Weakness, fatigue, cramps, paresthesias, constipation, ileus |
| **Severe (<2.5)** | Rhabdomyolysis, ascending paralysis, respiratory failure, ventricular arrhythmias |
| **Renal** | Nephrogenic diabetes insipidus (resistance to ADH), worsening hypertension |

### ECG Changes in Hypokalemia (Progressive)
1. Flattening of T waves
2. ST segment depression
3. Prominence of U waves
4. Prolonged QT interval (due to U wave)
5. Severe: ventricular arrhythmias

### Treatment [^30^][^31^]

#### Severity-Based Treatment

| Severity | Potassium Level | Treatment |
|----------|----------------|-----------|
| **Mild** | 3.0-3.4 mEq/L | Oral KCl 20-40 mEq 2-3x/day; treat underlying cause |
| **Moderate** | 2.5-2.9 mEq/L | Oral KCl 40 mEq 3-4x/day; OR IV 20-40 mEq/L at 10 mEq/hour |
| **Severe** | <2.5 mEq/L | IV 40-80 mEq/L via central line at 20-40 mEq/hour with continuous ECG monitoring |

#### IV Potassium Administration

| Parameter | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| **Peripheral line** | Max concentration 40 mEq/L; max rate 10 mEq/hour |
| **Central line** | Max concentration 80-100 mEq/L; max rate 20-40 mEq/hour |
| **Solution** | KCl in normal saline (avoid D5W — glucose stimulates insulin, shifts K intracellularly) |
| **Monitoring** | Check K every 2-4 hours during IV repletion; continuous ECG if severe |
| **Contraindications** | Oliguria/anuria, severe renal impairment, digitalis toxicity with AV block |

#### Oral Potassium Supplementation
| Form | Dosing | Notes |
|------|--------|-------|
| KCl tablets (extended-release) | 20-40 mEq 2-3x/day | Micro-K, K-Dur; take with food; GI irritation |
| KCl liquid | 20-40 mEq 2-3x/day | Better tolerated than tablets; can dilute in juice |
| KCl powder | 20 mEq packets | Mix with 4-8 oz liquid |
| Potassium bicarbonate/citrate | 20-30 mEq 2-3x/day | Preferred if metabolic acidosis present |

#### Key Treatment Principles
- **Replete magnesium first:** Hypomagnesemia causes renal potassium wasting; K will not correct until Mg >2.0 mg/dL
- **Give with glucose-free fluids:** D5W can worsen hypokalemia
- **Oral preferred:** If GI tract functional, oral is safer and more efficient
- **Monitor frequently:** Check K every 2-4 hours during aggressive IV repletion
- **Address underlying cause:** Essential for preventing recurrence

## 9.4 Hyperkalemia [^32^][^33^][^34^]

### Overview
Hyperkalemia (serum potassium >5.0 mEq/L) is a potentially life-threatening electrolyte disorder. Severe hyperkalemia (>6.0-6.5 mEq/L) or hyperkalemia with ECG changes requires emergency treatment to prevent fatal arrhythmias.

### Causes

| Category | Examples |
|----------|----------|
| **Pseudohyperkalemia** | Hemolysis (traumatic draw), thrombocytosis, leukocytosis, fist clenching during draw, delayed separation |
| **Decreased renal excretion** | AKI, CKD (most common), hypoaldosteronism (Type 4 RTA), adrenal insufficiency |
| **Medications** | ACE inhibitors, ARBs, potassium-sparing diuretics (spironolactone, eplerenone, amiloride, triamterene), NSAIDs, heparin, beta-blockers, calcineurin inhibitors, digoxin toxicity, succinylcholine |
| **Cellular shifts** | Acidosis, hyperosmolar states, rhabdomyolysis, tumor lysis syndrome, massive hemolysis |
| **Increased intake** | Potassium supplements, salt substitutes (KCl), potassium-containing IV fluids |

### ECG Changes in Hyperkalemia (Progressive — Classic Sequence) [^32^]

| Stage | ECG Finding | Approximate K Level |
|-------|-------------|-------------------|
| 1 | Peaked, narrow-based T waves (earliest sign) | >5.5-6.0 |
| 2 | PR prolongation | >6.0-6.5 |
| 3 | P wave flattening and widening | >6.5-7.0 |
| 4 | P wave disappearance | >7.0-8.0 |
| 5 | QRS widening (intraventricular conduction delay) | >7.0-8.0 |
| 6 | Sine wave pattern (pre-arrest) | >8.0-10.0 |
| 7 | Ventricular fibrillation, asystole, pulseless electrical activity | >8.0 |

> **CRITICAL:** ECG changes are unpredictable. Some patients have no ECG changes at K >8.0; others have sine waves at K 6.5. Do NOT rely solely on potassium level — assess ECG on every patient with K >6.0.

### Three-Step Emergency Treatment [^32^][^34^]

> **RED FLAG:** Severe hyperkalemia (K >6.5) or hyperkalemia with ANY ECG changes is a medical emergency requiring immediate treatment.

#### Step 1: Cardiac Membrane Stabilization (Minutes)
| Treatment | Dose | Onset | Notes |
|-----------|------|-------|-------|
| **Calcium gluconate 10%** | 10-20 mL (1-2 g) IV over 2-3 minutes | 1-3 minutes | Does NOT lower K; antagonizes cardiac membrane effects; protects against arrhythmias; can repeat if ECG changes persist |
| **Calcium chloride 10%** | 5-10 mL (0.5-1 g) IV via central line | 1-3 minutes | More elemental calcium than gluconate; use central line (tissue necrosis if extravasation) |

#### Step 2: Shift Potassium into Cells (Minutes to Hours — Temporary)
| Treatment | Dose | Onset | Duration | Notes |
|-----------|------|-------|----------|-------|
| **Regular insulin + D50** | 5-10 units insulin IV + 25g D50 IV | 30-60 min | 4-6 hours | Monitor glucose hourly; give additional D50 if glucose <140 |
| **Albuterol nebulizer** | 10-20 mg over 10-15 minutes | 30-90 min | 2-4 hours | Additive with insulin; tachycardia side effect |
| **Sodium bicarbonate** | 50-100 mEq IV push | 15-30 min | 1-2 hours | Only effective if metabolic acidosis present (pH <7.2) |

#### Step 3: Remove Potassium from Body (Hours — Definitive)
| Treatment | Dose | Onset | Notes |
|-----------|------|-------|-------|
| **Sodium zirconium cyclosilicate (SZC/Lokelma)** | 10 g TID x 48h, then 5-10 g daily | 1-2 hours | Potassium binder; exchanges Na and H for K; well tolerated |
| **Patiromer (Veltassa)** | 8.4-25.2 g once daily | 4-7 hours | Exchanges calcium for potassium; give separately from other oral meds by 3 hours |
| **SPS (Kayexalate/sodium polystyrene sulfonate)** | 15-30 g PO or PR | Hours | **Avoid if possible** — risk of intestinal necrosis/bowel perforation; limited efficacy |
| **Furosemide** | 40-80 mg IV | 30-60 min | If adequate renal function; must ensure urine output |
| **Hemodialysis** | | Immediate | Most effective K removal; indicated for life-threatening hyperkalemia with renal failure |

**Combination approach:** For severe hyperkalemia, use Step 1 + Step 2 immediately, then Step 3 for definitive removal.

### Chronic Hyperkalemia Management [^33^]
- Dietary potassium restriction (40-60 mEq/day)
- Optimize RAAS inhibitors (ACE-I/ARB reduce mortality in CKD/heart failure) rather than discontinuing
- Consider potassium binders (patiromer, SZC) to enable continued ACE-I/ARB
- Loop or thiazide diuretics
- Treat metabolic acidosis (sodium bicarbonate)
- Adjust offending medications
- Fludrocortisone 0.1-0.2 mg daily for hyporeninemic hypoaldosteronism (Type 4 RTA)

## 9.5 Hypocalcemia

### Overview
Hypocalcemia (corrected total calcium <8.5 mg/dL or ionized calcium <4.0 mg/dL [1.0 mmol/L]) causes neuromuscular irritability and can progress to life-threatening seizures and laryngospasm.

### Common Causes

| Cause | Mechanism | Notes |
|-------|-----------|-------|
| **Post-surgical hypoparathyroidism** | Accidental removal/damage to parathyroid glands during thyroid or parathyroid surgery | Most common cause of acquired hypoparathyroidism |
| **Autoimmune hypoparathyroidism** | Autoantibodies against calcium-sensing receptor or parathyroid tissue | May be part of autoimmune polyendocrine syndrome |
| **Vitamin D deficiency** | Decreased intestinal calcium absorption | Extremely common; check 25(OH)D |
| **Hypomagnesemia** | Impairs PTH secretion and action | Must correct Mg first |
| **Chronic kidney disease** | Secondary hyperparathyroidism with inability to maintain normal calcium | Due to phosphate retention, low 1,25(OH)2D |
| **Hungry bone syndrome** | Rapid bone mineralization after parathyroidectomy | Can be severe; requires aggressive calcium repletion |
| **Acute pancreatitis** | Saponification of peripancreatic fat | |
| **Hyperphosphatemia** | Phosphate binds calcium | Tumor lysis, rhabdomyolysis, CKD |

### Clinical Features

| System | Manifestations |
|--------|---------------|
| **Neuromuscular** | Perioral numbness and tingling, paresthesias of fingers/toes, muscle cramps, carpopedal spasm, tetany |
| **Signs** | Chvostek's sign (facial twitching with tapping of facial nerve), Trousseau's sign (carpal spasm with BP cuff inflation) |
| **Severe** | Laryngospasm, bronchospasm, seizures, altered mental status |
| **Cardiac** | Prolonged QT interval on ECG, arrhythmias, hypotension, reduced cardiac contractility |
| **Chronic** | Cataracts, basal ganglia calcification, dermatitis, coarse hair, brittle nails |

### Treatment

#### Acute Symptomatic Hypocalcemia

| Parameter | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| **IV calcium gluconate** | 1-2 g (10-20 mL of 10% solution) over 10-20 minutes |
| **Follow with infusion** | 0.5-1.5 mg/kg/hour (or 10g calcium gluconate in 1L D5W at 50-100 mL/hour) |
| **Monitoring** | Check ionized calcium every 30-60 minutes initially; continuous ECG |
| **Hypomagnesemia** | Replete magnesium FIRST (1-2 g MgSO4 IV) — calcium will not correct if Mg is low |
| **Avoid bicarbonate** | If severe metabolic acidosis present, correct gradually (rapid correction worsens ionized hypocalcemia) |

#### Chronic Hypocalcemia

| Treatment | Dosing | Notes |
|-----------|--------|-------|
| **Calcium carbonate** | 1000-1500 mg elemental calcium/day in divided doses | Take with meals for best absorption |
| **Calcium citrate** | 1000-1500 mg elemental calcium/day | Better absorption without acid; preferred if on PPIs |
| **Active vitamin D (calcitriol)** | 0.25-1.0 mcg/day | If PTH absent/deficient (post-surgical); most potent |
| **Alfacalcidol** | 0.25-1.0 mcg/day | Alternative to calcitriol; requires hepatic 25-hydroxylation |
| **Cholecalciferol (D3)** | 1000-2000 IU/day (higher if deficient) | For vitamin D repletion |
| **Thiazide diuretics** | Hydrochlorothiazide 25 mg daily | Reduce urinary calcium excretion in hypoparathyroidism |

**Target:** Maintain calcium in low-normal range (avoid hypercalciuria, which increases kidney stone risk). Monitor 24-hour urine calcium (goal <300 mg/day).

## 9.6 Hypercalcemia [^35^]

### Overview
Hypercalcemia (corrected total calcium >10.5 mg/dL or ionized calcium >5.6 mg/dL [1.4 mmol/L]) is most commonly caused by primary hyperparathyroidism (outpatients) or malignancy (inpatients). Severe hypercalcemia (>14 mg/dL) is a medical emergency.

### Causes

| Cause | Mechanism | Key Features |
|-------|-----------|--------------|
| **Primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT)** | Autonomous PTH secretion from adenoma (80%) or hyperplasia | Elevated or inappropriately normal PTH with hypercalcemia; most common outpatient cause |
| **Malignancy** | PTHrP-mediated (humoral hypercalcemia), osteolytic metastases, ectopic PTH | Low PTH; most common inpatient cause; poor prognosis |
| **Vitamin D toxicity** | Excessive exogenous vitamin D | Elevated 25(OH)D; suppressed PTH |
| **Granulomatous disease** | Ectopic 1-alpha hydroxylase converts 25(OH)D to active 1,25(OH)2D | Elevated 1,25(OH)2D; sarcoidosis, TB, lymphoma |
| **Medications** | Thiazides, lithium, vitamin A, teriparatide | Medication history |
| **Familial hypocalciuric hypercalcemia (FHH)** | Inactivating mutation in CaSR | Low urine calcium; benign; no treatment needed |
| **Milk-alkali syndrome** | Excessive calcium and alkali intake | History of antacid abuse; elevated bicarbonate |
| **Immobility** | Bone resorption exceeds formation | Prolonged bed rest |
| **Thyrotoxicosis** | Increased bone resorption | Check TSH |

### Clinical Features

> **Classic mnemonic:** "Stones, bones, groans, psychic moans, and fatigue overtones"

| System | Manifestations |
|--------|---------------|
| **Neurologic** | Fatigue, depression, cognitive impairment, confusion, coma (severe) |
| **GI** | Constipation, nausea, anorexia, abdominal pain, peptic ulcer disease, pancreatitis |
| **Renal** | Polyuria, polydipsia (nephrogenic DI), nephrolithiasis, nephrocalcinosis |
| **Skeletal** | Bone pain, osteopenia/osteoporosis (brown tumors in severe PHPT) |
| **Cardiac** | Shortened QT interval, arrhythmias, hypertension |
| **Severe (>14 mg/dL)** | Altered mental status, coma, dehydration, renal failure, death if untreated |

### Diagnostic Workup

| Test | PHPT | Malignancy | FHH |
|------|------|-----------|-----|
| PTH | Elevated or inappropriately normal | Suppressed (<20 pg/mL) | Mildly elevated |
| Calcium | Elevated | Elevated | Elevated |
| Phosphorus | Low-normal | Low | Low-normal |
| 25(OH)D | Normal/low | Normal | Normal |
| 1,25(OH)2D | Elevated | May be elevated | Normal |
| Urine calcium | Elevated | Elevated | **Low** (UCa/Cr <0.01) |
| PTHrP | Normal | Elevated | Normal |
| Albumin | Correct total calcium | Correct total calcium | — |

### Treatment [^35^]

#### Severe/Symptomatic Hypercalcemia (Calcium >14 mg/dL or Crisis)

| Step | Treatment | Notes |
|------|-----------|-------|
| 1 | **IV fluids:** 0.9% saline 200-500 mL/hour | Rehydrate first; corrects dehydration from hypercalciuria-induced nephrogenic DI |
| 2 | **Loop diuretics (furosemide):** 20-40 mg IV | Only AFTER adequate hydration; promotes calciuresis |
| 3 | **Calcitonin:** 4 IU/kg SC or IM | Rapid onset (2-4 hours); tachyphylaxis after 48 hours; use as bridge to bisphosphonates |
| 4 | **Zoledronic acid:** 4 mg IV over 15 minutes | Preferred bisphosphonate for malignancy; onset 2-4 days; duration weeks |
| 5 | **Pamidronate:** 60-90 mg IV over 24 hours | Slower onset than zoledronic acid; use if renal impairment |
| 6 | **Denosumab:** 120 mg SC | If refractory to bisphosphonates or CKD (avoid bisphosphonates if eGFR <30) |
| 7 | **Glucocorticoids:** Prednisone 20-40 mg/day or hydrocortisone | If vitamin D-mediated (granulomatous disease, lymphoma) |
| 8 | **Dialysis** | For life-threatening hypercalcemia with renal failure |
| 9 | **Treat underlying cause** | Parathyroidectomy for PHPT; oncology treatment for malignancy |

> **DO NOT use thiazide diuretics** (they reduce calcium excretion and worsen hypercalcemia).

#### Primary Hyperparathyroidism Definitive Treatment

**Parathyroidectomy indications:**
- All symptomatic patients
- Asymptomatic patients with ANY of:
  - Age <50 years
  - Serum calcium >1.0 mg/dL above upper limit of normal
  - eGFR <60 mL/min
  - T-score <=-2.5 at any site
  - Vertebral fracture
  - Nephrolithiasis or nephrocalcinosis
  - 24-hour urine calcium >400 mg and increased stone risk
  - Osteoporosis on DEXA

**Surgical cure rate:** >95% for single adenoma

**Medical management (if not surgical candidate):**
- Cinacalcet (calcimimetic): 30-90 mg BID; lowers calcium by sensitizing CaSR; well tolerated
- Bisphosphonates: For bone density protection (alendronate, zoledronic acid)
- Monitor: Calcium, eGFR, BMD annually

## 9.7 Hypomagnesemia

### Overview
Hypomagnesemia (serum magnesium <1.8 mg/dL [0.74 mmol/L]) is common and frequently underdiagnosed. It is critically important because it causes refractory hypokalemia and hypocalcemia that will not correct until magnesium is repleted.

### Causes

| Mechanism | Examples |
|-----------|----------|
| **GI losses** | Diarrhea (most common), malabsorption, short bowel syndrome, NG suction, **proton pump inhibitors** (PPIs — reduce intestinal magnesium absorption) |
| **Renal losses** | Diuretics (loops, thiazides), alcohol use disorder, aminoglycosides, cisplatin, amphotericin B, calcineurin inhibitors, EGFR inhibitors |
| **Decreased intake** | Malnutrition, alcoholism, prolonged IV without magnesium |
| **Intracellular shifts** | Refeeding syndrome, acute pancreatitis, hungry bone syndrome |

### Clinical Features
- Often asymptomatic until severe (<1.2 mg/dL)
- Similar to hypocalcemia AND hypokalemia:
  - Tetany, tremor, seizures
  - Chvostek's and Trousseau's signs
  - Cardiac arrhythmias (torsades de pointes, ventricular fibrillation)
  - Hypokalemia refractory to potassium repletion
  - Hypocalcemia refractory to calcium repletion

### Treatment

| Route | Agent | Dosing | Notes |
|-------|-------|--------|-------|
| **Oral** | Magnesium oxide | 400-800 mg 2-3x/day | Less bioavailable but inexpensive |
| **Oral** | Magnesium glycinate | 200-400 mg 2-3x/day | Better tolerated, less diarrhea |
| **IV** | Magnesium sulfate | 1-2 g over 15 minutes, then 0.5-1 g/hour infusion | For severe or symptomatic; requires renal function monitoring |
| **IV** | Magnesium sulfate | 1-2 g over 15 minutes | For torsades de pointes or cardiac arrhythmias |

**Key principles:**
- Oral preferred for mild-moderate; IV for severe, symptomatic, or unable to take PO
- IV magnesium is renally cleared — reduce dose and monitor closely if renal impairment
- Recheck magnesium every 12-24 hours during IV repletion
- Treat until level >2.0 mg/dL AND other electrolytes normalize
- Address underlying cause (stop offending medications, treat diarrhea)

### Proton Pump Inhibitor-Associated Hypomagnesemia
- Occurs in ~1% of long-term PPI users
- Mechanism: Reduced intestinal magnesium absorption
- Usually occurs after >1 year of use
- Reversible with PPI discontinuation
- If PPI essential, add magnesium supplementation or switch to H2 blocker

## 9.8 Emergency Electrolyte Quick Reference Summary

| Electrolyte | Emergency Threshold | Key ECG Changes | Emergency Treatment |
|-------------|-------------------|----------------|-------------------|
| **Na (low)** | <120 with seizures/coma | None specific | 3% saline 100-150 mL bolus; max correction 8-10 mEq/L/24h |
| **Na (high)** | >160 with altered mental status | None specific | D5W; correct 0.5 mEq/L/hour max |
| **K (low)** | <2.5 or arrhythmias | Flattened T, U waves, prolonged QT | IV KCl 20-40 mEq/hr (central); replete Mg first |
| **K (high)** | >6.5 or ECG changes | Peaked T, widened QRS, sine wave | Ca gluconate, insulin + glucose, albuterol, binders |
| **Ca (low)** | <7.0 or seizures/laryngospasm | Prolonged QT | IV calcium gluconate 1-2 g; replete Mg |
| **Ca (high)** | >14 or crisis | Shortened QT | IVF, calcitonin, zoledronic acid |
| **Mg (low)** | <1.2 with arrhythmias | Torsades de pointes | Mg sulfate 1-2 g IV over 15 min |

### Universal Principles for All Electrolyte Corrections
1. **Check for pseudoelectrolyte abnormalities** (hemolysis for K, hyperlipidemia for Na)
2. **Assess rate of change** — acute changes require more urgent but careful correction
3. **Correct underlying cause** — essential to prevent recurrence
4. **Monitor frequently** during acute correction (every 30 min to 4 hours depending on severity)
5. **Avoid overcorrection** — more dangerous than undercorrection in many cases
6. **Check associated electrolytes** (Mg with K, Mg with Ca, phosphate with Ca)
7. **Renal function matters** — impaired kidneys require dose adjustments and closer monitoring

## Appendix E: Emergency Protocols Quick Reference

### E.1 Severe Diabetic Ketoacidosis (DKA) [^3^][^4^]
1. 0.9% saline 1 L over 1 hour (then adjust based on hemodynamics)
2. Check potassium; if K >3.3 mEq/L, start regular insulin 0.1 units/kg/hour IV
3. Add KCl 20-40 mEq/L to fluids when K <5.3 and urine output adequate
4. Add D5W to fluids when glucose <200-250 mg/dL (continue insulin to clear ketones)
5. Continue insulin until ketosis resolves (beta-hydroxybutyrate <0.6 mmol/L, pH >7.3, bicarbonate >18)
6. Give SC basal insulin 2-4 hours before stopping IV insulin
7. Monitor: glucose q1-2h, electrolytes q2-4h, VBG q4h

### E.2 Thyroid Storm [^8^]
1. PTU 600-1000 mg loading dose, then 200-250 mg q4h (or methimazole 60-80 mg load, then 20-25 mg q4h)
2. SSKI/Lugol's solution 5 drops q6h (give 1 hour AFTER antithyroid drug)
3. Propranolol 60-80 mg PO q4h (or IV esmolol if hemodynamically unstable)
4. Hydrocortisone 100 mg IV q8h
5. Supportive care: IV fluids, cooling blankets (NOT aspirin), acetaminophen, treat precipitant
6. Consider cholestyramine 4 g q6h

### E.3 Adrenal Crisis [^11^][^12^]
1. Hydrocortisone 100 mg IV bolus immediately (do not delay for labs)
2. 0.9% saline 1 L over 1 hour
3. Hydrocortisone 50 mg IV q6h (or 200 mg/24h infusion)
4. Treat precipitating cause (antibiotics if infection suspected)
5. Correct hypoglycemia if present
6. Switch to oral hydrocortisone when stable

### E.4 Myxedema Coma
1. Levothyroxine 200-400 mcg IV loading dose, then 50-100 mcg daily
2. Hydrocortisone 50-100 mg IV q8h (until adrenal insufficiency excluded)
3. Supportive care: Mechanical ventilation if needed, passive rewarming, IV fluids (carefully), treat precipitant
4. Consider liothyronine (T3) 5-20 mcg IV q8h if no improvement with T4 alone

### E.5 Severe Hyperkalemia with ECG Changes [^32^][^34^]
1. Calcium gluconate 1-2 g IV over 2-3 minutes (cardiac membrane stabilization; repeat if ECG changes persist)
2. Regular insulin 10 units IV + D50 25 g IV (shift K intracellularly; monitor glucose hourly)
3. Albuterol 10-20 mg nebulized over 10-15 minutes (additive with insulin)
4. Sodium zirconium cyclosilicate 10 g PO TID (remove K from body)
5. Furosemide 40-80 mg IV (if adequate renal function)
6. Dialysis if refractory, life-threatening, or severe renal failure

### E.6 Severe Symptomatic Hyponatremia [^19^][^20^]
1. 3% hypertonic saline 100-150 mL IV bolus over 10-15 minutes
2. Recheck sodium in 20 minutes
3. Repeat up to 2 more times if symptoms persist
4. Goal: 4-6 mEq/L increase in first 1-6 hours (enough to stop symptoms)
5. Then limit correction to max 8-10 mEq/L in 24 hours
6. Switch to fluid restriction, oral urea, or tolvaptan for slower correction

### E.7 Severe Hypocalcemia with Tetany/Seizures
1. Calcium gluconate 1-2 g IV over 10-20 minutes
2. Follow with infusion: 0.5-1.5 mg/kg/hour
3. Check and replete magnesium (1-2 g MgSO4 IV)
4. Continuous ECG monitoring
5. Check ionized calcium every 30-60 minutes
6. Once stable, transition to oral calcium + vitamin D

### E.8 Severe Hypercalcemia (Calcium >14 mg/dL) [^35^]
1. 0.9% saline 200-500 mL/hour (aggressive rehydration)
2. Furosemide 20-40 mg IV after hydration (promotes calciuresis)
3. Calcitonin 4 IU/kg SC/IM (rapid onset; bridge to bisphosphonates)
4. Zoledronic acid 4 mg IV over 15 minutes (definitive; onset 2-4 days)
5. Denosumab 120 mg SC if refractory or CKD
6. Glucocorticoids if vitamin D-mediated
7. Dialysis if life-threatening with renal failure

## Appendix G: Medication Interaction Alerts

| Drug/Class | Interacting Agent | Effect | Management |
|------------|------------------|--------|------------|
| **Levothyroxine** | Calcium, iron, PPIs, soy, coffee | Reduced absorption | Separate by 4 hours |
| **Levothyroxine** | Estrogen, carbamazepine, phenytoin, rifampin | Increased metabolism | May need higher levothyroxine dose; monitor TSH |
| **Warfarin** | Levothyroxine (initiation) | Increased INR | Monitor INR closely when starting thyroid hormone |
| **Metformin** | Iodinated contrast | Lactic acidosis risk | Hold if eGFR <60; restart after 48h if stable |
| **SGLT2-i** | Loop diuretics, insulin | Volume depletion, euglycemic DKA | Monitor volume status; hold SGLT2-i during acute illness |
| **GLP-1 RA** | Oral medications | Delayed gastric emptying | Take other oral meds 1 hour before GLP-1 RA |
| **Statins (simvastatin, atorvastatin)** | CYP3A4 inhibitors (clarithromycin, azoles) | Increased myopathy risk | Avoid combination or use lowest statin dose |
| **Statins** | Gemfibrozil | Severe myopathy risk | Avoid; use fenofibrate if fibrate needed |
| **Bisphosphonates (oral)** | Calcium, antacids, NSAIDs | Reduced absorption, esophagitis | Take on empty stomach; remain upright; separate from other meds |
| **PPIs** | — | Hypomagnesemia | Monitor Mg in long-term users |
| **Lithium** | — | Nephrogenic DI, hypothyroidism | Monitor TSH, creatinine, urine output |
| **Amiodarone** | — | Hypo- or hyperthyroidism | Check thyroid function q6 months |
| **Glucocorticoids** | — | Adrenal suppression, hyperglycemia, osteoporosis | Never abrupt discontinuation; bone protection; glucose monitoring |

## Appendix H: Endocrine Emergencies Summary

| Emergency | Key Clinical Features | Immediate Treatment | Mortality |
|-----------|----------------------|--------------------|-----------|
| **Diabetic ketoacidosis** | Kussmaul breathing, fruity breath, dehydration, glucose >250, ketonemia, pH <7.3 | IVF, insulin, potassium monitoring | 0.5-5% |
| **Hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state** | Profound dehydration, altered mental status, glucose >600, osmolality >320 | Aggressive IVF, gradual insulin | 10-20% |
| **Severe hypoglycemia** | Confusion, seizures, loss of consciousness | Glucagon 1 mg IM/IN; IV dextrose if available | Variable |
| **Thyroid storm** | Fever >38.5 C, tachycardia >140, agitation, GI symptoms, cardiovascular collapse | PTU, iodine, propranolol, hydrocortisone, supportive care | 10-30% |
| **Myxedema coma** | Hypothermia, altered mental status, bradycardia, hypoventilation, hyponatremia | IV levothyroxine, hydrocortisone, supportive care | 30-60% |
| **Adrenal crisis** | Severe weakness, vomiting, abdominal pain, hypotension/shock, hyponatremia, hyperkalemia | Hydrocortisone 100 mg IV, IVF, treat precipitant | 6-10% (peri-crisis) |
| **Pheochromocytoma crisis** | Severe hypertension, headache, sweating, palpitations, chest pain | Alpha-blockade (phenoxybenzamine), then beta-blockade | Variable |
| **Pituitary apoplexy** | Sudden severe headache, visual loss, ophthalmoplegia, altered mental status | Hydrocortisone, urgent neurosurgical evaluation | Variable |
| **Hyperkalemia with ECG changes** | Peaked T waves, QRS widening, sine wave pattern | Calcium gluconate, insulin/glucose, albuterol, binders | High if untreated |
| **Severe hyponatremia with seizures** | Seizures, coma, brainstem herniation signs | 3% saline 100-150 mL bolus; repeat if needed | Variable |

# Chapter 1: Well-Child Care

## 1.1 Overview

Well-child care is the foundation of pediatric preventive medicine, encompassing health supervision, anticipatory guidance, developmental surveillance, and preventive services from birth through young adulthood. The Bright Futures/American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) periodicity schedule provides the evidence-based framework for these visits, with specific goals tailored to each developmental stage. [^1^] [^2^]

## 1.2 Visit Schedule (Birth-21 Years)

The Bright Futures guidelines recommend the following visit schedule, with each visit having specific screening, counseling, and anticipatory guidance components: [^1^] [^2^] [^3^]

| Age | Visit Focus | Key Screenings |
|-----|-------------|----------------|
| **Newborn (3-5 days)** | Feeding assessment, weight change, jaundice monitoring, safe sleep counseling, cord care | Newborn metabolic screen, hearing screen, CCHD pulse oximetry |
| **1 month** | Growth trajectory, early motor skills, sleep routines, maternal postpartum health | Repeat newborn screen if indicated |
| **2 months** | Growth check, first vaccines, safe sleep reinforcement, tummy time introduction | Developmental surveillance |
| **4 months** | Developmental milestones, feeding cues, solid food readiness | — |
| **6 months** | Sitting, babbling, solid food introduction, choking prevention | Hemoglobin/lead risk assessment |
| **9 months** | Crawling, pincer grasp, stranger anxiety, childproofing | Anemia/lead screening per risk |
| **12 months** | Walking, first words, transition to whole milk, dental referral | Hemoglobin, lead level, TB risk assessment |
| **15 months** | Vocabulary growth, discipline strategies, routines | — |
| **18 months** | Running, 10-25 words, **M-CHAT autism screening**, toilet training readiness | **M-CHAT-R/F** [^3^] |
| **24 months (2 years)** | 2-word phrases, climbing, **M-CHAT rescreening**, toilet training | Lead screening, hemoglobin |
| **30 months** | Complex play, speech development, preschool readiness | Developmental screening (ASQ-3) |
| **3 years** | Sentences, social play, **vision screening**, screen time counseling | Vision acuity, hearing |
| **4 years** | School readiness, hearing/vision screening | Vision, hearing |
| **5-10 years** | Annual visits: BMI tracking, healthy eating, peer interaction, school performance | Vision annually, hearing per risk, dyslipidemia risk assessment |
| **11-12 years** | Puberty changes, depression screening, transition to adult provider prep | **Tdap, HPV, MenACWY vaccines** [^4^] |
| **13-15 years** | Mental health, risk behaviors (HEADSSS), sleep, social health | Depression screening (PHQ-A), substance use (CRAFFT) |
| **16-17 years** | Driving safety, independence, substance safety, college/career planning | MenACWY booster, MenACWY #2 at 16 |
| **18-21 years** | Transition to adult care, insurance, reproductive health, healthy habits | Final catch-up immunizations |

## 1.3 Growth Charts: WHO vs. CDC

Accurate growth monitoring is a cornerstone of pediatric preventive care. The selection of appropriate growth charts depends on patient age and clinical circumstances: [^5^] [^6^]

| Parameter | WHO Growth Charts | CDC Growth Charts |
|-----------|-------------------|-------------------|
| **Age range** | Birth to 24 months | 2 years to 20 years |
| **Reference population** | Breastfed infants in optimal conditions (6 countries) | US national survey data (NHANES) |
| **Key advantage** | Better reflects normal growth of breastfed infants; avoids misleading crossing of percentiles | Reflects contemporary US population diversity |
| **Transition** | Switch to CDC charts at age 2 years | Begin use at age 2 years |

### Critical Considerations for Growth Assessment

- **Corrected (adjusted) age**: Must be used when plotting growth for preterm infants (<37 weeks gestation) until age 2-3 years [^6^]
- **Z-scores**: More statistically precise than percentiles for tracking growth over time; represent standard deviations from the mean [^5^]
- **Specialized charts**: Syndrome-specific charts available for conditions including Trisomy 21, Turner syndrome, achondroplasia, and others [^6^]
- **Growth velocity**: Cross-sectional measurements should be supplemented with growth velocity assessment; crossing >2 major percentile lines warrants evaluation

### Failure to Thrive (FTT) / Weight Faltering

FTT represents inadequate weight gain or weight loss in childhood. Diagnostic criteria include: [^5^] [^6^]

- Weight for age <5th percentile on standardized growth charts
- Decrease in weight percentile crossing >2 major percentile lines
- Weight for length/height <10th percentile
- <75% expected weight for age (alternative criterion)

**Etiologic Framework:**

| Category | Examples |
|----------|----------|
| **Inadequate intake** | Breastfeeding difficulties, formula mixing errors, oral-motor dysfunction, cleft palate, neglect |
| **Increased losses** | Malabsorption (celiac, cystic fibrosis), chronic vomiting, protein-losing enteropathy |
| **Increased requirements** | Chronic infection, malignancy, hyperthyroidism, congenital heart disease |
| **Decreased growth potential** | Prematurity, intrauterine growth restriction, genetic syndromes |

**Workup**: Detailed feeding history (type, amount, frequency, duration), caloric intake assessment, review of bowel patterns, three-day food diary, developmental assessment, newborn screen review, physical examination (including airway, swallowing, cardiac), and targeted labs (CBC, CMP, TSH, celiac panel, urinalysis, stool studies) based on clinical suspicion. [^5^]

## 1.5 Developmental Screening Tools

### M-CHAT-R/F (Modified Checklist for Autism in Toddlers - Revised with Follow-up)

The M-CHAT-R/F is the most widely validated autism screening tool for toddlers. [^3^]

- **Administration**: 18-month and 24-month well-child visits (mandated by Bright Futures)
- **Format**: 20-item parent-completed questionnaire; follow-up structured interview for positive screens
- **Scoring**: Total score 0-20; risk stratification: 0-2 (low), 3-7 (medium), 8-20 (high)
- **Follow-up**: The follow-up interview (F) reduces false positives significantly
- **Positive predictive value**: Improved substantially with the follow-up interview component
- **Action**: Positive screen warrants referral for comprehensive autism evaluation and early intervention services

### Ages & Stages Questionnaires (ASQ-3)

- Parent-completed screening for children 1-66 months (38 age-specific questionnaires)
- Domains: Communication, gross motor, fine motor, problem-solving, personal-social
- Available in multiple languages; takes 10-15 minutes
- Results below cutoff in any domain warrant referral for developmental evaluation

### Other Screening Instruments

| Tool | Purpose | Age Range | Administration |
|------|---------|-----------|----------------|
| **PEDS** | General developmental screening | Birth-8 years | Parent interview or self-report |
| **SWYC** | Integrated developmental, behavioral, family risk screening | Birth-5 years | Parent report (20 items) |
| **PSC-17** | Behavioral and emotional screening | 4-18 years | Parent report |
| **PHQ-A** | Adolescent depression screening | 11-21 years | Self-report |

## 1.6 Vision and Hearing Screening

### Vision Screening Protocol [^1^] [^3^]

| Age | Screening Method | Abnormal Findings Requiring Referral |
|-----|-----------------|--------------------------------------|
| Newborn | Structural eye exam, red reflex | Absent red reflex, structural abnormalities |
| 6-12 months | Cover-uncover test, corneal light reflex, fixation preference | Strabismus, asymmetric corneal light reflex |
| 1-3 years | Photo screening or LEA symbols (if available) | Asymmetric readings, significant refractive error |
| 3-5 years | Visual acuity (LEA symbols or HOTV), cover-uncover test | Acuity <20/40 in either eye, 2-line difference between eyes |
| >=5 years | Snellen chart (line-by-line), color vision (males at ~8 years) | Acuity <20/30, 2-line inter-eye difference |

**Amblyopia Risk Factors**: Strabismus, anisometropia, high refractive error, media opacity, ptosis. Critical period for treatment is before age 7-8 years.

### Hearing Screening Protocol [^1^] [^3^]

| Stage | Action | Timeline |
|-------|--------|----------|
| **Newborn screening** | Otoacoustic emissions (OAE) or automated auditory brainstem response (AABR) before hospital discharge | Before 1 month of age |
| **Screen positive** | Diagnostic audiology evaluation | By 3 months |
| **Confirmed hearing loss** | Early intervention services enrollment, amplification fitting | By 6 months |
| **School-age** | Periodic hearing screening per school or clinical protocol | Annually or per risk |

**Risk factors for congenital hearing loss**: NICU stay >5 days, family history, craniofacial anomalies, TORCH infections, hyperbilirubinemia requiring exchange transfusion, ototoxic medications, syndromes associated with hearing loss.

## 1.7 Screening for Toxic Exposures

### Lead Screening [^1^]

- Risk assessment at 6, 9, 12, 18, 24 months; then annually ages 3-6 years
- Universal blood lead level (BLL) testing recommended in high-prevalence communities
- Risk factors: Medicaid eligibility, residence in housing built before 1950, renovation exposure, sibling/playmate with elevated BLL, recent immigration from high-risk country
- **Action thresholds**: BLL >=3.5 mcg/dL warrants environmental investigation and follow-up per CDC guidelines

### Anemia Screening [^1^]

- Risk assessment at 4, 6, 9 months
- Universal hemoglobin/hematocrit screening at 12 months
- Risk factors for repeat screening: Prematurity, low iron diet, exclusive breastfeeding beyond 6 months without iron supplementation, cow's milk before 12 months
- Treatment: Elemental iron 3-6 mg/kg/day if iron deficiency confirmed

### Tuberculosis Risk Assessment [^1^]

- Annual TB risk assessment beginning at 12 months
- TST (tuberculin skin test) or IGRA (interferon-gamma release assay) if risk factors present: travel to endemic areas, household contact with TB, HIV exposure, incarceration, homelessness, immigration from high-prevalence region

## 1.8 Anticipatory Guidance by Age

### Safety Counseling Priorities

| Age Group | Key Safety Topics |
|-----------|-------------------|
| **0-6 months** | Safe sleep (Back to Sleep), car seat (rear-facing), water heater <120F, smoke detectors, fall prevention |
| **6-12 months** | Childproofing (cabinets, outlets, gates), choking hazards, drowning prevention, sunscreen |
| **1-3 years** | Poison control number, helmet use, street safety, firearm storage, pool fencing, window guards |
| **3-6 years** | Pedestrian safety, car seat (forward-facing with harness), stranger safety, body safety |
| **School-age** | Booster seats, bicycle helmets, internet safety, bullying prevention |
| **Adolescents** | Seatbelt use, substance avoidance, safe driving, contraception, mental health awareness |

## 1.9 Dental Care

- **First dental visit**: By first tooth eruption or no later than 12 months of age [^3^]
- **Dental home**: Establish by age 1
- **Fluoride varnish**: Application beginning at primary tooth eruption; every 3-6 months if high caries risk
- **Fluoride supplementation**: Based on local water fluoride concentration (<0.6 ppm warrants supplementation)
- **Caries risk factors**: Bottle/sippy cup with milk/juice at bedtime, prolonged nighttime breastfeeding with teeth, high sugar diet, maternal caries history

# Chapter 2: Common Pediatric Illnesses

## 2.1 Acute Otitis Media (AOM)

### Overview

Acute otitis media is the most common childhood infection for which antibiotics are prescribed, with 23% of children diagnosed by age 1 and 60% by age 3. The annual cost exceeds $5 billion in the United States. [^7^] [^8^]

### Diagnostic Criteria (AAP Guidelines)

Definitive diagnosis requires:
- Moderate or severe bulging of the tympanic membrane (TM), OR
- New onset otorrhea not due to otitis externa, OR
- Mild bulging of TM AND recent (<48h) otalgia or intense TM erythema [^7^]

**Otoscopy essentials**: Pneumatic otoscopy showing impaired TM mobility is the most sensitive clinical finding. Redness alone is insufficient (may be due to fever or crying).

### AAP Treatment Algorithm [^7^] [^9^] [^10^]

| Patient Category | Recommendation |
|-----------------|----------------|
| **Age <6 months** | Immediate antibiotic therapy (no watchful waiting) |
| **6-23 months, bilateral AOM** | Immediate antibiotics |
| **6-23 months, unilateral AOM, mild symptoms** | Watchful waiting 48-72h with analgesia |
| **>=24 months, unilateral or bilateral, mild** | Watchful waiting 48-72h with follow-up |
| **Severe symptoms** (fever >=39C, severe otalgia >48h, otorrhea) | Immediate antibiotics |

### Antibiotic Therapy [^10^]

| Scenario | First-Line Therapy | Duration |
|----------|-------------------|----------|
| First episode, no recent antibiotics | Amoxicillin 75-90 mg/kg/day divided BID | 10 days (<2 years or severe); 5-7 days (>2 years, uncomplicated) |
| Recent antibiotics (past 30 days) | Amoxicillin-clavulanate 90 mg/kg/day (14:1 formulation) BID | 10 days |
| Recurrent AOM | Amoxicillin-clavulanate 90 mg/kg/day | 7-10 days |
| Penicillin allergy (non-anaphylactic) | Cefuroxime axetil or cefpodoxime proxetil | Per age |
| Penicillin allergy (anaphylactic) | Azithromycin or clarithromycin | 5-10 days |

**Watchful Waiting**: Only appropriate if reliable follow-up within 72 hours is possible and no comorbidities. Provide analgesia (acetaminophen 15 mg/kg or ibuprofen 10 mg/kg every 6-8 hours). Approximately 80% of uncomplicated AOM resolves without antibiotics. [^9^] [^10^]

> **RED FLAGS (Emergency)**: Post-auricular swelling/mastoiditis, proptosis, meningismus, facial nerve palsy, severe headache, persistent vomiting. These require immediate evaluation. [^8^]

## 2.2 Streptococcal Pharyngitis

### Overview

Group A Streptococcus (GAS; *Streptococcus pyogenes*) causes 20-30% of acute pharyngitis cases in children, peaking in school-aged children (5-15 years). Untreated GAS can lead to acute rheumatic fever and post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis. [^11^] [^12^]

### Centor/McIsaac Criteria [^11^] [^12^]

| Criterion | Points |
|-----------|--------|
| Fever (oral >38C / 100.4F) | +1 |
| Absence of cough | +1 |
| Tonsillar exudates or swelling | +1 |
| Tender anterior cervical lymphadenopathy | +1 |
| Age 3-14 years (McIsaac modification) | +1 |
| Age 15-44 years | 0 |
| Age >=45 years | -1 |

**Score Interpretation:**
- **0-1**: Low probability (~5% GAS); no testing or antibiotics indicated
- **2-3**: Intermediate (~20%); rapid antigen detection test (RADT) indicated
- **4-5**: High probability (~50%); empiric treatment or RADT reasonable

### Diagnostic Approach [^12^]

1. **Positive RADT**: Treat empirically (no confirmatory culture needed in children; highly specific)
2. **Negative RADT + Centor 2-3**: Confirm with throat culture (RADT sensitivity 70-90%)
3. **PCR testing**: Available with high sensitivity/specificity; may replace RADT in some settings

### Treatment [^12^]

| Regimen | Dosing | Duration |
|---------|--------|----------|
| **Amoxicillin** (first-line) | 50 mg/kg once daily (max 1000mg) OR 25 mg/kg BID | 10 days |
| Penicillin V | 250 mg BID-TID (<27 kg); 500 mg BID-TID (>=27 kg) | 10 days |
| Cephalexin (non-anaphylactic allergy) | 20 mg/kg/dose BID (max 500mg) | 10 days |
| Azithromycin (anaphylactic allergy) | 12 mg/kg once daily (max 500mg) | 5 days |

**Important**: Complete 10-day course (except 5-day azithromycin) to prevent rheumatic fever. Return if symptoms worsen after 48 hours.

### Differential Diagnosis

| Condition | Distinguishing Features |
|-----------|------------------------|
| Viral pharyngitis (most common) | Cough, rhinorrhea, hoarseness, conjunctivitis, oral ulcers |
| EBV/Infectious mononucleosis | Fatigue, splenomegaly, posterior cervical adenopathy, atypical lymphocytes |
| Mycoplasma pneumoniae | Persistent cough, bullous myringitis, older children |
| Peritonsillar abscess | "Hot potato" voice, trismus, uvular deviation, unilateral swelling |
| Kawasaki disease | Fever >5 days, conjunctivitis, rash, extremity changes, cervical lymphadenopathy |

> **RED FLAGS**: Drooling, stridor, trismus, toxic appearance, neck stiffness, airway compromise — evaluate immediately for deep space infection or epiglottitis.

## 2.3 Bronchiolitis

### Overview

Bronchiolitis is the most common cause of lower respiratory tract infection in children <12 months, peaking at 3-6 months of age. Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) causes 50-80% of cases; other pathogens include rhinovirus, influenza, parainfluenza, adenovirus, and human metapneumovirus. [^13^] [^14^]

### AAP 2015 Guidelines: Key Recommendations [^13^] [^14^]

**Diagnosis**: Clinical diagnosis based on history and physical examination in children <24 months with respiratory distress preceded by upper respiratory symptoms.

**What NOT to do (AAP strong recommendations against):**
- Chest X-ray: NOT routinely recommended
- Viral testing: NOT routinely recommended
- Bronchodilators (albuterol, epinephrine): NOT recommended
- Corticosteroids (systemic or inhaled): NOT recommended
- Chest physiotherapy: NOT recommended
- Routine supplemental oxygen if SpO2 >=90%: NOT recommended

**Supportive Management:**
- Hydration: Oral or IV as needed; smaller, more frequent feeds
- Oxygen: For documented hypoxemia (SpO2 <90% on room air)
- Nasal suctioning: Before feeds and sleep (saline drops + bulb suction or nasal aspirator)
- Fever control: Acetaminophen or ibuprofen (>=6 months)
- Trial of bronchodilator: May be considered for first episode with strong family history of atopy/asthma (observe for response; discontinue if no benefit)

### Palivizumab RSV Prophylaxis [^14^]

- Monoclonal antibody against RSV F protein
- **Dose**: 15 mg/kg IM once monthly
- **Maximum**: 5 monthly doses during RSV season (typically November-March)
- **Criteria** (2024 AAP): Infants born <29 weeks gestation OR <32 weeks with chronic lung disease (CLD) of prematurity OR hemodynamically significant congenital heart disease
- New nirsevimab (Beyfortus) single-dose monoclonal antibody now available for broader infant populations per 2024 CDC recommendations

### Hospitalization Criteria

| Finding | Action |
|---------|--------|
| SpO2 <90% on room air | Hospitalize for oxygen |
| Severe respiratory distress (grunting, nasal flaring, severe retractions) | Hospitalize |
| Apnea episodes (especially <2 months or history of prematurity) | Hospitalize, continuous monitoring |
| Dehydration (poor feeding, decreased urine output, dry mucous membranes) | IV fluids |
| Lethargy, altered mental status | Hospitalize |
| High-risk underlying condition | Lower threshold for admission |

> **RED FLAGS**: Apnea, severe retractions, grunting, cyanosis, lethargy, dehydration — immediate emergency department evaluation.

## 2.4 Croup (Laryngotracheobronchitis)

### Overview

Croup is a common childhood respiratory illness characterized by sudden onset of barky cough, hoarse voice, inspiratory stridor, and respiratory distress caused by upper airway inflammation and edema. Most commonly affects children 6 months to 6 years (peak: second year of life). Parainfluenza types 1 and 3 are the most common pathogens. [^1^] [^12^] [^15^]

### Westley Croup Score (Severity Classification) [^12^]

| Feature | 0 Points | 1 Point | 2 Points | 3 Points |
|---------|----------|---------|----------|----------|
| Level of consciousness | Normal | — | — | Altered |
| Cyanosis | None | With agitation | At rest | — |
| Stridor | None | With agitation | At rest | — |
| Air entry | Normal | Decreased | — | Severely decreased |
| Retractions | None | Mild | Moderate | Severe |

- **Mild**: Score <=2
- **Moderate**: Score 3-7
- **Severe**: Score >=8

### Treatment Algorithm [^1^] [^12^] [^15^]

| Severity | Treatment | Disposition |
|----------|-----------|-------------|
| **Mild** (occasional barking cough, no stridor at rest, mild retractions) | Single-dose oral dexamethasone 0.15 mg/kg; supportive care; home discharge | Home with return precautions |
| **Moderate** (frequent cough, stridor at rest, visible retractions) | Oral dexamethasone 0.15-0.6 mg/kg; nebulized epinephrine if distress; observe 2-4 hours after epinephrine | Observation then discharge if improved |
| **Severe** (prominent stridor, marked retractions, significant distress) | Nebulized epinephrine + dexamethasone 0.6 mg/kg; oxygen; hospital admission; ENT/pediatrics consultation | Hospital admission |

### Key Management Points [^12^] [^15^]

- **Dexamethasone**: Single dose sufficient for most; all doses (0.15, 0.3, 0.6 mg/kg) appear equally effective [^12^]
- **Nebulized epinephrine**: 0.5 mL/kg of L-epinephrine 1:1000 (max 5 mL) OR racemic epinephrine 0.5 mL of 2.25% solution [^12^]
- **Observation**: Minimum 2-4 hours after epinephrine administration before discharge (rebound edema risk) [^12^]
- **No role for**: Humidified air/mist (not evidence-based), antibiotics, beta-agonists in typical croup [^12^]

### Differential Diagnosis

| Condition | Distinguishing Features |
|-----------|------------------------|
| Epiglottitis | Toxic-appearing, tripod positioning, drooling, no cough, rapid progression; **medical emergency** |
| Bacterial tracheitis | High fever, toxic appearance, purulent secretions, poor response to croup therapy |
| Foreign body aspiration | Sudden onset, unilateral wheeze/decreased breath sounds, witnessed choking episode |
| Anaphylaxis | History of allergen exposure, urticaria, angioedema, hypotension |
| Retropharyngeal abscess | Neck stiffness, dysphagia, muffled voice, lateral neck swelling |

> **RED FLAGS (Emergency)**: Stridor at rest with severe distress, cyanosis, altered mental status, lethargy, progressive fatigue with decreasing retractions (impending respiratory failure). These require immediate emergency intervention.

## 2.5 Community-Acquired Pneumonia in Children

### Overview

Pneumonia is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in children worldwide. Etiology varies significantly by age and epidemiologic factors. [^16^] [^17^]

### Age-Based Etiology and Management [^17^]

| Age Group | Common Pathogens | Empiric Outpatient Treatment |
|-----------|-----------------|------------------------------|
| **Neonates (<1 month)** | Group B strep, E. coli, Listeria, Chlamydia | Hospital admission: Ampicillin + gentamicin or cefotaxime |
| **1-3 months** | RSV, Chlamydia, B. pertussis, S. pneumoniae | Macrolide + beta-lactam; hospitalize if ill-appearing |
| **3 months-5 years** | Viral (RSV, influenza), S. pneumoniae | Amoxicillin (high dose: 80-90 mg/kg/day) |
| **>=5 years** | S. pneumoniae, Mycoplasma, Chlamydia pneumoniae | Amoxicillin; add macrolide if atypical suspected |

### Outpatient Treatment Guidelines [^17^]

- **Fully immunized children (age-appropriate PCV and Hib)**: Amoxicillin is acceptable first-line therapy
- **Dose**: 80-90 mg/kg/day divided BID (high-dose amoxicillin to overcome penicillin-resistant S. pneumoniae)
- **Duration**: Typically 5-7 days; shorter courses (3-5 days) may be adequate for uncomplicated CAP in children >2 years
- **Penicillin allergy**: 3rd generation cephalosporin (cefdinir) or clindamycin
- **Atypical coverage** (children >=5 years with persistent cough, school outbreaks): Azithromycin 10 mg/kg day 1, then 5 mg/kg days 2-5

### Inpatient Treatment

| Scenario | Treatment |
|----------|-----------|
| Standard hospitalization | Ampicillin or ceftriaxone |
| Severe/ICU | Cefotaxime or ceftriaxone + vancomycin (if MRSA concern) |
| Atypical suspected | Add azithromycin |
| Influenza season | Consider oseltamivir if influenza positive/strongly suspected |

### Diagnostic Approach

- **Mild outpatient cases**: Clinical diagnosis; chest X-ray NOT routinely required
- **Hospitalized/mod-severe**: Chest X-ray (lobar infiltrate suggests bacterial; diffuse/interstitial suggests viral)
- **Blood cultures**: Only in hospitalized children with moderate-severe disease
- **Viral testing**: Consider during influenza/RSV season to guide isolation and antiviral therapy

> **RED FLAGS**: Respiratory distress with SpO2 <90%, toxic appearance, dehydration, altered mental status, concern for pleural effusion/empyema, failed outpatient therapy.

## 2.6 Acute Gastroenteritis

### Overview

Acute gastroenteritis (AGE) results in 1.5 million office visits, 200,000 hospitalizations, and approximately 300 deaths annually in US children. Rotavirus (declining with vaccination) and norovirus are the leading viral pathogens. [^6^] [^18^]

### Assessment of Dehydration

| Severity | Clinical Signs | Weight Loss | Management |
|----------|---------------|-------------|------------|
| **Mild** | Slightly dry mucous membranes, slightly decreased urine | 3-5% | ORT at home |
| **Moderate** | Dry mucous membranes, sunken eyes, decreased skin turgor, tachycardia, irritable | 6-9% | ORT in clinic/ED; ondansetron if vomiting |
| **Severe** | Altered mental status, hypotension, anuria, sunken fontanelle (infants), cold extremities, mottled skin | >=10% | IV fluids; hospitalization |

### Oral Rehydration Therapy (ORT) [^18^]

**First-line for mild-moderate dehydration**:
- **Solution**: WHO ORS or commercial rehydration solution (Pedialyte, Enfalyte)
- **Avoid**: Plain water, juice, sports drinks, soda (inadequate sodium; risk of hyponatremia)
- **Goal**: 50-100 mL/kg over 3-4 hours for mild-moderate dehydration
- **Technique**: Small, frequent volumes (5 mL every 1-2 minutes by spoon or syringe); increase as tolerated
- **Continue breastfeeding** during rehydration; resume age-appropriate diet as soon as rehydrated

### Ondansetron for Vomiting [^18^]

- **5-HT3 serotonin antagonist** that significantly improves ORT success
- **Dose**: 0.15 mg/kg (maximum 8 mg) orally or IV; single dose typically sufficient
- **Benefits**: Decreases vomiting episodes, reduces need for IV fluids, decreases hospitalization rates
- **Safety**: Excellent profile; rare QT prolongation (avoid in congenital long QT syndrome)
- **ESPGHAN/ESPID guidelines**: Encourage short-course use for vomiting children with AGE

### Zinc Supplementation (International/WHO Guidelines)

| Age | Dose | Duration |
|-----|------|----------|
| <6 months | 10 mg elemental zinc once daily | 10-14 days |
| >=6 months | 20 mg elemental zinc once daily | 10-14 days |

Evidence strongest in developing countries and malnourished children; reduces duration and severity of diarrhea.

### Indications for IV Fluids

- Severe dehydration (>10% body weight loss)
- Failed ORT (persistent vomiting despite ondansetron, unable to tolerate oral intake)
- Altered mental status, hemodynamic instability
- Surgical abdomen suspected

### Nutrition Management

- **Early refeeding** with age-appropriate diet as soon as rehydration achieved
- Continue breastfeeding throughout illness
- **Avoid prolonged BRAT diet** (inadequate calories, protein, fat)
- Temporary lactose intolerance uncommon in developed countries

> **RED FLAGS**: Bloody diarrhea (dysentery), bilious vomiting, severe abdominal distension, toxic appearance, altered mental status, hemodynamic instability, dehydration >10%.

## 2.7 Febrile Illness in Children

### Febrile Infants <3 Months

Young infants have immature immune systems and limited ability to localize infection. Management varies by age and clinical appearance. [^12^]

| Category | Workup | Management |
|----------|--------|------------|
| **Well-appearing, 29-90 days** (low risk per Rochester/Philadelphia/Boston criteria) | UA + urine culture (catheterized specimen), consider CBC/blood culture; LP per clinical judgment | Observation vs. empiric antibiotics; reliable follow-up essential |
| **Ill-appearing or <28 days** | Full sepsis workup: CBC, blood culture, UA/urine culture, **lumbar puncture**, chest X-ray if respiratory symptoms | Hospital admission; empiric IV antibiotics (ampicillin + gentamicin or cefotaxime) |

### Febrile Infants 3-36 Months

- Source evaluation based on clinical appearance and height of fever
- **UTI workup**: Catheterized or suprapubic urine specimen (bag urine NOT reliable for culture); UA + urine culture
- Highest UTI risk in: Girls <24 months, uncircumcised boys <12 months, fever without source, fever >39C
- **Occult bacteremia**: Now rare with widespread vaccination (PCV, Hib); consider blood culture if toxic appearance, no focus, temperature >39C, WBC >15,000
- **Immunization status**: Incomplete PCV/Hib increases risk of invasive bacterial infection

### Febrile Seizures

Febrile seizures affect 2-5% of children and represent the most common seizure type in childhood. [^12^]

| Feature | Simple Febrile Seizure | Complex Febrile Seizure |
|---------|----------------------|------------------------|
| Duration | <15 minutes | >15 minutes |
| Type | Generalized tonic-clonic | Focal features |
| Frequency | Single episode in 24 hours | Recurrent within 24 hours |
| Post-ictal | Returns to baseline within minutes | Persistent post-ictal changes |
| Age range | 6 months - 5 years | May extend beyond range |
| Neurological exam | Normal | May have abnormal exam |

**Management:**
- **Supportive care**: Protect airway, place on side, time seizure, abort with benzodiazepine if >5 minutes
- **Address fever source**: Antipyretics for comfort (do NOT prevent recurrence)
- **Lumbar puncture**: If concern for meningitis (ill-appearing, persistent lethargy, meningeal signs, complex features)
- **Imaging**: NOT routinely indicated for simple febrile seizure
- **Recurrence risk**: 30-50% if first seizure before age 1; 20% if after age 1
- **Prognosis**: Long-term outcomes excellent; no increased risk of epilepsy after simple febrile seizure

> **RED FLAGS**: Toxic appearance, petechiae/purpura, meningismus, focal neurologic deficit, persistent lethargy, seizure >15 minutes or focal features, immunocompromised host, unvaccinated status — immediate emergency evaluation.

## 2.8 Constipation in Children

### Functional Constipation: Rome IV Criteria

**Children <4 years** (must include >=2 of the following for >=1 month):
- <=2 defecations per week
- Stool withholding behavior or retentive posturing
- Hard/painful stools
- Large-diameter stools that may obstruct toilet
- Palpable abdominal fecal mass

**Children >=4 years** (must include >=2 of the following for >=2 months):
- <=2 defecations per week
- >=1 episode of fecal incontinence per week
- Stool withholding or retentive posturing
- Painful or hard bowel movements
- Large stools that may obstruct toilet
- Palpable abdominal fecal mass

### Management [^12^]

**Step 1: Disimpaction** (if fecal impaction present)
- Oral PEG 3350 (polyethylene glycol): 1-1.5 g/kg/day for 3-6 days until watery stools clear
- Alternative: Rectal therapies (enemas, suppositories) for severe cases

**Step 2: Maintenance Therapy**
- PEG 3350: 0.4-0.8 g/kg/day, titrated to goal of 1-2 soft stools per day
- Duration: Typically 6-12 months; gradual taper after regular bowel pattern established
- Early discontinuation leads to high relapse rate

**Step 3: Behavioral Modification**
- Toilet sitting 5-10 minutes after meals (exploit gastrocolic reflex)
- Positive reinforcement (sticker charts, rewards)
- Stool diary tracking
- Regular schedule and unhurried bathroom time

**Step 4: Dietary Counseling**
- Adequate fiber intake (age + 5 g/day minimum)
- Adequate fluid intake
- Limit milk to <24 oz/day in toddlers (excess reduces iron, contributes to constipation)

### Red Flags for Organic Constipation (NOT Functional)

| Red Flag | Potential Diagnosis |
|----------|-------------------|
| Onset in first month of life | Hirschsprung disease, meconium ileus |
| Failure to pass meconium within 48 hours | Hirschsprung disease, cystic fibrosis |
| Bilious vomiting, abdominal distension | Intestinal obstruction, Hirschsprung enterocolitis |
| Blood in stool with normal stool caliber | Anal fissure; rule out polyps, IBD |
| Poor growth, developmental delay | Celiac disease, hypothyroidism, metabolic disorder |
| Sacral dimple/tuft, leg weakness | Tethered cord, spina bifida occulta |
| Ribbon-like stools | Hirschsprung disease, anal stenosis |

## 2.9 Atopic Dermatitis in Infants and Children

### Clinical Features

- Onset typically 2-6 months of age; affects 10-20% of children
- **Infantile pattern** (0-2 years): Cheeks, scalp, forehead, extensor surfaces; notably spares diaper area
- **Childhood pattern** (2-12 years): Flexural areas (antecubital and popliteal fossae), wrists, ankles
- Hallmark features: Intense pruritus, xerosis (dry skin), eczematous patches with erythema and excoriations

### Management Hierarchy

| Step | Treatment | Notes |
|------|-----------|-------|
| **1: Foundation** | Liberal emollient application multiple times daily; lukewarm baths; mild non-soap cleansers | Apply emollient within 3 minutes of bathing |
| **2: Acute flares** | Low-potency topical corticosteroids (hydrocortisone 1-2.5%) for face/intertriginous areas; medium-potency (triamcinolone 0.1%) for body | Limit to 2-3 weeks; then taper |
| **3: Steroid-sparing** | Topical calcineurin inhibitors (tacrolimus 0.03%, pimecrolimus 1%) for sensitive areas | For children >=2 years |
| **4: Severe/refractory** | Wet wrap therapy; phototherapy; systemic therapy (dermatology referral) | Short-term wet wraps with mid-potency steroid |
| **Adjuncts** | Sedating antihistamines at bedtime for pruritus; bleach baths (diluted) for recurrent infection; dilute sodium hypochlorite 0.005% | 2-3 times weekly for infected eczema |

### Trigger Identification and Avoidance

- Irritants: Fragrances, wool, harsh soaps, detergents, overheating
- Allergens: Dust mites, pet dander, pollen (role of food allergy is limited; extensive elimination diets generally not recommended without IgE-mediated symptoms)
- Skin infection: S. aureus colonization common; treat secondary infection

> **RED FLAGS**: Eczema herpeticum (clustered vesicles, "punched-out" erosions, fever — **emergency**, requires IV acyclovir), widespread bacterial superinfection with systemic symptoms, treatment resistance (consider immunodeficiency, Wiskott-Aldrich), growth failure.

## 2.10 Common Parasitic Infestations

### Pinworms (Enterobius vermicularis)

- **Diagnosis**: Scotch tape test (apply clear tape to perianal area in morning before bathing; examine microscopically for eggs)
- **Treatment**: Albendazole 400 mg single dose, repeat in 2 weeks; OR mebendazole 100 mg single dose, repeat in 2 weeks; OR pyrantel pamoate 11 mg/kg (max 1g) single dose, repeat in 2 weeks
- **Household**: Treat all household contacts simultaneously; meticulous hand hygiene; short fingernails; morning bathing
- **Reinfection common**: Emphasize hygiene measures

### Head Lice (Pediculus capitis)

- **Diagnosis**: Visualization of live lice or nits firmly attached <1 cm from scalp (empty nits farther from scalp indicate old infestation)
- **First-line**: 1% permethrin cream rinse (Nix) applied to damp hair for 10 minutes, repeat in 7-10 days
- **Alternatives**: 0.5% malathion lotion, spinosad 0.9% suspension, oral ivermectin 200 mcg/kg (repeat in 7-10 days)
- **Mechanical**: Wet combing with fine-toothed nit comb every 3-4 days for 2 weeks
- **AAP guidance**: Treat only confirmed infestations; "no-nit" school policies NOT recommended (nits do not confirm active infestation); prophylactic treatment of contacts not indicated

## 3.4 Contraindications and Precautions

### True Contraindications (Do NOT Vaccinate)

- Severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis) to a previous dose or vaccine component
- Encephalopathy within 7 days after DTaP/DTP not due to another identifiable cause
- Severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID) — live vaccines contraindicated
- Pregnancy — live vaccines contraindicated

### Precautions (May Defer; Evaluate Risk-Benefit)

- Moderate or severe acute illness (with or without fever)
- Personal or family history of seizures (MMR, MMRV — use MMR components separately if concerned)
- Recent receipt of antibody-containing blood products (live vaccines may need deferral)
- Immunosuppression (live vaccines)

### NOT Contraindications (Vaccinate)

- Mild acute illness with or without low-grade fever
- Current antibiotic therapy
- Prematurity (vaccinate at chronological age, except HepB <2,000g)
- Pregnancy of household contact
- Breastfeeding
- Allergies to products not in vaccine (e.g., penicillin allergy not a contraindication to any vaccine)
- History of nonspecific allergies, relatives with allergies, positive allergy testing

# Chapter 4: Adolescent Health

## 4.1 Overview

Adolescence spans ages 10-21 years and represents a critical period of physical, cognitive, emotional, and social development. Healthcare encounters during this period should address the unique health risks and opportunities of adolescence, including chronic disease management, reproductive health, mental health, substance use, and the transition to adult-oriented care.

## 4.2 HEADSSS Psychosocial Assessment

The HEADSSS assessment is the standard framework for adolescent psychosocial risk screening, typically administered beginning at age 11-12 and repeated at subsequent visits. [^1^] [^2^]

| Domain | Assessment Content | Screening Questions |
|--------|-------------------|---------------------|
| **H - Home** | Living situation, family relationships, stability, feeling safe at home | "Who do you live with?" "Do you feel safe at home?" |
| **E - Education/Employment** | School performance, attendance, plans after high school, work | "How are your grades?" "Have you missed school?" "What do you want to do after school?" |
| **A - Activities** | Sports, hobbies, exercise, screen time, peer relationships | "What do you do for fun?" "How much time do you spend on screens?" "Do you exercise?" |
| **D - Drugs** | Tobacco, alcohol, marijuana, vaping, prescription misuse | "Do your friends use drugs or alcohol?" "Have you tried vaping?" "Any prescription drug use not prescribed to you?" |
| **S - Sexuality** | Sexual activity, partners, contraception, STI history, sexual orientation, gender identity | "Have you ever been sexually active?" "What do you use for protection?" "Any STI testing?" |
| **S - Suicide/Depression** | Mood, anxiety, self-harm thoughts, previous attempts, stress | "In the past 2 weeks, have you felt down or hopeless?" "Have you ever thought about hurting yourself?" |
| **S - Safety** | Seatbelts, helmets, texting while driving, bullying, violence, guns | "Do you always wear a seatbelt?" "Do you text and drive?" "Have you been bullied?" "Are there guns in your home?" |

### Administration Tips

- Conduct portion of interview with adolescent alone (beginning no later than age 11-12)
- Explain confidentiality and its limits (harm to self/others, abuse) before beginning
- Use normalized language ("A lot of kids your age...")
- Progress from less sensitive to more sensitive topics
- Address strengths, not just risk factors

## 4.3 Confidentiality in Adolescent Care

### Legal Framework

- Adolescents have the right to confidential health services for certain conditions in most jurisdictions (sexual health, substance use, mental health)
- Federal Title X regulations guarantee confidentiality for family planning services regardless of age or insurance status
- State laws vary regarding specific services and age thresholds
- Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) protects confidentiality for adolescents who have reached the age of majority

### Practical Application

- Inform both patient and parent/guardian about confidentiality limits
- Encourage parent involvement when appropriate and safe
- Separate confidential portions of medical record if needed
- Provide adolescents with time alone with the clinician at each visit

### Services That Should Be Confidential

- Sexual and reproductive health counseling and services
- Contraception and emergency contraception
- STI testing and treatment
- Substance use screening and counseling
- Mental health services
- Pregnancy testing and options counseling

## 4.4 Contraception Counseling

### Long-Acting Reversible Contraception (LARC) — First-Line for Adolescents

| Method | Efficacy | Duration | Key Considerations |
|--------|----------|----------|-------------------|
| **Etonogestrel implant (Nexplanon)** | >99% | 3 years | Most effective reversible method; irregular bleeding common; progestin-only |
| **Levonorgestrel IUD (Mirena, Kyleena, Liletta)** | >99% | 3-8 years | Decreased menstrual bleeding/cramping; pelvic infection risk minimal after first 20 days |
| **Copper IUD (Paragard)** | >99% | 10-12 years | Non-hormonal; may increase menstrual bleeding/cramping |

### Other Contraceptive Options

| Method | Efficacy (Typical Use) | Considerations |
|--------|----------------------|----------------|
| Combined oral contraceptive pills | 93% | Requires daily adherence; regulates menses; acne benefit |
| Contraceptive patch | 93% | Weekly application; estrogen exposure slightly higher than pills |
| Vaginal ring | 93% | Monthly; patient-controlled |
| DMPA injection (Depo-Provera) | 96% | Every 3 months; bone density concern with long-term use |
| Progestin-only pill | 93% | Requires strict timing (same time daily); breastfeeding-compatible |

### Emergency Contraception

| Method | Timing | Access | Notes |
|--------|--------|--------|-------|
| **Levonorgestrel 1.5 mg (Plan B One-Step)** | Within 72 hours (effective up to 120 hours with decreasing efficacy) | Over-the-counter (all ages) | Most effective within 24 hours; weight >165 lb may reduce efficacy |
| **Ulipristal acetate 30 mg (Ella)** | Within 120 hours | Prescription | More effective than levonorgestrel on days 3-5; requires prescription |
| **Copper IUD** | Within 5 days | Provider insertion | Most effective emergency contraception; provides ongoing contraception |

### Counseling Principles

- Emphasize dual protection (condoms + hormonal method for STI and pregnancy prevention)
- LARC methods are appropriate first-line options for nulliparous adolescents and do not require pelvic exam prior to insertion
- Screen for contraindications to estrogen-containing methods (migraine with aura, thrombophilia, smoking >15 cigarettes/day if age >35)
- Discuss future fertility — all methods are reversible

## 4.5 Substance Use Screening

### SBIRT Framework (Screening, Brief Intervention, and Referral to Treatment)

**Step 1: Screen**

| Tool | Age | Content | Scoring |
|------|-----|---------|---------|
| **CRAFFT 2.1** | 12-21 years | 6 items: Car, Relax, Alone, Forget, Friends, Trouble | >=2: High risk; 1: Discuss; 0: Reinforce abstinence |
| **AUDIT-C** | Adults/older adolescents | 3-item alcohol screen | >=3 women, >=4 men: Positive |
| **DAST-10** | Adolescents/adults | 10-item drug abuse screen | >=6: Suggests substance use disorder |

**CRAFFT 2.1 Questions:**
1. Have you ever ridden in a **CAR** driven by someone (including yourself) who was high or had been using alcohol or drugs?
2. Do you ever use alcohol or drugs to **RELAX**, feel better about yourself, or fit in?
3. Do you ever use alcohol or drugs while you are by yourself, **ALONE**?
4. Do you ever **FORGET** things you did while using alcohol or drugs?
5. Do your family or **FRIENDS** ever tell you that you should cut down on your drinking or drug use?
6. Have you ever gotten into **TROUBLE** while you were using alcohol or drugs?

**Step 2: Brief Intervention**
- Motivational interviewing approach
- Express concern non-judgmentally
- Provide feedback on health risks
- Assess readiness to change
- Set goals collaboratively

**Step 3: Referral to Treatment**
- Moderate-high risk: Refer to substance use disorder evaluation
- Provide resources: SAMHSA National Helpline (1-800-662-4357)
- Follow-up at next visit

## 4.6 Mental Health Screening

### Depression Screening (PHQ-A Modified for Adolescents)

- Recommended annually beginning at age 12
- Two-stage screening: PHQ-2 (anhedonia + depressed mood) followed by full PHQ-A if positive
- Positive screen warrants further evaluation and safety assessment
- Assess suicide risk for all positive depression screens

### Eating Disorder Screening (SCOFF)

| Question | Content |
|----------|---------|
| **S** | Do you make yourself **S**ick because you feel uncomfortably full? |
| **C** | Do you worry you have lost **C**ontrol over how much you eat? |
| **O** | Have you recently lost more than **O**ne stone (15 lb) in a 3-month period? |
| **F** | Do you believe yourself to be **F**at when others say you are too thin? |
| **F** | Would you say that **F**ood dominates your life? |

- **>=2 positive answers**: Suggests eating disorder; warrants referral
- **Warning signs**: Significant weight loss/gain, preoccupation with food/weight, amenorrhea, bradycardia, orthostatic hypotension, lanugo hair growth
- **Medical instability requiring hospitalization**: Heart rate <50 bpm, orthostatic hypotension (SBP drop >20), QTc prolongation, temperature <96F, electrolyte abnormalities, hemodynamic instability

## 4.7 Transition to Adult Care

- Begin transition preparation by age 12-14 for patients with chronic conditions
- Develop portable medical summary with diagnoses, medications, allergies, providers
- Transfer by age 21-26 depending on condition and insurance considerations
- Identify adult providers before transfer; warm handoff when possible
- Address insurance changes (Medicaid to adult coverage, parent's plan age limits)

# Chapter 5: Neonatal Care

## 5.1 Overview

The neonatal period (first 28 days of life) is a time of significant physiologic adaptation. Newborn care encompasses screening for congenital conditions, management of common neonatal problems, feeding support, and safe sleep education.

## 5.2 Newborn Screening

### Metabolic Screening (Blood Spot/Guthrie Test)

All states mandate newborn screening, though specific panels vary. Screening is typically performed 24-48 hours after birth (after sufficient protein intake). [^1^] [^3^]

| Category | Conditions Commonly Screened |
|----------|------------------------------|
| **Amino acid disorders** | Phenylketonuria (PKU), maple syrup urine disease, homocystinuria, tyrosinemia |
| **Fatty acid oxidation disorders** | Medium-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency (MCAD), very long-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency (VLCAD) |
| **Organic acid disorders** | Methylmalonic acidemia, propionic acidemia, isovaleric acidemia |
| **Endocrinopathies** | Congenital hypothyroidism, congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH) |
| **Hemoglobinopathies** | Sickle cell disease (SS, SC, S-beta thalassemia) |
| **Other disorders** | Biotinidase deficiency, galactosemia, severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID), cystic fibrosis |
| **Critical congenital heart disease** | Pulse oximetry screening |

**Important**: Repeat screening if initial screen obtained <24 hours of age. Abnormal results require immediate notification to the clinician and family; urgent confirmatory testing and specialist referral.

### Hearing Screening [^1^] [^3^]

| Stage | Action | Timeline |
|-------|--------|----------|
| Screening | Otoacoustic emissions (OAE) or automated auditory brainstem response (AABR) | Before hospital discharge |
| Refer (did not pass) | Diagnostic audiology evaluation | By 3 months of age |
| Confirmed hearing loss | Early intervention services, amplification fitting | By 6 months of age |

**Risk indicators for delayed-onset or progressive hearing loss:**
- Family history of childhood hearing loss
- NICU stay >5 days
- In utero infections (CMV, rubella, syphilis, toxoplasmosis, herpes)
- Craniofacial anomalies
- Hyperbilirubinemia requiring exchange transfusion
- Ototoxic medications (aminoglycosides, loop diuretics)
- Syndromes associated with hearing loss

### Critical Congenital Heart Disease (CCHD) Screening [^1^]

- **Method**: Pulse oximetry at 24-48 hours of life
- **Measurements**: Preductal (right hand) and postductal (one foot) oxygen saturation
- **Positive screen** (requires echocardiogram):
  - SpO2 <90% in either extremity, OR
  - SpO2 90-95% in both extremities with >3% preductal-postductal difference, OR
  - SpO2 90-95% in both extremities on 3 repeated measurements with <3% difference
- Most commonly detected lesions: Hypoplastic left heart syndrome, pulmonary atresia, tetralogy of Fallot, total anomalous pulmonary venous return, transposition of the great arteries, tricuspid atresia, truncus arteriosus

## 5.3 Neonatal Jaundice

### Overview

Physiologic jaundice occurs in 60-80% of term newborns. Pathologic jaundice requires urgent evaluation for underlying causes including hemolytic disease, infection, and metabolic disorders. [^1^]

### Risk Factors for Severe Hyperbilirubinemia

| Category | Risk Factors |
|----------|-------------|
| **Maternal/fetal** | ABO/Rh incompatibility, G6PD deficiency, hereditary spherocytosis, bruising/cephalohematoma, polycythemia |
| **Neonatal** | Prematurity (<37 weeks), low birth weight, exclusive breastfeeding with inadequate intake, East Asian ethnicity, sibling requiring phototherapy |
| **Clinical** | Jaundice within first 24 hours, rapid progression, poor feeding, excessive weight loss |

### Assessment

| Tool | Use | Notes |
|------|-----|-------|
| **Transcutaneous bilirubin (TcB)** | Screening; non-invasive | Useful for initial assessment; may underestimate TSB at higher levels |
| **Total serum bilirubin (TSB)** | Gold standard; confirmatory | Obtain if TcB >=15 mg/dL or near phototherapy threshold, or clinical concern |
| **Direct (conjugated) bilirubin** | If conjugated hyperbilirubinemia suspected | Elevated direct fraction suggests cholestasis or liver disease |
| **AAP hour-specific nomogram** (BiliTool) | Phototherapy/exchange transfusion decisions | Based on gestational age, hours since birth, neurotoxicity risk factors |

### Phototherapy and Exchange Transfusion Thresholds (AAP 2022 Guidelines) [^1^]

The AAP 2022 guidelines use a risk-based nomogram incorporating:
- Gestational age (lower thresholds for preterm)
- Hours since birth (thresholds rise with age)
- Neurotoxicity risk factors: Isoimmune hemolytic disease, G6PD deficiency, asphyxia, significant lethargy, temperature instability, sepsis, acidosis, albumin <3.0 g/dL

**General Principles:**
- Initiate phototherapy at lower thresholds for preterm infants and those with hemolytic disease
- Exchange transfusion at higher thresholds for severe/severe-rapidly rising bilirubin or signs of acute bilirubin encephalopathy
- Use BiliTool (bilitool.org) or similar AAP-approved calculator for precise decision-making

### Phototherapy Management

| Parameter | Management |
|-----------|------------|
| **Light source** | Blue-green light (460-490 nm wavelength) |
| **Skin exposure** | Maximum surface area exposed; remove clothing except diaper |
| **Hydration** | Maintain adequate feeding; IV fluids if intake insufficient |
| **Temperature** | Monitor for overheating or hypothermia |
| **Eye protection** | Occlusive eye shields during treatment |
| **Monitoring** | TSB every 4-12 hours depending on proximity to threshold |

### Acute Bilirubin Encephalopathy (ABE) — Medical Emergency

**Progressive signs:**
- **Phase 1**: Lethargy, hypotonia, poor feeding
- **Phase 2**: Moderate hypertonia (arching, retrocollis, opisthotonus), high-pitched cry, fever
- **Phase 3**: Prolonged hypertonia, apnea, seizures, coma

**Immediate intervention**: Intensive phototherapy, prepare for exchange transfusion, IVIG for isoimmune hemolytic disease.

### Red Flags for Pathologic Jaundice

| Finding | Significance |
|---------|-------------|
| Jaundice within first 24 hours of life | Hemolytic disease until proven otherwise |
| TSB rising >0.2 mg/dL/hour or >5 mg/dL/day | Exceeds physiologic rate |
| Conjugated (direct) bilirubin >1 mg/dL if TSB <5, or >20% of TSB | Cholestasis — urgent evaluation |
| Pale/acholic stools or dark urine | Biliary obstruction or cholestasis |
| Any signs of acute bilirubin encephalopathy | Medical emergency |

## 5.4 Breastfeeding Support

### AAP Recommendations [^1^]

- **Exclusive breastfeeding** for approximately 6 months
- **Continued breastfeeding** with complementary foods through at least 12 months and beyond as mutually desired
- **Initiate** breastfeeding within first hour after birth
- **Skin-to-skin** contact immediately after delivery
- **Rooming-in** (mother and infant together 24 hours/day)
- **Feed on demand**: 8-12 times per 24 hours in early weeks
- Avoid pacifiers and supplements unless medically indicated (after breastfeeding established)

### Contraindications to Breastfeeding

| Absolute Contraindications | Relative/Circumstantial |
|---------------------------|------------------------|
| Maternal HIV (in developed countries) | Maternal Hepatitis B (infant receives HBIG + vaccine) |
| Untreated active tuberculosis | Maternal Hepatitis C (not contraindicated; cracked/bleeding nipples: express and discard) |
| Maternal chemotherapy or radioactive compounds | Most maternal medications (check LactMed database) |
| Active illicit drug use (not methadone maintenance) | Alcohol (limit; wait 2 hours per drink before nursing) |
| Infant galactosemia | Marijuana (counsel to reduce/eliminate) |

### Signs of Adequate Intake

| Parameter | Expected Finding |
|-----------|-----------------|
| **Wet diapers** | 6+ per day by day 5 |
| **Stools** | 3-4+ yellow stools per day by day 5 |
| **Audible swallowing** | Heard during feeds |
| **Weight** | Return to birth weight by 10-14 days |
| **Weight gain** | 15-30 g/day after day 5 (4-8 oz/week) |

### Common Breastfeeding Problems

| Problem | Management |
|---------|-----------|
| **Sore nipples** | Optimize latch; apply expressed breast milk or lanolin; air dry |
| **Engorgement** | Frequent nursing/pumping; warm compresses before; cold after; cabbage leaves |
| **Plugged duct** | Frequent emptying, massage toward nipple, warm compresses, lecithin supplementation |
| **Mastitis** | Continue nursing; empty breast fully; antibiotics if bacterial (dicloxacillin, cephalexin); fluconazole if candidal |
| **Insufficient milk** | Evaluate latch/frequency; ensure 8-12 feeds/day; galactagogues; lactation consultation |
| **Foremilk/hindmilk imbalance** | Complete drainage of one breast before switching; block feeding |

## 5.5 Safe Sleep (Back to Sleep)

### AAP Recommendations to Reduce SIDS and Sleep-Related Infant Deaths [^1^] [^3^]

| Recommendation | Details |
|----------------|---------|
| **Back to sleep** | Supine position for every sleep (naps and nighttime) until 1 year of age |
| **Firm sleep surface** | Firm mattress with fitted sheet only; no soft mattress toppers |
| **No soft objects or loose bedding** | No pillows, blankets, bumper pads, toys, or positioners in sleep area |
| **Room-sharing without bed-sharing** | Infant sleep surface in parent's room for at least first 6 months (ideally first year) |
| **Appropriate sleep surface** | Crib, bassinet, portable crib meeting CPSC safety standards; no couches, armchairs, or adult beds |
| **Temperature** | Avoid overheating; dress in no more than one layer more than adult would wear |
| **Pacifier** | Offer at nap and bedtime (after breastfeeding established, ~3-4 weeks) |
| **Prenatal and postnatal smoke exposure** | No smoking during pregnancy or after birth |
| **Avoid alcohol and illicit drugs** | During pregnancy and after birth |
| **Breastfeeding** | Associated with reduced SIDS risk |
| **Immunizations** | Recommended per schedule; immunized infants have reduced SIDS risk |
| **Tummy time** | When awake and supervised to prevent positional plagiocephaly; begin early |

### What NOT to Use

- **Wedges and positioners**: Not proven to reduce SIDS; may increase suffocation risk
- **Home cardiorespiratory monitors**: Not proven to reduce SIDS (except for specified medical indications)
- **Weighted blankets, swaddles, or clothing**: Not recommended for routine sleep
- **Inclined sleep products**: CPSC has banned these; babies should sleep flat on their backs

# Chapter 6: Geriatric Syndromes

## 6.1 Overview

Geriatric syndromes are multifactorial clinical conditions common in older adults that do not fit neatly into discrete disease categories. They typically involve the interaction of multiple contributing factors across physiologic systems and are associated with significant morbidity, functional decline, healthcare utilization, and mortality. Core geriatric syndromes include frailty, falls, delirium, urinary incontinence, malnutrition, pressure injuries, and sarcopenia. [^20^] [^21^] [^22^]

## 6.2 Frailty

### Clinical Overview

Frailty is a state of increased vulnerability to stressors resulting from cumulative decline across multiple physiologic systems. It predicts falls, disability, hospitalization, and mortality. Prevalence increases with age: approximately 32% in those aged 60-69 to 68% in those >90 years. [^20^] [^21^] [^22^]

### Fried Phenotype (Original Criteria) [^20^] [^23^] [^22^]

The most widely validated frailty assessment tool uses five criteria:

| Criterion | Assessment Method | Cut-off (Frail) |
|-----------|-------------------|-----------------|
| **1. Weight Loss** | Unintentional loss >10 lbs (>4.5 kg) or >5% body weight in past year | Present |
| **2. Exhaustion** | Self-reported fatigue using CES-D questions: "I felt that everything I did was an effort" and "I could not get going" | 3-4 days/week |
| **3. Weakness** | Grip strength measured by handheld dynamometer | Sex and BMI-adjusted cutoffs (men <30 kg; women <20 kg) |
| **4. Slow Gait Speed** | Time to walk 15 feet (4.57 meters) | Sex and height-adjusted (typically >6-7 seconds) |
| **5. Low Physical Activity** | Self-reported energy expenditure | Men <383 kcal/week; Women <270 kcal/week |

**Scoring:**
- **Robust**: 0 criteria
- **Pre-frail**: 1-2 criteria
- **Frail**: 3-5 criteria [^20^] [^23^]

### FRAIL Scale (Quick Clinical Screening) [^22^]

| Component | Question |
|-----------|----------|
| **F** - Fatigue | Are you fatigued? |
| **R** - Resistance | Can you climb one flight of stairs? |
| **A** - Ambulation | Can you walk one block? |
| **I** - Illnesses | Do you have more than five illnesses? |
| **L** - Loss of weight | Have you lost more than 5% of your weight in the past 6 months? |

- **0 components**: Not frail
- **1-2 components**: Vulnerable/intermediate
- **>=3 components**: Frail

### Clinical Significance and Management

| Finding | Implication |
|---------|-------------|
| Frailty identified | Increased surgical risk; consider prehabilitation |
| Pre-frail state | Targeted intervention may prevent progression |
| Frailty + acute illness | High risk for functional decline; mobilize early, minimize iatrogenesis |
| Interventions | Multicomponent exercise, protein supplementation, vitamin D, medication optimization, management of comorbidities |

## 6.3 Falls

### Overview

Falls are the leading cause of injury-related death in adults >65 years. One in four older adults falls annually, and annual healthcare costs exceed $50 billion. Falls are not an inevitable consequence of aging — they are preventable. [^24^] [^25^]

### Risk Stratification [^24^]

| Risk Level | Criteria |
|------------|----------|
| **Low risk** | No history of falling OR single non-severe fall without gait/balance problems |
| **Intermediate risk** | Single non-severe fall WITH gait/balance problems |
| **High risk** | Fall with injury, multiple falls (>=2 in 12 months), known frailty, inability to get up after fall, transient loss of consciousness |

### Timed Up and Go (TUG) Test [^24^] [^25^]

**Procedure**: Patient stands from chair (with armrests), walks 3 meters, turns, returns, sits down.

| Result | Interpretation |
|--------|---------------|
| **<10 seconds** | Normal mobility |
| **10-12 seconds** | Some mobility limitation |
| **>12 seconds** | Increased fall risk; requires further evaluation |
| **>20 seconds** | High fall risk; significant functional impairment |
| **Unable to complete** | Very high fall risk |

The TUG should be combined with other measures (grip strength, 5-times chair stand, gait speed) for comprehensive assessment.

### Comprehensive Fall Risk Factors

| Category | Specific Risk Factors |
|----------|----------------------|
| **Medical conditions** | Orthostatic hypotension, vision impairment, peripheral neuropathy, arrhythmias, stroke, Parkinson disease, cognitive impairment, depression, osteoarthritis, foot disorders |
| **Medications (FRIDs)** | Sedatives/hypnotics, antidepressants (SSRIs, TCAs), antipsychotics, opioids, antihypertensives, diuretics, hypoglycemics, benzodiazepines |
| **Environmental** | Poor lighting, loose rugs, lack of grab bars, clutter, slippery surfaces, uneven surfaces, pets |
| **Functional** | Lower extremity weakness, gait/balance disorders, previous falls, fear of falling, use of assistive device |

### Evidence-Based Interventions (Multifactorial Approach) [^24^] [^26^]

1. **Exercise program** (strongest evidence): Balance, strength, and gait training; Tai Chi effective; minimum 2 hours/week for 25 weeks
2. **Medication review and deprescribing**: Eliminate fall risk-increasing drugs (FRIDs) when possible
3. **Vision optimization**: Cataract surgery, updated glasses, treatment of glaucoma/macular degeneration
4. **Environmental modification**: Home safety assessment; grab bars, non-slip surfaces, improved lighting, removal of tripping hazards
5. **Cardiovascular management**: Orthostatic hypotension treatment, arrhythmia management, pacemaker if indicated
6. **Footwear**: Low-heeled, non-slip, well-fitting shoes; avoid slippers and walking in socks
7. **Assistive devices**: Canes, walkers properly fitted and trained
8. **Vitamin D supplementation**: 800-1000 IU daily if deficient or at risk

### Post-Fall Assessment

| Component | Assessment |
|-----------|-----------|
| **Circumstances** | What were you doing? Time of day? Location? Symptoms before fall? |
| **Injuries** | Head trauma, fractures, lacerations, soft tissue injury |
| **Mechanism** | Trip, slip, syncope, mechanical collapse, dizziness |
| **Ability to get up** | Long lie time? Rhabdomyolysis risk? Fear development? |
| **Contributing factors** | New medications, illness, environmental hazards, footwear |
| **Interventions** | Imaging if head strike on anticoagulation; modify risk factors; referral to PT/OT |

> **RED FLAGS**: Fall with head strike while on anticoagulation (CT head), hip fracture (leg shortened, externally rotated, unable to bear weight), loss of consciousness (syncope workup), new neurologic deficit.

## 6.4 Delirium

### Overview

Delirium is an acute, fluctuating disturbance of attention and awareness that develops over hours to days. It is a medical emergency indicating an underlying precipitating condition. Delirium affects 15-53% of older hospitalized patients and is associated with increased mortality, prolonged hospitalization, and long-term cognitive decline. [^27^]

### Confusion Assessment Method (CAM) [^27^] [^28^] [^29^]

The CAM is the most widely validated delirium screening tool.

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **1. Acute onset and fluctuating course** | Is there evidence of an acute change from baseline in mental status that comes and goes? |
| **2. Inattention** | Does the patient have difficulty focusing attention, being easily distracted, or having difficulty keeping track of what is being said? |
| **3. Disorganized thinking** | Is the patient's thinking disorganized or incoherent, such as rambling or irrelevant conversation, unclear or illogical flow of ideas, or unpredictable switching from subject to subject? |
| **4. Altered level of consciousness** | Is the patient anything other than alert (e.g., vigilant/hyperalert, lethargic, stuporous, comatose)? |

**Diagnosis**: Features 1 AND 2 must be present, PLUS either 3 or 4. [^29^]

### Other Delirium Screening Tools

| Tool | Setting | Time | Sensitivity | Notes |
|------|---------|------|-------------|-------|
| **3D-CAM** | General hospital | 3 minutes | 95% | Diagnostic interview; highly sensitive and specific [^27^] |
| **CAM-ICU** | ICU (intubated/non-verbal) | 2 minutes | High | Designed for critical care [^28^] |
| **bCAM** | Emergency department | 1 minute | High | Brief CAM for verbal patients [^28^] |
| **DTS (Delirium Triage Screen)** | ED | <20 seconds | 98% sensitivity | Ultra-brief triage tool [^28^] |
| **UB-2** | Any setting | <1 minute | 96% | In persons with dementia; 2-item screen [^30^] |

### Delirium vs. Dementia [^27^]

| Feature | Delirium | Dementia |
|---------|----------|----------|
| Onset | Acute (hours to days) | Insidious (months to years) |
| Course | Fluctuating, variable | Progressive, generally irreversible |
| Attention | Markedly impaired | Normal (early stages) |
| Consciousness | Altered (hyperalert to comatose) | Normal until late stages |
| Orientation | Usually disoriented | Usually oriented (early) |
| Cause | Medical illness, drugs, infection, metabolic | Neurodegeneration, vascular disease |
| Treatment | Identify and treat underlying cause | Supportive; disease-modifying when available |

### Causes of Delirium (I WATCH DEATH Mnemonic)

| Letter | Cause | Examples |
|--------|-------|----------|
| **I** | Infection | UTI, pneumonia, sepsis, CNS infection |
| **W** | Withdrawal | Alcohol, benzodiazepines, opioids |
| **A** | Acute metabolic | Electrolyte disturbances, acid-base disorders, hepatic/renal failure |
| **T** | Toxins/drugs | Medications (anticholinergics, sedatives, opioids), alcohol, environmental toxins |
| **C** | CNS pathology | Stroke, hemorrhage, seizure, tumor, meningitis |
| **H** | Hypoxia | COPD, heart failure, pulmonary embolism, altitude |
| **D** | Deficiencies | Thiamine (Wernicke), B12, folate |
| **E** | Environmental | Sensory deprivation, sleep deprivation, unfamiliar setting, restraints |
| **A** | Acute vascular | MI, arrhythmia, hypertensive emergency, shock |
| **T** | Trauma, temperature | Head injury, heat stroke, hypothermia |
| **H** | Heavy metals, Heady injury | Lead, mercury; subdural hematoma |

### Management

**Nonpharmacologic interventions (first-line):**
- Frequent reorientation (clock, calendar, family photos, window)
- Cognitive stimulation (conversation, reading, music)
- Sleep hygiene (minimize nighttime disruptions, warm drink at bedtime)
- Early mobilization and physical activity
- Vision and hearing aids (ensure functional)
- Adequate hydration and nutrition
- Bowel and bladder care (prevent retention/constipation)
- **Avoid physical restraints** when possible (increase injury, prolong delirium)
- Involve family/caregivers in reorientation

**Pharmacologic (only for severe agitation threatening safety):**
- Low-dose haloperidol 0.5-1 mg PO/IM/IV
- Atypical antipsychotics: Quetiapine 12.5-50 mg (preferred in Parkinson/Lewy body due to lower extrapyramidal risk), olanzapine 2.5-5 mg, risperidone 0.25-0.5 mg
- **Avoid benzodiazepines** except for alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal (worsen delirium)
- Use lowest effective dose for shortest duration; taper when delirium resolves

### Prevention [^30^]

Multicomponent nonpharmacologic interventions (Hospital Elder Life Program — HELP):
- Maintain orientation and communication
- Ensure sensory input (glasses, hearing aids)
- Promote sleep (noise reduction, schedules)
- Early mobilization
- Adequate hydration and nutrition
- Prevent constipation and urinary retention
- Monitor medication exposure

### Delirium Superimposed on Dementia [^30^]

- Challenging to diagnose due to overlapping features
- **Key strategy**: Establish baseline cognitive status from family/caregivers
- Screen for attention deficits as marker of delirium (attention testing distinguishes delirium from baseline dementia)
- UB-2 tool has 96% sensitivity in persons with dementia [^30^]

## 6.5 Urinary Incontinence

### Overview

Urinary incontinence affects 15-30% of community-dwelling older adults and up to 50% of nursing home residents. It is associated with social isolation, depression, falls (rushing to bathroom), skin breakdown, and caregiver burden. Incontinence is NOT a normal consequence of aging — it is treatable. [^31^]

### Types and Management [^31^] [^32^]

| Type | Mechanism | Presentation | Treatment |
|------|-----------|-------------|-----------|
| **Stress** | Urethral sphincter weakness; increased intra-abdominal pressure exceeds urethral closure pressure | Leakage with cough, sneeze, laugh, exercise, lifting | Pelvic floor exercises (Kegels), behavioral therapy, weight loss, pessaries, alpha-agonists (pseudoephedrine), vaginal estrogen, surgical slings |
| **Urge** (overactive bladder) | Detrusor overactivity; involuntary bladder contractions | Sudden strong urge, frequency, nocturia, leakage before reaching toilet | Bladder training, timed voiding, antimuscarinics (oxybutynin, tolterodine, solifenacin), beta-3 agonists (mirabegron, vibegron), onabotulinum toxin A |
| **Overflow** | Detrusor underactivity or bladder outlet obstruction; incomplete emptying | Continuous dribbling, weak stream, hesitancy, sensation of incomplete emptying | Intermittent catheterization, alpha-blockers (tamsulosin), bethanechol, relieve obstruction (TURP if BPH) |
| **Functional** | Physical or cognitive impairment prevents timely toileting | Variable pattern; patient aware but unable to reach toilet in time | Address underlying cause, scheduled toileting, mobility aids, clothing modification, environmental adaptation, bedside commode |
| **Mixed** | Combination of types (commonly stress + urge) | Features of both types | Treat predominant symptom first |

### Initial Evaluation

| Component | Assessment |
|-----------|-----------|
| **History** | Onset, pattern, triggers, severity (pad count, voiding diary), prior treatments, obstetric history, pelvic surgery |
| **Medications** | Diuretics, alpha-blockers, ACE inhibitors, sedatives, alcohol, caffeine |
| **Physical exam** | Pelvic exam (prolapse, atrophy), prostate assessment (BPH), neurologic exam (sacral reflexes), cough stress test |
| **Urinalysis** | Rule out infection, hematuria, glycosuria |
| **Post-void residual (PVR)** | Bladder scan after voiding; >200 mL suggests retention/overflow |

### Reversible Causes (DIAPPERS Mnemonic)

| Letter | Cause |
|--------|-------|
| **D** | Delirium/confusion |
| **I** | Infection (UTI) |
| **A** | Atrophic vaginitis/urethritis |
| **P** | Pharmaceuticals (diuretics, sedatives, anticholinergics, alpha-blockers) |
| **P** | Psychological (depression) |
| **E** | Excess urine output (heart failure, hyperglycemia, diuretics) |
| **R** | Restricted mobility |
| **S** | Stool impaction |

### Pharmacologic Considerations in Elderly [^33^]

| Class | Agents | Concerns in Elderly |
|-------|--------|---------------------|
| **Antimuscarinics** | Oxybutynin, tolterodine, solifenacin, trospium | Constipation, dry mouth, urinary retention, cognitive impairment (anticholinergic burden); oxybutynin has highest CNS penetration |
| **Beta-3 agonists** | Mirabegron, vibegron | Hypertension (mirabegron contraindicated with severe uncontrolled HTN); less cognitive impact than antimuscarinics |
| **Vaginal estrogen** | Estradiol cream, ring, tablet | For atrophic vaginitis-associated incontinence; improves urethral and vaginal epithelium |
| **Alpha-blockers** | Tamsulosin, alfuzosin | For overflow related to BPH; orthostatic hypotension |

> **Beers Criteria Warning**: Desmopressin is potentially inappropriate in elderly due to high risk of severe hyponatremia. [^33^]

> **RED FLAGS**: Gross hematuria, pelvic mass, neurologic deficits suggesting cord compression, recurrent UTIs, PVR >200-300 mL, new incontinence with back pain/cauda equina, acute urinary retention.

## 6.6 Malnutrition

### Overview

Malnutrition in older adults is associated with increased complications, functional decline, prolonged hospitalization, and mortality. It is often insidious and underdetected in clinical practice. [^34^] [^35^] [^36^]

### Mini Nutritional Assessment (MNA) [^34^] [^35^] [^36^]

The MNA is the most validated nutritional screening tool for older adults.

**MNA-SF (Short Form)** — preferred for clinical use; takes <5 minutes:

| Item | Scoring |
|------|---------|
| Food intake decline (past 3 months) | Severe=0, Moderate=1, No=2 |
| Weight loss (past 3 months) | >3kg=0, Unknown=1, 1-3kg=2, None=3 |
| Mobility | Bed/chair bound=0, Able to get out of bed/chair=1, Goes out=2 |
| Acute disease/psychological stress (past 3 months) | Yes=0, No=2 |
| Neuropsychological problems | Severe dementia/depression=0, Mild dementia=1, No=2 |
| BMI (or calf circumference) | BMI <19=0, 19-<21=1, 21-<23=2, >=23=3 (CC <31cm=0, >=31cm=3) |

**Scoring:**
- **12-14 points**: Normal nutritional status
- **8-11 points**: At risk of malnutrition
- **0-7 points**: Malnourished [^36^]

**Validation**: Sensitivity 89%, specificity 82%; used in >2,400 clinical studies; included in 22+ expert guidelines. [^35^] [^37^]

### Risk Factors for Malnutrition in Older Adults

| Category | Risk Factors |
|----------|-------------|
| **Medical** | Chronic illness (COPD, heart failure, CKD, cancer), dysphagia, poor dentition, GI disorders, polypharmacy, chronic pain |
| **Functional** | ADL impairment, shopping/cooking difficulty, feeding dependency |
| **Psychosocial** | Depression, social isolation, dementia, alcoholism, poverty, bereavement |
| **Medication-related** | Drugs causing anorexia, nausea, dry mouth, altered taste (metronidazole, ACE inhibitors, opioids) |

### Interventions

| Strategy | Implementation |
|----------|---------------|
| **Dietary counseling** | High-protein, nutrient-dense foods; small frequent meals; fortify foods |
| **Oral nutritional supplements** | Between meals (not with meals to avoid early satiety); high-calorie, high-protein |
| **Appetite stimulants** | Megestrol (with caution: increases mortality risk, thrombosis, adrenal suppression); mirtazapine if depression present |
| **Address underlying causes** | Dental referral, dysphagia evaluation, depression treatment, medication review |
| **Enteral nutrition** | For those unable to meet needs orally but with functional GI tract; consider risks/benefits in advanced dementia |
| **Monitor** | Weekly weights initially; quarterly MNA rescreening |

## 6.7 Pressure Injuries

### Overview

Pressure injuries are localized damage to the skin and/or underlying soft tissue, usually over a bony prominence, resulting from sustained pressure with or without shear. Prevention is the highest level of care. [^38^] [^39^] [^40^]

### Staging (NPIAP — National Pressure Injury Advisory Panel) [^39^]

| Stage | Description | Key Features |
|-------|-------------|--------------|
| **Stage 1** | Non-blanchable erythema of intact skin | May have pain, temperature change (warmer/cooler), firmness; **skin intact** |
| **Stage 2** | Partial-thickness skin loss with exposed dermis | Shallow open ulcer or intact/popped serum-filled blister; no slough or bruising |
| **Stage 3** | Full-thickness skin loss | Adipose (fat) visible in ulcer; granulation tissue present; no muscle/bone/tendon exposed; may include undermining/tunneling |
| **Stage 4** | Full-thickness skin and tissue loss | Exposed fascia, muscle, tendon, ligament, cartilage, or bone; epibole (rolled wound edges) common |
| **Unstageable** | Full-thickness skin and tissue loss | Wound base completely covered by slough and/or eschar; true depth cannot be determined until debrided |
| **Deep Tissue Pressure Injury (DTPI)** | Persistent non-blanchable deep red/maroon/purple discoloration | Intact or non-intact skin with localized discoloration; may have pain, firmness, temperature change; **may evolve rapidly** |

**Do NOT stage**: Mucosal pressure injuries (cannot be staged); suspected deep tissue injury if skin intact but discolored.

### Braden Scale for Predicting Pressure Sore Risk

| Factor | Score 4 | Score 3 | Score 2 | Score 1 |
|--------|---------|---------|---------|---------|
| **Sensory perception** | No impairment | Slightly limited | Very limited | Completely limited |
| **Moisture** | Rarely moist | Occasionally moist | Often moist | Constantly moist |
| **Activity** | Walks frequently | Walks occasionally | Chairfast | Bedfast |
| **Mobility** | No limitations | Slightly limited | Very limited | Completely immobile |
| **Nutrition** | Excellent | Adequate | Probably inadequate | Very poor |
| **Friction/shear** | No apparent problem | Potential problem | Problem | Problem |

- **19-23**: Not at risk
- **15-18**: Mild risk
- **13-14**: Moderate risk
- **10-12**: High risk
- **<=9**: Very high risk

### Prevention Strategies [^38^] [^39^]

| Strategy | Implementation |
|----------|---------------|
| **Repositioning** | Every 2 hours in bed; every 15-30 minutes in chair; use 30-degree lateral tilt (not 90-degree side lying) |
| **Support surfaces** | Pressure redistribution mattress (low-air-loss, alternating pressure) for high risk; **no donut cushions** (occlude blood flow) [^39^] |
| **Skin care** | Keep clean and dry; gentle cleansing; moisturize dry skin; protect from moisture (incontinence-associated dermatitis prevention) |
| **Heel offloading** | Suspend heels using pillows or heel-offloading devices; do not place directly on surface |
| **Nutrition** | MNA screening; adequate protein (1.2-1.5 g/kg/day) and calories for wound healing |
| **Early mobilization** | Progressive activity as tolerated |

### Management [^38^]

| Component | Management |
|-----------|-----------|
| **Pressure redistribution** | Offload completely from affected area; specialty mattress or overlay |
| **Debridement** | Remove devitalized tissue (autolytic, enzymatic, sharp, surgical); **do not debride** dry intact eschar on heels unless signs of infection |
| **Wound care** | Moisture-balanced environment; foam or silicone dressings recommended [^38^]; negative pressure wound therapy for Stage 3-4 |
| **Infection management** | Culture if suspected; topical antiseptics for critically colonized wounds; systemic antibiotics for significant cellulitis/osteomyelitis |
| **Nutritional support** | Essential for healing; protein supplementation; consider arginine, zinc, vitamin C |
| **Specialist referral** | Wound care specialist, plastic surgery, infectious disease, vascular surgery if indicated |

## 6.8 Sarcopenia

### Overview

Sarcopenia is a progressive, generalized skeletal muscle disorder characterized by low muscle strength, low muscle quantity/quality, and low physical performance. It is associated with increased risk of falls, fractures, physical disability, and mortality. [^41^] [^42^]

### EWGSOP2 Definition (2018) [^41^]

| Category | Criteria |
|----------|----------|
| **Probable sarcopenia** | Low muscle strength (Criterion 1) |
| **Confirmed sarcopenia** | Low muscle strength + low muscle quantity/quality (Criteria 1 + 2) |
| **Severe sarcopenia** | Low muscle strength + low muscle quantity/quality + low physical performance (Criteria 1 + 2 + 3) |

### F-A-C-S Screening and Diagnostic Pathway [^41^]

| Step | Tool | Cut-off |
|------|------|---------|
| **F - Find** | SARC-F questionnaire | Score >=4 indicates risk |
| **A - Assess** | Grip strength (dynamometer) OR chair stand test | Men <27 kg, Women <16 kg (grip) |
| **C - Confirm** | DXA (preferred), BIA, CT, MRI | Low appendicular skeletal muscle mass |
| **S - Severity** | Gait speed, SPPB, TUG | Gait speed <=0.8 m/s indicates severe |

### SARC-F Questionnaire [^41^]

| Question | Scoring (0-2) |
|----------|--------------|
| **S**trength: How much difficulty do you have lifting and carrying 10 pounds? | None=0, Some=1, A lot/unable=2 |
| **A**ssistance walking: How much difficulty walking across a room? | None=0, Some=1, A lot/unable=2 |
| **R**ise from chair: How much difficulty transferring from a chair or bed? | None=0, Some=1, A lot/unable=2 |
| **C**limb stairs: How much difficulty climbing a flight of 10 stairs? | None=0, Some=1, A lot/unable=2 |
| **F**alls: How many times have you fallen in the past year? | None=0, 1-3=1, >=4=2 |

- **Score 0-3**: Low risk
- **Score >=4**: High risk for sarcopenia; proceed to strength assessment

### Management

| Intervention | Details |
|-------------|---------|
| **Progressive resistance exercise** | Most effective intervention; individualized program; 2-3 sessions per week |
| **Protein supplementation** | 1.0-1.2 g/kg/day standard; 1.2-1.5 g/kg/day during acute illness or in active training |
| **Vitamin D supplementation** | 800-1000 IU daily if deficient or insufficient; target 25-OH vitamin D >30 ng/mL |
| **Multimodal approach** | Exercise + nutrition optimization yields best outcomes |
| **Address underlying causes** | Inflammation, endocrine disorders (hypogonadism, thyroid), medication effects (steroids) |

# Chapter 7: Polypharmacy

## 7.1 Overview

Polypharmacy, commonly defined as the use of five or more medications, affects approximately 40-50% of older adults. In the nursing home setting, the average resident takes 8-10 medications. Polypharmacy is associated with increased risk of adverse drug events, drug-drug interactions, medication non-adherence, functional decline, hospitalization, and mortality. The goal of geriatric medication management is not simply reducing pill count but optimizing medication regimens through evidence-based tools and patient-centered deprescribing. [^33^]

## 7.2 AGS Beers Criteria (2023 Update)

### Overview

The American Geriatrics Society (AGS) Beers Criteria is the most widely used list of potentially inappropriate medications (PIMs) for adults >=65 years in ambulatory, acute, and institutionalized settings (except hospice/end-of-life care). It is intended to improve medication selection, educate clinicians and patients, and serve as a quality-of-care evaluation tool. [^33^] [^43^]

### Five List Sections [^43^]

1. **PIMs potentially inappropriate in most older adults** (drugs to avoid)
2. **PIMs potentially inappropriate in older adults with specific diseases/syndromes**
3. **Medications to use with caution**
4. **Medication combinations to avoid** (clinically important drug-drug interactions)
5. **Medications with potentially clinically important drug-disease interactions**

### Key PIMs by Drug Class [^33^] [^43^]

| Drug Class | Examples | Concerns in Older Adults | Recommendation |
|------------|----------|-------------------------|----------------|
| **First-generation antihistamines** | Diphenhydramine, chlorpheniramine, hydroxyzine, doxylamine | High anticholinergic activity; confusion, cognitive impairment, delirium, constipation, urinary retention, dry mouth, sedation, falls | **Avoid** |
| **Antipsychotics (long-term)** | Haloperidol, risperidone, quetiapine, olanzapine | Increased mortality (black box in dementia), stroke, metabolic syndrome, falls, extrapyramidal symptoms | Avoid except for schizophrenia, bipolar, short-term as antiemetic; avoid for behavioral symptoms of dementia unless nonpharmacologic approaches fail and patient poses threat |
| **Benzodiazepines** | Diazepam, lorazepam, alprazolam, temazepam | Cognitive impairment, delirium, falls, fractures, tolerance, dependence, respiratory depression | **Avoid** as first-line; use shortest duration possible if needed; avoid diazepam (long half-life) |
| **Non-benzodiazepine hypnotics (Z-drugs)** | Zolpidem, eszopiclone, zaleplon | Similar adverse effects to benzodiazepines; confusion, falls, complex sleep behaviors | **Avoid**; minimal efficacy improvement over placebo |
| **Tricyclic antidepressants** | Amitriptyline, nortriptyline, imipramine | High anticholinergic, orthostatic hypotension, cardiac arrhythmias, falls | **Avoid** amitriptyline; nortriptyline/desipramine less anticholinergic but still avoid as first-line |
| **NSAIDs (oral, chronic)** | Ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac | GI bleeding, renal impairment, cardiovascular events (HTN, heart failure, stroke), platelet dysfunction | Avoid chronic use; use PPI gastroprotection if unavoidable; topical NSAIDs preferred for localized pain |
| **Meperidine** | Demerol | Neurotoxic metabolite (normeperidine); delirium, seizures; poor analgesia | **Avoid** in all older adults |
| **Anticholinergics (various)** | Oxybutynin, tolterodine, TCAs, antihistamines | Cumulative anticholinergic burden increases delirium, constipation, urinary retention, dry mouth, cognitive impairment, blurred vision | Minimize anticholinergic burden; calculate ACB score |
| **Long-acting sulfonylureas** | Glyburide (glibenclamide) | High hypoglycemia risk; long half-life | **Avoid**; prefer glipizide (shorter acting) or non-sulfonylurea agents |
| **Alpha-1 blockers** | Doxazosin, prazosin, terazosin | High risk of orthostatic hypotension, syncope, falls | Avoid as antihypertensive; tamsulosin acceptable for BPH |
| **Corticosteroids (oral, chronic)** | Prednisone, methylprednisolone | Hyperglycemia, osteoporosis, adrenal suppression, weight gain, mood changes | Use lowest effective dose; bone protection if >=3 months |
| **PPIs (long-term)** | Omeprazole, esomeprazole | C. difficile infection, B12 deficiency, hypomagnesemia, bone loss, kidney disease | Avoid >8 weeks without indication; deprescribe when no longer needed |
| **Memantine + amantadine combination** | Both NMDA antagonists | Increased adverse effects; duplicative mechanism | Avoid concurrent use |

### 2023 Key Updates [^33^]

- Updated evidence base with new literature since 2019 edition
- New criteria added for several medication classes
- Modifications to existing criteria based on new evidence
- Enhanced formatting for clinical usability
- Expanded guidance on deprescribing approaches

## 7.3 STOPP/START Criteria Version 3 (2023)

### Overview

STOPP (Screening Tool of Older Persons' Prescriptions) identifies potentially inappropriate medications (overprescribing). START (Screening Tool to Alert doctors to Right Treatment) identifies potential prescribing omissions. Together they provide comprehensive medication appropriateness assessment. [^44^] [^45^] [^46^]

**STOPP/START Version 3**: 190 total criteria (133 STOPP + 57 START), a 66.7% increase from Version 2. Validated by Delphi consensus with 11 European geriatric pharmacotherapy experts. Organized by physiological systems. [^44^] [^46^]

### STOPP Criteria Categories

| System | Examples of PIMs |
|--------|-----------------|
| **Cardiovascular** | Loop diuretic for dependent ankle edema without clinical heart failure; CCB for isolated systolic hypertension (risk of reflex tachycardia) |
| **CNS** | Anticholinergics in patients with cognitive impairment; benzodiazepines for >4 weeks; antipsychotics for behavioral symptoms of dementia |
| **Gastrointestinal** | PPI for peptic ulcer without H. pylori testing; anticholinergic antispasmodic with constipation |
| **Respiratory** | Long-acting beta-agonist without inhaled corticosteroid in asthma; oral corticosteroids instead of inhaled for maintenance COPD |
| **Musculoskeletal** | Long-term NSAIDs without gastroprotection; long-term corticosteroids without bone protection |
| **Urogenital** | Anticholinergics with urinary retention; alpha-blockers in patients with syncope |
| **Endocrine** | Sulfonylureas with long half-life; metformin with eGFR <30 |
| **Falls risk** | Drugs causing orthostatic hypotension, sedation, or gait disturbance in patients with history of falls |

### START Criteria Categories (Prescribing Omissions)

| System | Examples of PPOs |
|--------|-----------------|
| **Cardiovascular** | ACE inhibitor with systolic heart failure; statin with documented CAD; anticoagulation with atrial fibrillation (CHA2DS2-VASc) |
| **Respiratory** | Inhaled corticosteroid for moderate-severe COPD; smoking cessation counseling |
| **CNS** | Antidepressant with moderate-severe depression; L-DOPA with Parkinson disease |
| **Gastrointestinal** | PPI with chronic high-dose NSAID use in high GI bleed risk |
| **Endocrine** | Metformin for type 2 diabetes (if not contraindicated); ACE inhibitor for diabetic nephropathy |
| **Musculoskeletal** | Calcium + vitamin D with osteoporosis; bisphosphonate with fracture history |

### Comparison: Beers vs. STOPP/START [^45^]

| Feature | Beers Criteria | STOPP/START |
|---------|---------------|-------------|
| Primary focus | PIMs to avoid | Overprescribing AND underprescribing |
| Overprescribing detection | Yes (comprehensive list) | Yes (STOPP criteria) |
| Underprescribing detection | Limited | Yes (START criteria) |
| Predictive validity for ADEs | Good | STOPP criteria more predictive of ADEs than Beers |
| Setting | US-focused | International/European origin |
| Target age | >=65 years | >=65 years (commonly used) |

## 7.4 Deprescribing Principles

### Framework for Systematic Deprescribing

| Step | Action |
|------|--------|
| **1. Document complete medication list** | Include all prescriptions, OTCs, supplements, herbal products; include indication for each |
| **2. Identify potentially inappropriate medications** | Apply Beers Criteria, STOPP/START, clinical judgment |
| **3. Assess overall medication burden** | Number of medications, regimen complexity, adherence barriers |
| **4. Evaluate each medication** | Is there a valid indication? Is it effective? Is the dosage appropriate? Are there adverse effects? |
| **5. Prioritize deprescribing candidates** | Consider: risk of harm, benefit vs. time to benefit, patient preference, goals of care |
| **6. Taper safely** | Gradual dose reduction for most medications to avoid withdrawal/rebound; monitor for adverse effects |
| **7. Monitor and follow up** | Assess outcomes after deprescribing; document rationale for continuing or stopping |

### Medications Commonly Targeted for Deprescribing

| Medication Class | Deprescribing Consideration | Taper Strategy |
|-----------------|---------------------------|----------------|
| **PPIs** | Reassess indication; deprescribe if no longer needed | Step down dose; switch to H2 blocker; stop if asymptomatic |
| **Antihypertensives** | Avoid overtreatment (SBP <130 may be too low in frail elderly); orthostatic hypotension | Reduce dose or discontinue one agent; monitor standing BP |
| **Hypoglycemic agents** | Avoid tight control in elderly (HbA1c <7% may cause harm); hypoglycemia risk | Reduce sulfonylurea or insulin dose; target HbA1c 7-8% in most frail elderly |
| **Statins** | Limited life expectancy (>75 years with no vascular disease) | Can stop if >75 with no prior MI/stroke and limited life expectancy |
| **Benzodiazepines** | Falls, cognitive impairment, dependence | Gradual taper over weeks to months (10-25% dose reduction every 1-2 weeks) |
| **Antipsychotics** | Metabolic effects, stroke risk, mortality in dementia | Gradual taper; monitor for behavioral symptoms |
| **Cholinesterase inhibitors** | Limited efficacy in advanced dementia; GI side effects | Gradual taper; monitor cognition/function |
| **Opioids** | Constipation, falls, dependence, sedation | Gradual taper 10-20% per week; multimodal pain management |

## 7.5 Medication Reconciliation

### Best Practices

- Obtain complete medication list at every transition of care (admission, discharge, transfer, office visit)
- Compare current medications against newly ordered medications
- Identify discrepancies, duplications, interactions, allergies
- Include OTC products, supplements, herbal remedies, eye drops, inhalers, creams
- Verify patient/caregiver understanding of each medication's purpose
- Document medication allergies and adverse drug reactions with specific reaction descriptions

### Adverse Drug Event (ADE) Prevention in Older Adults

| Strategy | Implementation |
|----------|---------------|
| **Monitor organ function** | Renal function (eGFR, creatinine) and hepatic function (LFTs) at least annually; dose-adjust for age-related changes |
| **Assess anticholinergic burden** | Calculate ACB score; minimize cumulative burden |
| **Watch for drug-drug interactions** | Review each new medication against existing regimen; use clinical decision support |
| **Patient education** | Teach purpose, side effects, and administration for each medication; use teach-back method |
| **Regular review** | Comprehensive medication review every 6 months for polypharmacy (>=5 medications) |
| **Simplify regimen** | Reduce dosing frequency when possible; synchronize refills; use pill organizers or blister packs |

### Anticholinergic Burden (ACB Score)

Medications with anticholinergic properties accumulate and increase risk of delirium, constipation, urinary retention, dry mouth, blurred vision, and cognitive impairment. Calculate cumulative burden:

| ACB Score | Risk Level |
|-----------|-----------|
| 0 | No anticholinergic burden |
| 1-2 | Low burden |
| 3+ | High burden — associated with increased mortality, hospitalization, cognitive decline |

Common anticholinergic medications: Diphenhydramine (3), oxybutynin (3), amitriptyline (3), paroxetine (2), tolterodine (2), hydroxyzine (2), furosemide (1), digoxin (1), nifedipine (1).

# Chapter 8: Cognitive Impairment in the Elderly

## 8.1 Overview

Cognitive impairment in older adults exists on a continuum from normal age-related changes through mild cognitive impairment (MCI) to dementia. Early detection enables implementation of supportive interventions, safety planning, treatment of reversible causes, and advance care planning. Cognitive impairment is NOT a normal part of aging — any decline warrants evaluation.

## 8.2 Screening Tools

### Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA)

| Feature | Details |
|---------|---------|
| **Time** | 10-15 minutes |
| **Score range** | 0-30 |
| **Cut-off** | <26 suggests cognitive impairment |
| **Education correction** | Add 1 point if <=12 years of education |
| **Domains** | Executive function, naming, memory, attention, language, abstraction, delayed recall, orientation |
| **Advantages** | More sensitive than MMSE for MCI; includes executive function testing; free online with registration; available in multiple languages |
| **Limitations** | Less effective in low-literacy populations; ceiling effect in highly educated individuals |

### Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE)

| Feature | Details |
|---------|---------|
| **Time** | 5-10 minutes |
| **Score range** | 0-30 |
| **Cut-offs** (education-adjusted) [^47^] | No high school: <26.5 (MCI), <26.5 (dementia); High school: <28.5 (MCI), <26.5 (dementia) |
| **Domains** | Orientation, registration, attention/calculation, recall, language, construction |
| **Advantages** | Most widely recognized; extensively validated; useful for tracking progression |
| **Limitations** | Ceiling effect in MCI; less sensitive to executive function; copyrighted (licensing fees) |

### Saint Louis University Mental Status Examination (SLUMS)

| Feature | Details |
|---------|---------|
| **Time** | 10 minutes |
| **Score range** | 0-30 |
| **Cut-offs** (education-adjusted) [^47^] [^48^] | No high school: <27.5 (MCI), <27.5 (dementia); High school: <29.5 (MCI), <27.5 (dementia) |
| **Domains** | Orientation, memory, attention, executive function (clock drawing), naming, verbal fluency, mathematical reasoning |
| **Advantages** | Free to use; more sensitive than MMSE for MCI; includes executive function [^48^] |
| **Limitations** | Less widely used than MMSE/MoCA; validation data less extensive |

### Comparison of Screening Tools [^47^] [^48^]

| Characteristic | MoCA | MMSE | SLUMS |
|----------------|------|------|-------|
| Sensitivity for MCI | **High** | Low | **High** |
| Sensitivity for dementia | High | **High** | High |
| Executive function testing | Yes | No | Yes |
| Cost | Free (registration) | Licensed | **Free** |
| Time | 10-15 min | 5-10 min | 10 min |
| Literacy requirement | Moderate | Moderate | Moderate |
| **Recommendation** | Preferred for MCI detection | Useful for tracking progression | Good alternative to MoCA |

### Clinical Recommendation

- Use one screening tool consistently within your practice
- Consider combining tests if initial screen negative but clinical concern high
- All positive screens require formal neuropsychological evaluation for definitive diagnosis
- Repeat screening annually for at-risk patients or when functional decline noted

## 8.3 Dementia Types and Management

### Alzheimer's Disease (60-70% of Cases)

| Feature | Details |
|---------|---------|
| **Presentation** | Gradual onset; progressive memory loss (episodic memory first); word-finding difficulty; visuospatial deficits; impaired judgment; apathy |
| **Pathology** | Amyloid plaques, neurofibrillary tangles (tau); neuronal loss in hippocampus and temporal lobes |
| **Imaging** | MRI: Medial temporal lobe atrophy, generalized cortical atrophy; FDG-PET: Posterior cingulate and temporal-parietal hypometabolism |
| **Pharmacologic management** | **Cholinesterase inhibitors**: Donepezil 5-10 mg daily, rivastigmine 3-12 mg daily, galantamine 8-24 mg BID — for mild to moderate; **Memantine** (Namenda) 10 mg BID — for moderate to severe; **Combination**: Donepezil + memantine for moderate-severe |
| **Nonpharmacologic** | Cognitive stimulation, structured routines, physical exercise, social engagement, caregiver education |
| **Disease-modifying therapies** | Anti-amyloid monoclonal antibodies (lecanemab, donanemab) for early AD with confirmed amyloid; require MRI monitoring for ARIA; eligibility and access evolving |

### Vascular Dementia (15-20%)

| Feature | Details |
|---------|---------|
| **Presentation** | Stepwise decline; executive dysfunction prominent; processing speed impairment; history of stroke/TIA; gait disturbance common |
| **Risk factors** | Hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, atrial fibrillation, smoking, prior stroke |
| **Imaging** | MRI: White matter hyperintensities, lacunar infarcts, strategic infarcts |
| **Management** | Aggressive vascular risk factor modification (BP control, statins, diabetes management, anticoagulation for AF); cholinesterase inhibitors may provide modest benefit |

### Lewy Body Dementia (5-10%)

| Feature | Details |
|---------|---------|
| **Presentation** | Fluctuating cognition with variable attention/alertness; visual hallucinations (well-formed); parkinsonism (rigidity, bradykinesia); REM sleep behavior disorder; autonomic dysfunction (orthostatic hypotension, constipation); neuroleptic sensitivity |
| **Pathology** | Alpha-synuclein Lewy bodies in cortex and brainstem |
| **Imaging** | MRI: Minimal atrophy early; SPECT: Reduced dopamine transporter activity |
| **Management** | **Rivastigmine** preferred cholinesterase inhibitor; levodopa for parkinsonism (start low, go slow); **AVOID ALL ANTIPSYCHOTICS** (severe sensitivity); melatonin for REM sleep behavior disorder; manage orthostatic hypotension |

### Frontotemporal Dementia (5-10%)

| Feature | Details |
|---------|---------|
| **Presentation** | Early behavioral variant (disinhibition, apathy, loss of empathy, compulsive behaviors) OR language variant (primary progressive aphasia); relatively preserved memory early; younger onset (45-65 years) |
| **Pathology** | Tau or TDP-43 inclusions in frontal and temporal lobes |
| **Imaging** | MRI: Disproportionate frontal and/or temporal lobe atrophy |
| **Management** | Primarily supportive; no FDA-approved disease-modifying therapies; SSRIs may help behavioral symptoms; caregiver support essential |

### Mixed Dementia

- Combination of Alzheimer's and vascular pathology; common in oldest old
- Management combines approaches from both (cholinesterase inhibitors + vascular risk factor optimization)

## 8.4 Comprehensive Dementia Evaluation

| Component | Assessment |
|-----------|-----------|
| **History** | Patient and collateral source; onset, progression, functional impact, behavioral changes, sleep, mood |
| **Cognitive testing** | MoCA, MMSE, or SLUMS; formal neuropsychological testing if diagnosis uncertain |
| **Physical exam** | Neurologic exam for focal deficits, parkinsonism, gait; cardiovascular exam |
| **Laboratory tests** | CBC, CMP, TSH, B12, folate, RPR/VDRL, HIV (per risk), LFTs, lipid panel |
| **Imaging** | MRI brain preferred (CT if MRI contraindicated); evaluate for stroke, tumor, NPH, subdural |
| **Other** | Depression screening (pseudodementia), medication review, sleep apnea evaluation |

### Reversible Causes of Cognitive Impairment (Rule Out)

| Cause | Evaluation |
|-------|-----------|
| **Medications** | Review for anticholinergics, sedatives, opioids |
| **Depression** | PHQ-9, geriatric depression scale; trial of antidepressant if indicated |
| **Hypothyroidism** | TSH |
| **Vitamin B12 deficiency** | Serum B12 (or methylmalonic acid if borderline) |
| **Normal pressure hydrocephalus** | MRI: Ventriculomegaly out of proportion to atrophy; gait MRI, cognitive decline triad |
| **Sleep apnea** | Sleep study if snoring, daytime somnolence |
| **Alcohol** | CAGE, MAST; elevated GGT, MCV |
| **Infection** | Syphilis serology, HIV if risk factors |
| **Metabolic** | Electrolytes, glucose, renal function, hepatic function |

## 8.5 Caregiver Support

### Caregiver Burden Assessment

- High rates of depression (40-60%), anxiety, physical illness among dementia caregivers
- Evaluate caregiver stress, health, and coping at each visit
- Assess safety of caregiving situation (burnout risk, abuse risk)

### Support Strategies

| Strategy | Resources |
|----------|-----------|
| **Caregiver education** | Disease course, behavioral management techniques, communication strategies |
| **Respite care** | Adult day programs, in-home respite, short-term residential care, family/friend relief |
| **Support groups** | Alzheimer's Association support groups (alz.org); local caregiver groups |
| **Technology** | Medication reminders, GPS tracking, home monitoring systems, telehealth |
| **Financial/legal planning** | Elder law attorney, advance directives, power of attorney, trusts |
| **Long-term care planning** | Adult day care, assisted living, memory care, nursing home; evaluate when >3 ADL dependencies |
| **Counseling** | Individual or family therapy for caregiver distress |

### Medicare/Medicaid Coverage

- Medicare covers some respite in hospice
- Medicaid waiver programs vary by state
- Alzheimer's Association 24/7 Helpline: 1-800-272-3900

## 8.6 Safety Assessment in Dementia

| Domain | Assessment | Intervention |
|--------|-----------|-------------|
| **Driving** | Road test, family observations, crash history | Reporting requirements vary by state; gradual transition plan |
| **Wandering** | History of getting lost, elopement risk | Door alarms, GPS tracking, ID bracelet, MedicAlert + Alzheimer's Association Safe Return |
| **Home safety** | Fall hazards, kitchen safety, medication access | Remove firearms, lock cleaning chemicals, stove auto-shutoff, simplify environment |
| **Financial management** | Bill payment, scam vulnerability, financial errors | Automate payments, monitor accounts, establish durable financial power of attorney |
| **Medication management** | Pill organizer use, missed doses, errors | Pillbox, blister packs, pre-poured medications, supervised administration |

# Chapter 9: Functional Assessment

## 9.1 Activities of Daily Living (ADLs)

ADLs are basic self-care tasks essential for independent living. ADL assessment guides care planning, determines need for supportive services, and monitors disease progression. [^20^]

### Basic ADLs (Katz Index)

| Activity | Independent Criteria |
|----------|---------------------|
| **Bathing** | Able to bathe entire body; may use assistive devices |
| **Dressing** | Able to select clothes and dress/undress independently |
| **Toileting** | Able to use toilet, clean self, manage clothing |
| **Transferring** | Able to move between bed, chair, toilet independently (may use assistive device) |
| **Continence** | Able to manage bladder and bowel function (with or without toileting schedule) |
| **Feeding** | Able to eat independently (not necessarily prepare food) |

**Scoring**: Katz ADL Scale scores 0-6 (1 point per activity); lower scores indicate greater dependence.
**Barthel Index**: 0-100 scoring system; widely used in rehabilitation settings. [^20^]

## 9.2 Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADLs)

IADLs are complex tasks required for independent community living. Loss of IADLs typically precedes loss of ADLs in progressive conditions. [^20^]

| Activity | Assessment Question |
|----------|-------------------|
| **Phone use** | Can the patient make and receive phone calls independently? |
| **Shopping** | Can the patient purchase groceries and necessities? |
| **Food preparation** | Can the patient prepare meals safely? |
| **Housekeeping** | Can the patient maintain their home? |
| **Laundry** | Can the patient wash clothes? |
| **Transportation** | Can the patient drive safely or use public transportation? |
| **Medication management** | Can the patient take medications correctly (right dose, right time)? |
| **Financial management** | Can the patient manage money, pay bills, avoid scams? |

**Lawton IADL Scale**: Scores 0-8 (0-27 in some versions); loss of 3+ IADLs indicates need for significant supportive services. [^20^]

### Clinical Significance of Functional Assessment

| Functional Status | Implication |
|-------------------|-------------|
| **Independent in all ADLs/IADLs** | Community living with minimal support |
| **Impaired IADLs, intact ADLs** | May need assistance with finances, transportation, shopping; early dementia, mild cognitive impairment |
| **Dependent in 1-2 ADLs** | Requires home services, caregiver support; consider home health aide |
| **Dependent in >=3 ADLs** | High risk for hospitalization and institutionalization; sharp increase in care burden; consider assisted living or nursing home [^49^] |
| **Functional decline in hospital** | Iatrogenic deconditioning; mobilize early; PT/OT evaluation |

## 9.3 Advance Care Planning

### Overview

Advance care planning (ACP) involves discussing and documenting patient preferences for future medical care, particularly important when patients may lose decision-making capacity. ACP improves quality of care, reduces unwanted interventions, and decreases caregiver stress. [^49^]

### Triggers for ACP Discussion [^49^]

| Trigger | Action |
|---------|--------|
| Newly diagnosed serious illness | Stage IV cancer, ESRD, advanced dementia, advanced COPD, advanced heart failure |
| Functional decline | Loss of >=3 ADLs |
| Pre-surgical planning | Major surgery with significant risk |
| Early cognitive decline | Identify surrogate decision-maker before capacity lost |
| Transition between care settings | Hospital to home, nursing home admission |
| Age >85 or frailty | Proactive discussion even without acute illness |

### Key Components of ACP

| Component | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Advance Directive** | Legal document including: Living will (treatment preferences at end of life) + Durable Power of Attorney for Healthcare (names surrogate decision-maker) |
| **POLST/MOLST** | Physician Orders for Life-Sustaining Treatment; portable medical orders for seriously ill patients; translates preferences into actionable orders across settings |
| **Code status** | Full code, DNR (do not resuscitate), DNR/DNI (do not intubate) |
| **Goals of care** | What matters most to the patient (quality vs. quantity of life, functional goals, home vs. hospital) |
| **Healthcare proxy** | Surrogate decision-maker identification and discussion with designated person |
| **Capacity assessment** | Document decision-making capacity when completing advance directives |

### Capacity Assessment (5 Components)

| Component | Assessment |
|-----------|-----------|
| **Understand** | Can the patient understand the relevant information about their condition and treatment options? |
| **Appreciate** | Can the patient appreciate how this information applies to their situation? |
| **Reason** | Can the patient reason through the risks and benefits of options? |
| **Express choice** | Can the patient communicate a stable choice? |
| **Values** | Can the patient describe their values and goals? |

## 9.4 Palliative Care Integration

### Palliative Care Principles

Palliative care is specialized medical care focused on relief of symptoms and suffering at any stage of serious illness. It is **NOT** the same as hospice and can be provided concurrently with disease-modifying treatment.

| Principle | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Patient-centered** | Honors patient goals, values, and preferences |
| **Interdisciplinary** | Physician, nurse, social work, chaplaincy, pharmacist, PT/OT |
| **Symptom-focused** | Pain, dyspnea, nausea, fatigue, constipation, anxiety, depression |
| **Whole-person** | Physical, psychological, social, spiritual dimensions |
| **Concurrent** | Provided alongside curative/disease-modifying treatment |
| **Any stage** | Appropriate early in serious illness, not just end-of-life |

### Hospice Eligibility (Medicare Criteria)

| Criterion | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Prognosis** | Life expectancy <=6 months if disease runs natural course |
| **Functional decline** | Significant decline in functional status |
| **Weight loss** | Progressive unintentional weight loss |
| **Symptom burden** | Increasing symptoms despite optimal management |
| **Biomarkers** | Disease-specific indicators of advanced stage |
| **Physician certification** | Attending physician and hospice medical director must certify |

**Hospice covered services**: Nursing care, medications related to terminal diagnosis, medical equipment (hospital bed, wheelchair), supplies, respite care, bereavement support, social work, chaplaincy, volunteer services.

### Symptom Management in Palliative Care

| Symptom | Management |
|---------|-----------|
| **Pain** | WHO analgesic ladder: non-opioid (acetaminophen, NSAIDs) -> mild opioid (tramadol, codeine) -> strong opioid (morphine, oxycodone, hydromorphone); adjuvants (gabapentin, TCAs, steroids) |
| **Dyspnea** | Low-dose opioids (morphine 2.5-5 mg), low-dose anxiolytics, positioning (elevate head), oxygen if hypoxemic, fan to face, pulmonary interventions |
| **Constipation** | Prophylactic bowel regimen with all opioid use: senna + polyethylene glycol; add bisacodyl, suppositories, enemas as needed |
| **Nausea** | Target mechanism: prokinetic (metoclopramide for gastroparesis), antihistamine (meclizine for vestibular), antidopaminergic (haloperidol for chemical), 5-HT3 antagonist (ondansetron for chemotherapy) |
| **Delirium** | Nonpharmacologic first (reorientation, sensory aids, sleep hygiene); antipsychotics for severe agitation threatening safety |
| **Terminal secretions (death rattle)** | Anticholinergics: glycopyrrolate 0.2-0.4 mg q4h or hyoscyamine 0.125-0.25 mg q4h; repositioning |

## 9.5 Goals of Care Discussions

### Framework for Effective Communication

| Step | Technique |
|------|-----------|
| **1. Ask** | "What do you understand about your illness?" "What are your hopes and worries?" "What is most important to you?" |
| **2. Tell** | Provide prognostic information in understandable terms; avoid precise timelines unless certain |
| **3. Align** | Connect medical recommendations to patient's stated goals and values |
| **4. Recommend** | Offer recommendation based on goals; "Given what you've told me, I would recommend..." |
| **5. Document** | Record preferences in medical record; update periodically and with clinical changes |

### Communication Techniques

| Technique | Application |
|-----------|-------------|
| **"Wish" statements** | "I wish we had a treatment that would cure this" — acknowledges emotion |
| **NURSE** | Name, Understand, Respect, Support, Explore — framework for responding to emotion |
| **SPIKES** | Setting, Perception, Invitation, Knowledge, Emotions, Strategy/Support — delivering bad news |
| **Avoid jargon** | Use "serious illness" rather than specific prognosis estimates |
| **Allow silence** | Give patients and families time to process |
| **Ask-Tell-Ask** | Ask what they know, tell new information, ask what they understood |

# Chapter 10: Elder Abuse

## 10.1 Overview

Elder abuse refers to intentional or negligent acts by a caregiver or trusted individual that cause harm or serious risk of harm to an older adult. It affects an estimated 10% of community-dwelling older adults and 50% of those with dementia. Healthcare providers are uniquely positioned to identify and intervene. [^50^] [^51^]

## 10.2 Types of Elder Abuse

| Type | Definition | Signs and Indicators |
|------|-----------|---------------------|
| **Physical abuse** | Infliction of physical pain or injury | Unexplained bruises (especially inner arm/thigh), fractures, burns, restraint marks, pattern injuries, inconsistent explanation |
| **Emotional/psychological abuse** | Verbal assaults, threats, harassment, intimidation, humiliation, isolation | Depression, withdrawal, anxiety, fearfulness around specific person, sudden change in alertness, unusual behavior |
| **Sexual abuse** | Non-consensual sexual contact of any kind | Bruising to genitals or breasts, unexplained STIs or genital infections, torn/stained underwear, bleeding |
| **Financial exploitation** | Illegal or improper use of funds, property, or assets | Unexplained bank withdrawals, missing valuables, sudden will changes, unpaid bills despite resources, new "best friends," forged signatures |
| **Neglect** | Failure to provide basic needs (food, shelter, medical care, hygiene) | Poor hygiene, malnutrition, dehydration, untreated medical conditions, unsafe living conditions, pressure injuries, inadequate clothing |
| **Self-neglect** | Inability or refusal to meet own basic needs | Similar to neglect but without identified perpetrator; may coexist with cognitive impairment, depression, substance use |

## 10.3 Screening

### Elder Abuse Suspicion Index (EASI) [^50^] [^51^]

The EASI is a 6-item tool completed by the healthcare professional:

| Item | Question |
|------|----------|
| 1 | Have you relied on people for any of the following: bathing, dressing, shopping, banking, or meals? |
| 2 | Has anyone prevented you from getting food, clothes, medication, glasses, hearing aids, or medical care, or from being with people you wanted to be with? |
| 3 | Has anyone made you afraid, touched you in ways that you did not want, or hurt you physically? |
| 4 | Has anyone tried to force you to sign papers or to use your money against your will? |
| 5 | Has anyone made you feel ashamed or threatened? |
| 6 | If you needed help, do you have someone you trust and can go to? (If answer is "no," consider abuse) |

- **Sensitivity**: 0.77; **Specificity**: 0.44 [^51^]
- **Administration time**: ~2 minutes
- **Validated in**: Family practice and ambulatory care settings
- **Limitation**: Low specificity — positive screen requires further investigation

### Other Screening Tools [^51^]

| Tool | Items | Administration | Setting |
|------|-------|---------------|---------|
| **H-S/EAST** | 6 | Self-report or interview | Community |
| **VASS** | 12 | Self-report | Various |
| **BASE (Brief Elder Screen)** | 4 | Self-report | Primary care |
| **CASE (Caregiver Abuse Screen)** | 8 | Caregiver self-report | Various |
| **EAI (Elder Assessment Instrument)** | 44 | Professional assessment | Comprehensive evaluation |

### Clinical Red Flags

| Indicator | Action |
|-----------|--------|
| Unexplained injuries in various stages of healing | Thorough documentation; photograph if consent obtained |
| Frequent ER visits or physician changes | Evaluate for care coordination issues; interview patient privately |
| Poor hygiene or malnutrition despite adequate resources | Assess for neglect; evaluate cognitive and functional status |
| Social isolation | Identify perpetrator of isolation; assess for controlling caregiver |
| Caregiver substance abuse or mental illness | Assess caregiver burden and support needs |
| Conflicting accounts of injury from patient and caregiver | Interview patient alone; document discrepancies |
| Patient fearful or withdrawn around caregiver | Private interview essential; direct questioning about safety |
| Missing medications or overdosing | Assess medication management system; rule out diversion |

## 10.4 Mandatory Reporting

### Legal Requirements [^50^]

| Component | Details |
|-----------|---------|
| **State laws** | All 50 states have elder abuse reporting laws; specifics vary by jurisdiction |
| **Mandatory reporters** | Healthcare providers are mandatory reporters in most states; some states include all citizens |
| **Where to report** | Adult Protective Services (APS) or designated state agency; law enforcement if imminent danger |
| **Long-term care** | Some states require separate reporting to facility licensing agency or long-term care ombudsman |
| **Federal reporting** | Nursing facilities must report to State Survey and Certification Agency |
| **Timing** | Immediate reporting when abuse is suspected; delays may endanger patient |

### Reporting Process

1. **Document objectively**: Record physical findings with measurements; use direct quotes from patient; document discrepancies between accounts
2. **Photograph injuries**: With patient consent; include measuring device (ruler) for scale
3. **Report to APS**: Contact local Adult Protective Services (state-specific phone numbers available at eldercare.acl.gov)
4. **Ensure patient safety**: If imminent danger, arrange for immediate safe placement; involve social work
5. **Follow up**: Verify investigation occurs; continue to monitor patient
6. **Maintain confidentiality**: While fulfilling reporting obligations; explain reporting process to patient

## 10.5 Prevention Strategies

| Strategy | Implementation |
|----------|---------------|
| **Social connection** | Maintain community involvement; identify isolated older adults |
| **Caregiver support** | Respite services, support groups, education about dementia behaviors, mental health services |
| **Financial safeguards** | Direct deposit, trusted contact on accounts, power of attorney with checks and balances |
| **Advance planning** | Advance directives, durable power of attorney completed while cognitively intact |
| **Home services** | Home health, Meals on Wheels, homemaker services to reduce caregiver burden |
| **Regular monitoring** | Frequent check-ins by healthcare providers, neighbors, family |
| **Education** | Public awareness about signs of abuse and reporting mechanisms |

## 10.6 Self-Neglect

Self-neglect is the most common form of elder mistreatment reported to Adult Protective Services. It involves the inability or refusal to meet one's own basic needs.

| Risk Factors | Assessment | Management |
|-------------|-----------|------------|
| Cognitive impairment (dementia), depression, substance use, social isolation, chronic illness, hoarding disorder | Functional status, cognitive screening, mental health evaluation, home safety assessment | Address underlying cause; involuntary intervention may be necessary if lacks capacity and significant danger; APS referral; conservatorship/guardianship if lacks capacity and refuses help |

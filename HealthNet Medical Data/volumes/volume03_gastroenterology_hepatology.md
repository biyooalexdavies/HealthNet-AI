# Chapter 1: GERD and Acid-Related Disorders {#chapter1}

## 1.1 Overview

Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) is a chronic, highly prevalent disorder defined by the reflux of gastric contents into the esophagus causing troublesome symptoms or complications. It is one of the most common gastrointestinal conditions worldwide, affecting approximately 20% of the Western population. The pathophysiology is multifactorial and involves dysfunction of the anti-reflux barrier at the gastroesophageal junction.

### 1.1.1 Epidemiology
- Prevalence: 18-28% in North America and Europe; increasing globally
- No significant gender predominance for typical GERD; men at higher risk for complications
- Risk increases with age, obesity, and Western dietary patterns
- Strong association with obesity: each 1 kg/m2 increase in BMI increases GERD risk by ~2.5% [^91^]

### 1.1.2 Pathophysiology

| Mechanism | Description | Clinical Correlation |
|-----------|-------------|----------------------|
| Transient LES relaxation (TLESR) | Inappropriate relaxation of the lower esophageal sphincter unrelated to swallowing | Primary mechanism in most patients with non-erosive reflux disease (NERD) |
| Hypotensive resting LES pressure | Baseline LES tone below critical threshold (<10 mmHg) | More common in patients with erosive esophagitis |
| Impaired esophageal acid clearance | Reduced peristaltic clearance or salivary bicarbonate | Prolonged acid exposure; worse in supine position |
| Hiatal hernia | Displacement of the gastroesophageal junction above the diaphragm | Associated with more severe reflux and complications |
| Delayed gastric emptying | Prolonged gastric retention increases volume available for reflux | Consider in refractory cases; assess with gastric emptying study |
| Increased intra-abdominal pressure | Obesity, pregnancy, tight clothing | Weight loss is the most effective lifestyle intervention |

## 1.2 Clinical Presentation

### 1.2.1 Typical (Esophageal) Symptoms
- **Heartburn**: Retrosternal burning sensation, often postprandial or nocturnal
- **Regurgitation**: Sour/bitter taste in mouth; passive reflux of gastric contents
- **Chest pain**: May mimic cardiac pain; must exclude cardiac etiology first

### 1.2.2 Atypical (Extra-Esophageal) Symptoms
- Chronic cough (particularly nocturnal)
- Hoarseness and laryngitis (reflux laryngitis)
- Asthma exacerbation
- Dental erosions
- Globus sensation (lump in throat)
- Chronic sinusitis

> **Important**: Extra-esophageal symptoms have lower response rates to PPI therapy. A careful evaluation to exclude non-GERD causes is essential before attributing these to reflux.

## 1.3 Alarm Features (Red Flags)

**The presence of any alarm feature mandates prompt upper endoscopy (EGD):**

| Alarm Feature | Significance | Recommended Action |
|---------------|-------------|-------------------|
| Dysphagia (progressive) | May indicate stricture, malignancy, or severe esophagitis | Urgent EGD |
| Odynophagia (painful swallowing) | Severe esophagitis, pill esophagitis, infectious esophagitis | Urgent EGD |
| Unintentional weight loss (>5% body weight) | Malignancy until proven otherwise | Urgent EGD |
| GI bleeding (melena, hematemesis, hematochezia) | Bleeding ulcer, malignancy, severe esophagitis | Emergency EGD |
| Iron-deficiency anemia | Chronic blood loss; malignancy or severe esophagitis | EGD + colonoscopy |
| Persistent vomiting | Gastric outlet obstruction, malignancy | Urgent EGD with imaging |
| New-onset symptoms age >60 years | Higher pre-test probability of malignancy | EGD indicated |
| Family history of upper GI malignancy or Barrett's esophagus | Increased personal risk | Screening EGD |

## 1.4 Diagnosis

### 1.4.1 Empiric PPI Trial
- Standard first approach for classic symptoms without alarm features
- Administer PPI once daily 30-60 minutes before breakfast for 4-8 weeks
- Positive response supports GERD diagnosis (sensitivity ~80%, specificity ~50-60%)

### 1.4.2 Upper Endoscopy (EGD)
- Indicated for: alarm features, screening for Barrett's esophagus in high-risk patients, refractory symptoms, and evaluation before anti-reflux surgery
- Endoscopic findings in GERD: esophagitis (Los Angeles grades A-D), Barrett's esophagus, strictures, hiatal hernia

### 1.4.3 Reflux Testing
- **24-hour pH monitoring**: Gold standard for documenting acid reflux; indicated when EGD is normal and diagnosis is uncertain
- **Impedance-pH testing**: Detects both acid and non-acid reflux; use in patients with persistent symptoms on PPI therapy
- **Esophageal manometry**: Required before fundoplication to exclude achalasia and assess motility

## 1.5 Differential Diagnosis

| Condition | Key Distinguishing Features | Diagnostic Test |
|-----------|---------------------------|-----------------|
| Eosinophilic esophagitis | Young male, dysphagia to solids, food impaction, history of allergies | Endoscopy with biopsy (>15 eos/hpf) |
| Achalasia | Progressive dysphagia to solids AND liquids, regurgitation of undigested food, absent peristalsis | High-resolution manometry |
| Gastric ulcer | Epigastric pain related to meals, may improve with antacids | Upper endoscopy |
| Coronary artery disease | Chest pain with exertion, ECG changes, elevated troponins | ECG, troponins, cardiac workup |
| Functional dyspepsia | Postprandial fullness, early satiety, epigastric pain, no retrosternal burning | Normal endoscopy, Rome IV criteria |
| Pill esophagitis | Sudden-onset odynophagia, medication history (bisphosphonates, NSAIDs, doxycycline, potassium, iron) | Endoscopy showing localized ulceration |
| Rumination syndrome | Effortless regurgitation of recently ingested food within minutes, re-chewing and re-swallowing | Clinical diagnosis; impedance monitoring |

## 1.6 Management

### 1.6.1 Lifestyle Modifications (All Patients)

| Intervention | Recommendation | Evidence Level |
|-------------|----------------|----------------|
| Weight loss | Target BMI <25; strongest evidence-based intervention | High |
| Head of bed elevation | 6-8 inches (20 cm) using blocks or wedge pillow | Moderate |
| Meal timing | Avoid eating >3 hours before lying down | Moderate |
| Dietary triggers | Limit fatty/fried foods, chocolate, peppermint, citrus, tomato, coffee, alcohol | Low-moderate |
| Smoking cessation | Strongly recommended | Moderate |
| Avoid tight clothing | Reduces intra-abdominal pressure | Low |
| Sleep position | Left lateral decubitus position reduces nocturnal reflux | Moderate |

### 1.6.2 Step-Up Pharmacologic Approach

**Step 1: Mild/Intermittent Symptoms**
- Antacids (calcium carbonate 500-1000 mg PRN): rapid, short-duration relief
- Alginate-based formulations (e.g., Gaviscon) after meals and at bedtime
- H2-receptor antagonists: famotidine 20-40 mg BID

**Step 2: Moderate-Severe Symptoms or Frequent Symptoms (>2 days/week)**
- PPI once daily 30-60 minutes before breakfast for 8 weeks:

| PPI | Standard Dose | Notes |
|-----|--------------|-------|
| Omeprazole | 20 mg daily | Generic, widely available |
| Esomeprazole | 20-40 mg daily | Higher bioavailability than omeprazole |
| Lansoprazole | 30 mg daily | Available as orally disintegrating tablet |
| Pantoprazole | 40 mg daily | Lower CYP interaction potential |
| Rabeprazole | 20 mg daily | Less CYP metabolism |
| Dexlansoprazole | 30-60 mg daily | Dual-release formulation |

**Step 3: Refractory GERD (Persistent Symptoms on Once-Daily PPI)**
- Confirm adherence to correct dosing (before meals, not after)
- Switch to a different PPI (variable individual response)
- Twice-daily PPI (before breakfast and dinner)
- Evaluate for non-GERD causes (functional heartburn, rumination)
- Impedance-pH testing on PPI therapy to assess for non-acid reflux or poor correlation
- Consider surgical evaluation

### 1.6.3 Surgical and Endoscopic Options
- **Laparoscopic Nissen fundoplication**: 360-degree wrap; best long-term outcomes; indicated for documented GERD with incomplete response to PPI or patient preference to avoid lifelong medication
- **Magnetic sphincter augmentation (LINX device)**: Alternative to fundoplication; preserves ability to belch and vomit
- **Transoral incisionless fundoplication (TIF)**: Endoscopic approach for select patients without hiatal hernia >2 cm
- All surgical candidates require preoperative manometry, pH testing, and EGD

## 1.7 Long-Term PPI Therapy: Safety Considerations

Long-term PPI use is generally well-tolerated and safe for most patients. The following associations have been reported, though causality remains uncertain for many:

| Potential Risk | Evidence Summary | Clinical Action |
|---------------|------------------|-----------------|
| C. difficile infection | Association in observational studies; PPI as independent risk factor | Avoid unnecessary PPI use; lowest effective dose |
| Hypomagnesemia | Rare; typically after >1 year of use | Monitor magnesium annually if long-term use |
| Vitamin B12 deficiency | Possible malabsorption with prolonged use | Consider monitoring in elderly or malnourished patients |
| Bone fractures | Epidemiologic association; mechanism unclear | Ensure adequate calcium/vitamin D; not a contraindication |
| CKD | Observational association | Not proven causal |
| Dementia | Inconsistent observational data | No proven causal link |

> **Key Principle**: Use PPIs at the lowest effective dose. Attempt periodic step-down or on-demand therapy in stable patients. Do not discontinue PPIs abruptly in patients with severe esophagitis or Barrett's esophagus. [^89^]

## 1.8 Barrett's Esophagus

### 1.8.1 Definition and Diagnosis
- Replacement of normal squamous epithelium with columnar epithelium containing intestinal metaplasia
- Requires endoscopic visualization of salmon-colored mucosa extending above the gastroesophageal junction AND histologic confirmation of intestinal metaplasia with goblet cells
- Diagnosed and confirmed by systematic biopsy protocol (Seattle protocol: 4-quadrant biopsies every 1-2 cm)

### 1.8.2 Risk Factors and Screening
- Risk factors: chronic GERD symptoms (>5 years), male sex, age >50, central obesity, smoking, family history of Barrett's or esophageal adenocarcinoma
- **Screening EGD**: Consider in men with chronic GERD and >/=2 risk factors; women generally not screened unless multiple risk factors

### 1.8.3 Surveillance
| Dysplasia Grade | Management |
|----------------|------------|
| No dysplasia | Surveillance every 3-5 years |
| Indefinite for dysplasia | Repeat EGD in 6-12 months with optimized PPI |
| Low-grade dysplasia | Endoscopic eradication therapy (preferred) or surveillance every 6-12 months |
| High-grade dysplasia | Endoscopic eradication therapy (radiofrequency ablation + endoscopic mucosal resection of visible lesions) |

### 1.8.4 Management
- High-dose PPI therapy (omeprazole 40 mg BID or equivalent) for all patients
- Endoscopic eradication therapy for all grades of dysplasia and T1a adenocarcinoma
- Lifelong surveillance after eradication therapy

## 1.9 Special Populations

| Population | Considerations |
|-----------|---------------|
| **Pregnancy** | Lifestyle modifications first-line; antacids and sucralfate safe; H2 blockers (famotidine) preferred if pharmacotherapy needed; PPIs (omeprazole, lansoprazole) considered safe |
| **Pediatrics** | GERD common in infants but usually resolves by 12 months; thickened feeds, positioning; PPIs reserved for erosive esophagitis only |
| **Elderly** | Higher risk of complications; maintain low threshold for endoscopy; PPIs generally well-tolerated; monitor for drug interactions |

## 1.10 Follow-up and When to Refer
- Response to empiric PPI within 2-4 weeks expected
- Refer to gastroenterology for: alarm features, refractory symptoms despite optimized PPI, Barrett's esophagus, need for surgical evaluation, atypical symptoms with unclear etiology

# Chapter 2: Peptic Ulcer Disease {#chapter2}

## 2.1 Overview

Peptic ulcer disease (PUD) is characterized by a discontinuity in the gastrointestinal mucosa extending through the muscularis mucosae into the deeper layers of the submucosa. It most commonly affects the stomach (gastric ulcer) and the proximal duodenum (duodenal ulcer). The two principal etiologies are *Helicobacter pylori* infection and NSAID use. Less common causes include Zollinger-Ellison syndrome, stress ulceration, viral infections (CMV, HSV), and malignancy. [^88^]

### 2.1.1 Epidemiology
- Lifetime prevalence: 5-10% of the global population
- Duodenal ulcers outnumber gastric ulcers approximately 3:1
- *H. pylori* infection accounts for 70-90% of duodenal ulcers and 60-70% of gastric ulcers
- NSAID/aspirin use accounts for approximately 15-30% of ulcers
- Significant overlap: *H. pylori*-positive patients on NSAIDs have the highest ulcer risk

### 2.1.2 Pathophysiology
The balance between aggressive factors (acid, pepsin, *H. pylori*, NSAIDs) and defensive factors (mucus, bicarbonate, prostaglandins, mucosal blood flow, epithelial regeneration) determines ulcer formation.

| Factor | Mechanism |
|--------|-----------|
| *H. pylori* | Urease production, cytotoxins (CagA, VacA), disrupts mucus layer, triggers inflammatory response |
| NSAIDs | Systemic inhibition of COX-1, reduced prostaglandin synthesis, impaired mucosal defense |
| Acid/pepsin | Required for ulcer formation; higher acid output in duodenal ulcers; often normal in gastric ulcers |
| Smoking | Reduces mucosal blood flow, increases acid secretion, delays healing |
| Alcohol | Mucosal irritation at high concentrations; may increase *H. pylori* virulence |

### 2.1.3 Clinical Features

| Feature | Duodenal Ulcer | Gastric Ulcer |
|---------|---------------|---------------|
| Pain timing | 2-3 hours after meals; nocturnal pain | Within 15-30 minutes after meals |
| Pain relief | With food or antacids | Worsened by food |
| Weight change | Normal or weight gain | Weight loss (fear of eating) |
| *H. pylori* prevalence | 90-95% | 60-80% |
| Malignant potential | Essentially never | Must biopsy all gastric ulcers to exclude malignancy |
| Location | Bulb (95%) | Lesser curvature, antrum |

## 2.2 Diagnosis

### 2.2.1 Endoscopy (EGD)
- Gold standard for diagnosis; allows direct visualization, biopsy, and therapeutic intervention
- **All gastric ulcers must be biopsied** (multiple biopsies from ulcer edge and base) to exclude malignancy
- Repeat endoscopy at 8-12 weeks for gastric ulcers to confirm healing and repeat biopsy if not healed
- Duodenal ulcers generally do not require repeat endoscopy if symptoms resolve

### 2.2.2 *H. pylori* Testing
- Testing is indicated in ALL patients with PUD [^88^]

| Test | Sensitivity | Specificity | Notes |
|------|------------|-------------|-------|
| Urea breath test | 95% | 95% | Non-invasive; test of cure preferred method |
| Stool antigen test | 94% | 92% | Non-invasive; suitable for test of cure |
| Endoscopic biopsy (histology) | 95% | 95% | Required during EGD; can assess gastritis |
| Endoscopic biopsy (rapid urease) | 90-95% | 95% | Quick result (<24 hours); inexpensive |
| Serology (IgG antibody) | 85% | 80% | Cannot distinguish active from past infection |

> **Critical Testing Considerations**: PPIs, bismuth, and antibiotics suppress *H. pylori* and cause false-negative results. Discontinue PPIs for 2 weeks and bismuth/antibiotics for 4 weeks before testing if possible.

### 2.2.3 Test-of-Cure
- Must be performed in ALL patients after *H. pylori* eradication therapy
- Use urea breath test or stool antigen test (not serology)
- Perform at least 4 weeks after completing antibiotics and 2 weeks after stopping PPIs

## 2.3 Differential Diagnosis

| Condition | Key Distinguishing Features |
|-----------|---------------------------|
| GERD | Retrosternal burning, regurgitation, worse with recumbency |
| Functional dyspepsia | Chronic epigastric pain without structural lesion; normal endoscopy |
| Gastric malignancy | Progressive symptoms, weight loss, persistent vomiting, anemia; must biopsy all gastric ulcers |
| Biliary colic | Right upper quadrant pain, radiates to back/right shoulder, after fatty meals |
| Pancreatitis | Severe epigastric pain radiating to back, elevated amylase/lipase |
| Mesenteric ischemia | Postprandial pain, food fear, weight loss, vascular risk factors |
| Cardiac ischemia | Chest pain with exertion, dyspnea, diaphoresis; ECG changes |
| Zollinger-Ellison syndrome | Multiple or refractory ulcers, diarrhea, elevated fasting gastrin |

## 2.4 *H. pylori* Eradication Regimens

Current guidelines recommend 14-day regimens to maximize eradication rates (>90% target). [^88^] [^94^]

| Regimen | Components | Duration | Notes |
|---------|-----------|----------|-------|
| **Bismuth quadruple therapy (preferred)** | PPI BID + bismuth subsalicylate 300 mg QID + tetracycline 500 mg QID + metronidazole 500 mg TID | 14 days | First-line in most regions; effective despite clarithromycin resistance |
| Concomitant therapy | PPI BID + amoxicillin 1 g BID + clarithromycin 500 mg BID + metronidazole/tinidazole 500 mg BID | 14 days | Not using bismuth; avoid if macrolide exposure |
| Clarithromycin triple | PPI BID + amoxicillin 1 g BID + clarithromycin 500 mg BID | 14 days | Only if clarithromycin resistance <15% locally and no prior macrolide |
| Vonoprazan-based dual | Vonoprazan 20 mg BID + amoxicillin 1 g TID | 14 days | Potassium-competitive acid blocker; high eradication rates |
| Vonoprazan triple | Vonoprazan 20 mg BID + amoxicillin 1 g BID + clarithromycin 500 mg BID | 14 days | If no macrolide resistance |

> **Salvage Therapy**: After first-line failure, use bismuth quadruple therapy if not used initially, or fluoroquinolone-based regimens. Always perform culture with sensitivity testing if available after two treatment failures.

### 2.4.1 *H. pylori* Eradication: Key Principles
- Emphasize adherence: full 14-day course, all doses
- Use 14-day regimens (superior to 7- or 10-day)
- Document test-of-cure in all patients
- Avoid clarithromycin triple therapy if prior macrolide exposure or local resistance >15%

## 2.5 NSAID-Related Ulcer Management

| Scenario | Management |
|----------|------------|
| Ulcer with active NSAID use | Discontinue NSAID if possible; start PPI (omeprazole 20-40 mg daily); test and treat *H. pylori* |
| Must continue NSAID | Lowest effective NSAID dose + PPI or switch to COX-2 selective + PPI; test and treat *H. pylori* |
| Primary prevention (high-risk patient on NSAIDs) | PPI prophylaxis (omeprazole 20 mg daily) OR misoprostol 200 mcg QID |
| Aspirin for cardiovascular disease | Continue aspirin if possible; add PPI; use lowest aspirin dose (81 mg) |

**High-risk factors for NSAID ulcer complications**: Age >65, prior ulcer/bleeding, concomitant anticoagulants or corticosteroids, high-dose NSAIDs, dual antiplatelet therapy, *H. pylori* infection.

## 2.6 Complications

### 2.6.1 Bleeding (Most Common)
- Presentation: hematemesis, coffee-ground emesis, melena, hematochezia (brisk bleeding)
- Management: see Chapter 10 for detailed approach
- Endoscopic evaluation within 24 hours with hemostasis if active bleeding or visible vessel
- High-dose IV PPI after successful endoscopic hemostasis

### 2.6.2 Perforation
- Presentation: sudden severe abdominal pain, rigid abdomen, free air on upright chest X-ray or CT
- Mortality: 5-25% depending on patient factors and timing
- Management: urgent surgical repair (laparoscopic or open); omental patch for duodenal perforation; gastric ulcer perforation requires biopsy to exclude malignancy

### 2.6.3 Gastric Outlet Obstruction
- Presentation: early satiety, nausea, vomiting (large-volume, non-bilious), abdominal distension, weight loss
- Causes: edema and scarring from pyloric/channel ulcer, malignancy
- Management: NG decompression, IV PPI, IV fluids; endoscopic balloon dilatation; surgery if refractory

### 2.6.4 Penetration
- Ulcer extends into adjacent organ (pancreas most common)
- Persistent deep epigastric pain radiating to back; amylase/lipase may be elevated

### 2.6.5 Forrest Classification of Ulcer Bleeding

| Classification | Endoscopic Appearance | Risk | Endoscopic Therapy |
|---------------|----------------------|------|-------------------|
| IA | Active spurting hemorrhage | Very high | Required |
| IB | Active oozing hemorrhage | Very high | Required |
| IIA | Non-bleeding visible vessel | High | Recommended |
| IIB | Adherent clot | Moderate | Consider (after clot removal) |
| IIC | Flat pigmented spot (hematin) | Low | Not indicated |
| III | Clean base | Very low | Not indicated |

## 2.7 Medications for PUD

| Drug | Dosing | Indication | Notes |
|------|--------|------------|-------|
| Omeprazole | 20-40 mg daily | Healing, maintenance | Generic; most widely studied |
| Esomeprazole | 20-40 mg daily | Healing, maintenance | S-enantiomer of omeprazole |
| Pantoprazole | 40 mg daily | Healing, maintenance | Lower drug interaction potential |
| Misoprostol | 200 mcg QID with meals | NSAID ulcer prevention | Diarrhea common; abortifacient |
| Sucralfate | 1 g QID (1 hour before meals and bedtime) | Ulcer healing | Minimal absorption; safe in pregnancy |
| Vonoprazan | 20 mg daily | Healing, *H. pylori* eradication | Faster acid suppression than PPIs |

## 2.8 Special Populations

| Population | Key Considerations |
|-----------|-------------------|
| **Pregnancy** | Avoid bismuth subsalicylate (3rd trimester); test-and-treat *H. pylori* if positive; PPIs and amoxicillin generally safe; misoprostol contraindicated |
| **Elderly** | Highest bleeding risk with NSAIDs; avoid NSAIDs when possible; use PPI prophylaxis when NSAIDs necessary; higher mortality with complications |
| **Pediatrics** | *H. pylori* testing only when PUD or gastric MALT lymphoma present; primary PUD rare in children |
| **Anticoagulated patients** | Highest bleeding risk; avoid NSAIDs; PPI prophylaxis recommended |

## 2.9 Prevention

- Eradicate *H. pylori* in all infected patients
- Avoid unnecessary NSAIDs; use lowest effective dose when required
- PPI or misoprostol prophylaxis for high-risk patients on chronic NSAIDs
- COX-2 selective NSAIDs with PPI for highest-risk patients
- Smoking cessation
- Limit alcohol consumption

# Chapter 3: Irritable Bowel Syndrome {#chapter3}

## 3.1 Overview

Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) is a functional gastrointestinal disorder characterized by recurrent abdominal pain associated with alterations in bowel habits. It is the most commonly diagnosed GI condition, affecting an estimated 10-15% of the global population, with significant impacts on quality of life and healthcare utilization. IBS is classified as a disorder of gut-brain interaction, involving visceral hypersensitivity, altered gut motility, mucosal immune activation, intestinal permeability changes, and dysbiosis.

### 3.1.1 Epidemiology
- Global prevalence: 10-15% (varies by criteria used)
- Female:male ratio approximately 2:1 in clinical settings
- Peak onset: late teens to early 40s
- Strong association with anxiety, depression, somatization, and history of childhood adversity

### 3.1.2 Pathophysiology

| Mechanism | Role in IBS |
|-----------|-------------|
| Visceral hypersensitivity | Lowered pain threshold to intestinal distension; central sensitization |
| Altered gut motility | Variable; may involve delayed or accelerated transit depending on subtype |
| Gut-brain axis dysfunction | Bidirectional signaling abnormalities; stress amplifies symptoms |
| Intestinal permeability | "Leaky gut" in subset; particularly post-infectious IBS |
| Gut microbiome alterations | Dysbiosis; altered Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio; responds to antibiotics and diet |
| Low-grade inflammation | Increased mast cells and lymphocytes in mucosa |
| Bile acid metabolism | Altered bile acid absorption in IBS-D |

## 3.2 Rome IV Diagnostic Criteria

> **Rome IV Criteria for IBS**: Recurrent abdominal pain on average at least 1 day per week in the last 3 months, associated with 2 or more of:
> 1. Related to defecation
> 2. Associated with a change in stool frequency
> 3. Associated with a change in stool form (appearance)
>
> Criteria must be fulfilled for the last 3 months, with symptom onset at least 6 months before diagnosis.

## 3.3 IBS Subtypes

| Subtype | Definition | Prevalence |
|---------|-----------|------------|
| **IBS-C** (Constipation-predominant) | >25% hard/lumpy stools (Bristol 1-2), <25% loose/watery stools (Bristol 6-7) | ~35% |
| **IBS-D** (Diarrhea-predominant) | >25% loose/watery stools (Bristol 6-7), <25% hard/lumpy stools (Bristol 1-2) | ~35% |
| **IBS-M** (Mixed) | >25% hard/lumpy AND >25% loose/watery stools | ~25% |
| **IBS-U** (Unclassified) | Does not meet criteria for IBS-C, IBS-D, or IBS-M | ~5% |

### 3.3.1 Bristol Stool Form Scale
| Type | Description | Clinical Correlation |
|------|------------|---------------------|
| 1 | Separate hard lumps | Constipation |
| 2 | Sausage-shaped but lumpy | Constipation |
| 3 | Sausage with cracks | Normal |
| 4 | Smooth soft sausage/snake | Normal (ideal) |
| 5 | Soft blobs with clear edges | Normal (tends toward diarrhea) |
| 6 | Fluffy pieces with ragged edges | Diarrhea |
| 7 | Watery, no solid pieces | Diarrhea |

## 3.4 Red Flags (Alarm Features)

The following features are NOT consistent with IBS and require further investigation:

| Red Flag | Significance | Recommended Workup |
|----------|-------------|-------------------|
| Onset after age 50 years | Increased malignancy risk | Colonoscopy |
| Unintentional weight loss | Malignancy, IBD, malabsorption | CT, colonoscopy, enteroscopy |
| Rectal bleeding or melena | Malignancy, IBD, polyp | Colonoscopy urgently |
| Iron-deficiency anemia | Colorectal cancer, celiac disease | CBC, iron studies, colonoscopy, celiac serology |
| Nocturnal symptoms (waking to defecate) | Organic disease more likely | Full colonic evaluation |
| Family history of CRC, IBD, or celiac disease | Increased personal risk | Targeted screening |
| Recent antibiotic use | Consider C. difficile infection | C. difficile toxin PCR |
| Fever | Infection, IBD, malignancy | Full infectious and inflammatory workup |
| Progressive symptoms | Malignancy or evolving organic disease | Comprehensive evaluation |
| Blood in stool | Never normal; always investigate | Colonoscopy |

## 3.5 Differential Diagnosis

| Condition | Key Distinguishing Features | Initial Test |
|-----------|---------------------------|-------------|
| Celiac disease | Positive tissue transglutaminase IgA, villous atrophy on biopsy | tTG-IgA with total IgA |
| Inflammatory bowel disease | Bloody diarrhea, weight loss, elevated inflammatory markers, endoscopic findings | Fecal calprotectin, CRP, colonoscopy |
| Microscopic colitis | Watery diarrhea (especially older women), normal endoscopy, diagnosed by biopsy | Colonoscopy with biopsies |
| Small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO) | Bloating after meals, positive breath test, response to antibiotics | Glucose or lactulose breath test |
| Bile acid diarrhea | Post-cholecystectomy, watery diarrhea, positive SeHCAT test or serum C4 | SeHCAT retention, serum C7/C4 |
| Colorectal cancer | Age >50, weight loss, anemia, rectal bleeding, change in bowel habits | Colonoscopy |
| Thyroid dysfunction | Systemic symptoms, abnormal TSH | TSH, free T4 |
| Lactose intolerance | Symptoms with dairy, positive breath test | Lactose hydrogen breath test |
| Pancreatic exocrine insufficiency | Steatorrhea, nutritional deficiencies, post-prandial pain | Fecal elastase |
| Giardiasis | Travel, camping, persistent diarrhea, bloating | Stool antigen or microscopy |

## 3.6 Diagnostic Workup

For patients meeting Rome IV criteria without alarm features, limited testing is appropriate:

1. **CBC**: Exclude anemia
2. **CRP or fecal calprotectin**: Screen for inflammatory disease
3. **Celiac serology** (tTG-IgA with total IgA): Especially in IBS-D
4. **TSH**: If symptoms suggest thyroid dysfunction
5. **Age-appropriate colorectal cancer screening**: Standard population screening applies

> **Note**: Routine colonoscopy is NOT indicated in young patients with typical IBS symptoms and no alarm features.

## 3.7 Management: Stepwise Approach

### 3.7.1 Step 1: Dietary and Lifestyle Modifications

**Low FODMAP Diet** [^91^]
- The most evidence-based dietary intervention for IBS
- Mechanism: reduces fermentable carbohydrates that are poorly absorbed, drawing water into the lumen and producing gas through bacterial fermentation
- **Structure**:
  1. **Restriction phase** (2-6 weeks): Strict elimination of high-FODMAP foods
  2. **Reintroduction phase** (6-8 weeks): Systematic challenge of FODMAP subgroups to identify triggers
  3. **Personalization phase**: Long-term individualized diet avoiding only identified triggers
- **Response rate**: 50-80% of patients report symptom improvement
- Should be guided by a trained dietitian to ensure nutritional adequacy

| FODMAP Group | High-FODMAP Foods | Low-FODMAP Alternatives |
|-------------|-------------------|------------------------|
| Oligosaccharides (fructans/GOS) | Wheat, rye, onion, garlic, legumes | Rice, oats, quinoa, potatoes, green beans |
| Lactose | Milk, yogurt, soft cheese | Lactose-free milk, hard cheeses, butter |
| Polyols | Apples, pears, stone fruits, cauliflower, mushrooms, sorbitol, mannitol | Berries, citrus, bananas, carrots, cucumber |
| Excess fructose | Honey, mango, watermelon, high-fructose corn syrup | Maple syrup, glucose, oranges, strawberries |

**Other Dietary Interventions**:
- **Soluble fiber (psyllium)**: 3.5 g daily; only fiber with demonstrated benefit in IBS; start low and titrate slowly
- **Regular meal pattern**: Avoid large meals; eat at consistent times
- **Adequate hydration**: 1.5-2 L/day
- **Limit caffeine and alcohol**: Both can trigger symptoms
- **Avoid spicy/fatty foods**: If individually identified as triggers

**Lifestyle Interventions**:
- Regular physical activity (30 minutes moderate exercise, 5 days/week)
- Stress management: mindfulness, meditation, yoga, adequate sleep
- Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT): strong evidence for improving symptoms and quality of life
- Gut-directed hypnotherapy: NICE-recommended; effective for refractory symptoms

### 3.7.2 Step 2: Pharmacotherapy by Subtype

#### IBS-C (Constipation-Predominant)

| Drug | Class | Dose | Notes |
|------|-------|------|-------|
| **Linaclotide** | Guanylate cyclase-C agonist | 290 mcg daily (30 min before first meal) | Increases intestinal fluid secretion and transit; FDA-approved for IBS-C |
| **Plecanatide** | Guanylate cyclase-C agonist | 3 mg daily | Similar mechanism to linaclotide; may have lower diarrhea rates |
| **Lubiprostone** | Chloride channel activator | 8 mcg BID with food | Well-established; nausea is common side effect |
| **Prucalopride** | 5-HT4 agonist | 2 mg daily | Prokinetic; also effective for chronic constipation |
| **PEG 3350** | Osmotic laxative | 17 g daily | Over-the-counter; good safety profile |

#### IBS-D (Diarrhea-Predominant)

| Drug | Class | Dose | Notes |
|------|-------|------|-------|
| **Loperamide** | Opioid agonist (peripheral) | 2-4 mg PRN, max 16 mg/day | First-line symptom control; take 30 min before meals/travel |
| **Rifaximin** | Non-absorbable antibiotic | 550 mg TID x 14 days | FDA-approved for IBS-D; targets gut dysbiosis; can repeat |
| **Eluxadoline** | Mixed opioid agonist/antagonist | 75-100 mg BID | Take with food; **contraindicated in patients without gallbladder** (pancreatitis risk) |
| **Alosetron** | 5-HT3 antagonist | 0.5-1 mg BID | Restricted access due to ischemic colitis risk; only for severe IBS-D in women |
| **Bile acid sequestrants** | Cholestyramine 4 g BID | 4 g BID, titrate to response | For bile acid diarrhea; may cause constipation |

#### All IBS Subtypes (Pain/Bloating Predominant)

| Drug | Class | Dose | Notes |
|------|-------|------|-------|
| **Peppermint oil** | Smooth muscle relaxant | 180-225 mg TID enteric-coated | Effective for pain and bloating; heartburn possible |
| **Antispasmodics** | Hyoscyamine, dicyclomine, mebeverine | Various | Use before meals for postprandial symptoms; anticholinergic side effects |
| **Amitriptyline** | TCA (low-dose) | 10-50 mg QHS | Start 10 mg, titrate weekly; effective for pain; may cause constipation |
| **Nortriptyline** | TCA (low-dose) | 10-50 mg QHS | Alternative if amitriptyline side effects intolerable |
| **SSRIs** (fluoxetine, sertraline) | Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors | Standard antidepressant doses | For pain with comorbid anxiety/depression |

### 3.7.3 Step 3: Referral
- Gastroenterology referral for: refractory symptoms despite optimized therapy, diagnostic uncertainty, need for specialized testing
- Consider: SIBO breath testing, anorectal manometry (if defecatory disorder suspected), specialized dietary counseling, pain management for central sensitization

## 3.8 Special Populations

| Population | Considerations |
|-----------|---------------|
| **Pregnancy** | Dietary management first-line; avoid most IBS-specific medications; loperamide may be used short-term; avoid eluxadoline; peppermint oil may be safe |
| **Pediatrics** | Rome IV criteria apply with age-appropriate modifications; emphasize dietary and psychological approaches; avoid eluxadoline |
| **Elderly** | Higher medication side effect risk; avoid anticholinergics; start all medications at lowest dose; drug interactions common |

## 3.9 Follow-up and Monitoring
- Reassess at 4-6 weeks after initiating treatment
- Document response using validated tools (IBS-SSS, IBS-QOL)
- Adjust therapy based on predominant symptoms
- Re-evaluate diagnosis if no response to multiple targeted therapies

# Chapter 4: Inflammatory Bowel Disease {#chapter4}

## 4.1 Overview

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) encompasses two chronic, immune-mediated inflammatory conditions of the gastrointestinal tract: Crohn's disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC). Both are characterized by relapsing and remitting courses with potential for significant complications. The incidence of IBD is increasing globally, particularly in newly industrialized countries. IBD results from complex interactions between genetic susceptibility, environmental triggers, intestinal microbiome alterations, and dysregulated mucosal immune responses.

### 4.1.1 Epidemiology
- Global prevalence: UC 5-500 per 100,000; CD 3-300 per 100,000 (varies by region)
- Highest prevalence in North America and Europe
- Peak age of onset: 15-35 years (CD and UC); second smaller peak at 50-70 years
- Family history increases risk 8-10 fold
- Smoking increases CD risk but is protective against UC (though not recommended due to overall harm)

### 4.1.2 Crohn's Disease vs. Ulcerative Colitis: Key Differences

| Feature | Crohn's Disease | Ulcerative Colitis |
|---------|-----------------|-------------------|
| **Anatomic distribution** | Anywhere from mouth to anus; skip lesions (discontinuous) | Colon only; continuous from rectum proximally |
| **Depth of inflammation** | Transmural (full-thickness bowel wall) | Mucosal and submucosal only |
| **Rectal involvement** | Rectal sparing common | Always involves rectum (proctitis) |
| **Granulomas** | Non-caseating granulomas in ~50% | Absent |
| **Fistulas and strictures** | Common (penetrating and stricturing behavior) | Rare (except in chronic disease) |
| **Perianal disease** | Common (fissures, fistulas, abscesses) | Rare |
| **Toxic megacolon** | Less common | More common (severe colitis) |
| **Colorectal cancer risk** | Increased (primarily with colonic involvement) | Significantly increased (duration-dependent) |
| **Recurrence after surgery** | Very high (>70% endoscopic recurrence at 1 year) | Low if total proctocolectomy |
| **Smoking effect** | Worsens disease; cessation beneficial | Paradoxically protective (but still not recommended) |

### 4.1.3 Classification Systems

**Montreal Classification for Crohn's Disease**:
| Category | Code | Description |
|----------|------|-------------|
| Age at diagnosis | A1 | <17 years |
| | A2 | 17-40 years |
| | A3 | >40 years |
| Location | L1 | Ileal |
| | L2 | Colonic |
| | L3 | Ileocolonic |
| | L4 | Isolated upper GI (modifier) |
| Behavior | B1 | Non-stricturing, non-penetrating (inflammatory) |
| | B2 | Stricturing |
| | B3 | Penetrating/fistulizing |
| | p | Perianal disease modifier |

**Montreal Classification for Ulcerative Colitis**:
| Extent | Code | Description |
|--------|------|-------------|
| | E1 | Ulcerative proctitis (limited to rectum) |
| | E2 | Left-sided colitis (distal to splenic flexure) |
| | E3 | Extensive/pancolitis (proximal to splenic flexure) |

## 4.2 Clinical Presentation

### 4.2.1 Crohn's Disease
- **Gastrointestinal**: Abdominal pain (often RLQ), diarrhea (may be non-bloody), weight loss, fatigue, palpable abdominal mass
- **Perianal**: Tags, fissures, fistulas, abscesses (may precede intestinal diagnosis)
- **Extraintestinal manifestations**: Arthritis, uveitis, erythema nodosum, pyoderma gangrenosum, primary sclerosing cholangitis, osteoporosis
- **Complications**: Intestinal obstruction (strictures), fistulas (enteroenteric, perianal, enterovesical, enterocutaneous), abscesses, malnutrition

### 4.2.2 Ulcerative Colitis
- **Gastrointestinal**: Bloody diarrhea (hallmark), urgency, tenesmus, abdominal cramping (usually LLQ), fecal incontinence
- **Severity grading**: 
  - Mild: <4 stools/day, minimal blood, normal inflammatory markers
  - Moderate: 4-6 stools/day, blood, mild systemic symptoms
  - Severe: >6 bloody stools/day, fever, tachycardia, anemia, elevated ESR/CRP
- **Extraintestinal manifestations**: Similar to CD; primary sclerosing cholangitis more strongly associated with UC
- **Complications**: Toxic megacolon, severe bleeding, perforation, colorectal cancer

## 4.3 Red Flags (Emergency Features)

| Feature | Significance | Immediate Action |
|---------|-------------|-----------------|
| Severe abdominal pain with bloody diarrhea >6 stools/day | Severe colitis, toxic megacolon | Hospital admission; IV steroids; surgical consultation |
| Fever >38.5 C with tachycardia >100 bpm | Severe active disease, possible infection | Hospital admission; infectious workup |
| Toxic megacolon (colonic dilation >6 cm with systemic toxicity) | Life-threatening; risk of perforation | NPO, IV fluids, broad-spectrum antibiotics, urgent surgery evaluation |
| Severe dehydration with orthostatic hypotension | Hemodynamic compromise | IV resuscitation; hospital admission |
| Bowel obstruction symptoms (vomiting, obstipation, distension) | Stricture, abscess, or adhesions | Imaging; surgical consultation |
| Perianal abscess/fistula with systemic signs | Sepsis risk | Drainage; antibiotics |
| Massive GI bleeding | Hemodynamic compromise | Resuscitation; emergency surgery/endoscopy |
| Free air on imaging | Perforation | Emergency laparotomy |
| Deep vein thrombosis/pulmonary embolism | IBD is prothrombotic | Anticoagulation; treat underlying flare |

## 4.4 Diagnosis

### 4.4.1 Laboratory Studies
| Test | Purpose | Expected Findings |
|------|---------|-----------------|
| CBC | Anemia, leukocytosis | Iron deficiency anemia, leukocytosis in active disease |
| CRP, ESR | Inflammatory markers | Elevated (correlate with disease activity) |
| Fecal calprotectin | Intestinal inflammation | Elevated (>250 mcg/g suggests active inflammation) |
| CMP | Nutritional status, electrolytes | Hypoalbuminemia, electrolyte disturbances |
| Iron studies, B12, folate, vitamin D | Nutritional deficiencies | Common in CD; screen at diagnosis |
| p-ANCA, ASCA | Serologic markers | p-ANCA positive in UC; ASCA positive in CD (low sensitivity) |

### 4.4.2 Endoscopic and Imaging Evaluation
- **Colonoscopy with ileoscopy** (UC and colonic CD): Assesses extent, severity, obtains biopsies; contraindicated in toxic megacolon or severe colitis
- **Upper endoscopy** (CD): If upper GI symptoms present
- **Capsule endoscopy** (CD): Evaluates small bowel mucosa; requires patency capsule first if strictures suspected
- **CT/MR enterography** (CD): Assesses small bowel disease, strictures, fistulas, abscesses; preferred for initial evaluation and monitoring
- **MRI pelvis**: Excellent for perianal disease assessment

### 4.4.3 Histopathology
| Feature | Crohn's Disease | Ulcerative Colitis |
|---------|-----------------|-------------------|
| Distribution | Patchy, skip lesions | Continuous |
| Depth | Transmural inflammation | Mucosal/submucosal |
| Granulomas | Non-caseating (40-60%) | Absent |
| Crypt architecture | Focal cryptitis, branching | Diffuse cryptitis, crypt abscesses |
| Goblet cells | Preserved | Depleted in active disease |

## 4.5 Differential Diagnosis

| Condition | Key Distinguishing Features |
|-----------|---------------------------|
| Infectious colitis | Acute onset, positive stool culture, resolves with antimicrobial therapy |
| C. difficile infection | Recent antibiotic exposure, positive C. difficile PCR/toxin, pseudomembranes on endoscopy |
| Ischemic colitis | Acute onset in elderly/vascular disease, segmental colitis at watershed areas (splenic flexure) |
| Microscopic colitis | Watery diarrhea, normal endoscopy, biopsy diagnosis (lymphocytic or collagenous) |
| Celiac disease | Positive celiac serology (tTG-IgA), villous atrophy on duodenal biopsy |
| Colorectal cancer | Focal mass lesion, older age, weight loss, anemia; biopsy confirms |
| Behcet's disease | Oral/genital ulcers, pathergy, systemic vasculitis, rare GI involvement |
| NSAID enteropathy | History of NSAID use, diaphragm-like strictures, web-like strictures |
| Radiation enteritis/colitis | History of pelvic/abdominal radiation |
| Small bowel bacterial overgrowth | Bloating, diarrhea, positive breath test, response to antibiotics |

## 4.6 Management

### 4.6.1 Treatment Targets (Treat-to-Target Approach)
Modern IBD management follows a "treat-to-target" paradigm with the following goals:
- **Clinical remission**: Resolution of symptoms
- **Biochemical remission**: Normal CRP and fecal calprotectin
- **Endoscopic remission** (mucosal healing): Normal or near-normal mucosa on endoscopy (the most important long-term target)
- **Transmural remission** (CD): No active inflammation on cross-sectional imaging

### 4.6.2 Mild Ulcerative Colitis
| Regimen | Dosing | Notes |
|---------|--------|-------|
| Mesalamine suppositories | 1 g daily | First-line for proctitis (E1) |
| Mesalamine enemas | 1-4 g daily | For left-sided disease (E2) |
| Oral mesalamine | 2-4.8 g daily | For left-sided or extensive UC |
| Budesonide foam | 2 mg BID | Topical corticosteroid if 5-ASA intolerant |

### 4.6.3 Moderate-Severe Ulcerative Colitis

**Induction Therapy**:

Systemic corticosteroids (prednisone 40-60 mg daily) for induction, followed by steroid-sparing advanced therapy:

| Drug Class | Agent | Dosing | Efficacy |
|-----------|-------|--------|----------|
| **Anti-TNF** | Infliximab | IV: 5 mg/kg at weeks 0, 2, 6, then q8wk | Higher (especially with immunomodulator) |
| | Adalimumab | SC: 160 mg wk 0, 80 mg wk 2, then 40 mg q2wk | Intermediate |
| | Golimumab | SC: 200 mg wk 0, 100 mg wk 2, then 100 mg q4wk | Intermediate |
| **Anti-integrin** | Vedolizumab | IV: 300 mg at weeks 0, 2, 6, then q8wk | Higher; gut-selective (lower infection risk) |
| **Anti-IL-12/23** | Ustekinumab | IV induction, then 90 mg SC q8wk | Higher |
| **Anti-IL-23** | Risankizumab | IV induction, then SC maintenance | Higher |
| | Guselkumab | IV induction, then SC maintenance | Higher; now approved for UC |
| | Mirikizumab | SC | Intermediate |
| **JAK inhibitors** | Tofacitinib | 10 mg BID induction, 5 mg BID maintenance | Intermediate; oral |
| | Upadacitinib | 45 mg induction, 15-30 mg maintenance | Higher; oral |
| | Filgotinib | 200 mg daily | Intermediate; oral |
| **S1P modulators** | Ozanimod | 0.92 mg daily (after titration) | Higher; oral |
| | Etrasimod | 2 mg daily | Higher; oral |

**Key AGA 2024 Guideline Principles** [^87^] [^90^] [^92^]:
- Advanced therapies recommended over conventional step-up therapy after 5-ASA failure in moderate-severe UC
- Combination therapy (anti-TNF + immunomodulator) superior to anti-TNF monotherapy
- Thiopurine monotherapy NOT recommended for induction
- Consider stopping 5-ASA when escalating to advanced therapies (cost-saving without efficacy loss)
- Higher-efficacy agents may be preferred first-line for patients with poor prognostic factors

### 4.6.4 Crohn's Disease Management [^93^]

| Disease Severity/Location | Treatment |
|--------------------------|-----------|
| Mild ileocecal CD | Budesonide 9 mg daily (controlled ileal release) |
| Moderate-severe CD | Anti-TNF (infliximab, adalimumab, certolizumab) + immunomodulator |
| Fistulizing CD | Infliximab (best evidence); may add antibiotics (metronidazole, ciprofloxacin) |
| Postoperative prophylaxis | Anti-TNF in high-risk patients (second resection, penetrating disease, extensive disease) |
| Post-TNF failure | Ustekinumab, vedolizumab, risankizumab, upadacitinib |

**ACG 2024 Guideline Key Points** [^93^]:
- Oral mesalamine NOT recommended for CD (not superior to placebo)
- Against requiring conventional therapy failure before initiating advanced therapy
- Combination infliximab + immunomodulator superior to monotherapy
- Biosimilar infliximab, adalimumab, and ustekinumab are effective and interchangeable
- Mirikizumab and guselkumab now recommended for CD
- Upadacitinib recommended for anti-TNF-exposed patients

### 4.6.5 Immunomodulators

| Drug | Dosing | Monitoring | Notes |
|------|--------|------------|-------|
| Azathioprine | 2-2.5 mg/kg/day | TPMT/NUDT15 testing before initiation; CBC, LFTs q3mo | Oncologic and infection risk; avoid in elderly |
| Mercaptopurine (6-MP) | 1-1.5 mg/kg/day | Same as above | Alternative if azathioprine intolerance |
| Methotrexate | 25 mg SC weekly + folic acid 1 mg daily | CBC, LFTs, creatinine; chest X-ray | Contraindicated in pregnancy |

### 4.6.6 Corticosteroids
- Prednisone 40-60 mg daily for moderate-severe disease induction
- NOT for maintenance therapy
- Budesonide 9 mg for mild-moderate ileocecal CD (fewer systemic side effects)
- Taper over 4-8 weeks; advance to steroid-sparing therapy early

## 4.7 Monitoring and Surveillance

| Parameter | Frequency | Purpose |
|-----------|-----------|---------|
| Fecal calprotectin | Every 3-6 months | Non-invasive marker of intestinal inflammation; predicts relapse |
| CRP, ESR | Every 3-6 months | Systemic inflammation monitoring |
| CBC, CMP | Every 3-6 months | Anemia, nutritional status, medication safety |
| Drug levels and anti-drug antibodies | At week 14 and with loss of response | Therapeutic drug monitoring for biologic optimization |
| Endoscopy | 6-12 months after starting advanced therapy | Assess mucosal healing (treat-to-target) |
| Cross-sectional imaging (CT/MR enterography) | Annually or with symptoms (CD) | Assess transmural healing, strictures, fistulas |
| Colorectal cancer surveillance | Every 1-2 years after 8-10 years of colonic disease | Chromoendoscopy with targeted biopsies preferred |
| Bone density | At steroid initiation, then every 1-2 years | Steroid-induced osteoporosis screening |

## 4.8 Complications

| Complication | Manifestation | Management |
|-------------|--------------|------------|
| Colorectal cancer | Dysplasia on surveillance | Endoscopic resection if localized; proctocolectomy if extensive |
| Primary sclerosing cholangitis | Elevated ALP, MRCP findings | Ursodiol; close CRC surveillance; liver transplant for end-stage |
| Osteoporosis | DEXA T-score <-2.5 | Calcium, vitamin D, bisphosphonates; minimize steroids |
| Iron deficiency anemia | Low ferritin, low MCV | IV iron supplementation (oral poorly tolerated) |
| Malnutrition | Weight loss, vitamin deficiencies | Nutritional support; enteral nutrition if needed |
| Intestinal obstruction (CD) | Stricture | Endoscopic balloon dilatation or surgical resection |
| Fistulas (CD) | Enteroenteric, perianal, enterovesical | Anti-TNF (infliximab), antibiotics, surgical drainage |
| Abscess (CD) | Fever, localized tenderness | Percutaneous or surgical drainage + antibiotics |
| Thromboembolism | DVT, PE | Anticoagulation; higher risk during flares |

## 4.9 Special Populations

| Population | Considerations |
|-----------|---------------|
| **Pregnancy** | Most biologics (anti-TNF, vedolizumab, ustekinumab) considered safe; active disease poses greater risk than medications; discontinue methotrexate and tofacitinib; plan delivery timing to optimize drug levels; vaginal delivery preferred unless active perianal disease |
| **Pediatrics** | Similar treatment principles; growth monitoring essential; earlier use of biologics in severe disease; exclusive enteral nutrition as first-line induction for CD |
| **Elderly** | Higher infection risk with immunosuppression; avoid thiopurines; lower threshold for PJP prophylaxis; consider drug-drug interactions |
| **Perioperative** | Continue most biologics through surgery; coordinate timing; hold methotrexate 1 week before and after |

## 4.10 Pre-Treatment Evaluation Before Immunosuppression

| Assessment | Purpose |
|-----------|---------|
| Tuberculosis screening (IGRA or PPD, chest X-ray) | Reactivation risk with anti-TNF and JAK inhibitors |
| Hepatitis B screening (HBsAg, anti-HBc) | Risk of HBV reactivation |
| Hepatitis C screening | Treat before immunosuppression |
| HIV screening | Immunosuppression contraindicated if untreated |
| Vaccination status | Update before immunosuppression (influenza, pneumococcal, hepatitis B, shingles, COVID-19) |
| Varicella serology (if no history) | Vaccinate if non-immune before starting |
| Pregnancy test (women of childbearing age) | Required before teratogenic medications |

## 4.11 Prevention and Lifestyle

- **Smoking cessation**: Essential for CD; cessation improves prognosis, reduces flares and complications
- **Nutrition**: Balanced diet; no specific diet universally recommended; Mediterranean-style diet may be beneficial
- **Exercise**: Regular physical activity may reduce inflammation and improve quality of life
- **Stress management**: Psychologic support, CBT, mindfulness
- **CRC surveillance**: Essential for all patients with colonic disease >8-10 years duration
- **Vaccination**: Complete all indicated vaccines before initiating immunosuppression
- **Thromboprophylaxis**: During hospitalizations for flares

# Chapter 5: Acute and Chronic Diarrhea {#chapter5}

## 5.1 Overview

Diarrhea is defined as the passage of loose or watery stools, typically occurring more than three times per day. It represents one of the most common gastrointestinal complaints across all age groups and clinical settings. Classification by duration guides the diagnostic approach:

| Classification | Duration | Typical Etiologies |
|---------------|----------|-------------------|
| **Acute** | <14 days | Infectious (viral, bacterial, protozoal), food poisoning, medication-related |
| **Persistent** | 14-30 days | Persistent infection (Giardia, C. difficile), post-infectious malabsorption |
| **Chronic** | >4 weeks | Functional (IBS-D), inflammatory (IBD), malabsorptive (celiac, pancreatic), secretory, drug-induced |

### 5.1.1 Pathophysiologic Mechanisms

| Mechanism | Description | Examples |
|-----------|-------------|----------|
| Osmotic | Non-absorbable solutes draw water into lumen; decreases with fasting | Lactose intolerance, laxative abuse, magnesium-containing antacids |
| Secretory | Active intestinal secretion exceeds absorption; persists with fasting | Cholera, VIPoma, bile acid diarrhea, some bacterial toxins |
| Inflammatory | Mucosal damage with exudation of blood, pus, and protein | IBD, invasive bacterial infections, C. difficile |
| Malabsorptive | Impaired nutrient absorption with osmotic component | Celiac disease, chronic pancreatitis, small bowel bacterial overgrowth |
| Motility | Accelerated or disordered transit | Hyperthyroidism, IBS-D, post-surgical |

## 5.2 Acute Infectious Diarrhea

### 5.2.1 Epidemiology
- Most common cause of acute diarrhea globally
- Viral etiologies (norovirus, rotavirus) account for 50-70% of cases in developed countries
- Bacterial etiologies more common with travel, foodborne outbreaks, immunocompromise
- Responsible for significant global mortality, particularly in children <5 years in developing countries

### 5.2.2 Clinical Assessment

| Feature | Viral | Bacterial | Protozoal |
|---------|-------|-----------|-----------|
| Onset | Gradual (12-48 hours) | Often abrupt (6-24 hours for toxins; longer for invasive) | Subacute to chronic (>2 weeks) |
| Duration | 1-3 days | 1-7 days | Weeks to months |
| Stool character | Watery | Watery or bloody/mucoid | Watery, greasy, foul-smelling |
| Fever | Low-grade | High-grade with invasive | Low-grade or absent |
| Vomiting | Prominent | Variable | Uncommon |
| Tenesmus | Absent | Common with invasive | Absent |

### 5.2.3 When to Obtain Stool Studies [^98^]
- Dysentery (bloody diarrhea with fever)
- Severe illness (dehydration, hospitalization required)
- Duration >7 days without improvement
- Immunocompromised host
- Recent antibiotic use (test for C. difficile)
| Recent travel (especially to endemic areas)
- Food handler, healthcare worker, or institutionalized patient
- Age >65 years with comorbidities
- Community outbreak or public health concern

### 5.2.4 Management of Acute Infectious Diarrhea

**General Measures (All Patients)**:
- **Hydration**: Oral rehydration solution (ORS) is the cornerstone; WHO formula or commercial preparations (Pedialyte); goal is urine output >0.5 mL/kg/hr
- **Diet**: Early refeeding as tolerated; BRAT diet is unnecessary; avoid high-fat and high-simple-sugar foods
- **Avoid**: Antimotility agents if bloody diarrhea, high fever, or suspected C. difficile

**Symptomatic Treatment**:
| Agent | Dosing | Indications | Contraindications |
|-------|--------|-------------|-------------------|
| Loperamide | 4 mg, then 2 mg after each loose stool (max 16 mg/day) | Non-bloody, afebrile diarrhea | Bloody diarrhea, fever, suspected C. difficile, dysentery |
| Bismuth subsalicylate | 524 mg (2 tablets) every 30-60 min (max 8 doses/day) | Mild-moderate diarrhea | Pregnancy, children with viral illness, aspirin allergy, anticoagulation |
| Racecadotril | 100 mg TID (adults) | Enkephalinase inhibitor; reduces secretion | Not available in all countries |

**Antibiotic Therapy**:
Most acute diarrhea is self-limited and does not require antibiotics. Antibiotics are indicated for:
- Severe traveler's diarrhea
- Dysentery (bloody diarrhea with fever)
- Suspected cholera
- Immunocompromised patients
- Sepsis or severe systemic illness
- Certain high-risk pathogens (Giardia, E. histolytica, Shigella in specific settings)

| Pathogen/Scenario | First-Line Treatment | Alternative |
|-------------------|---------------------|-------------|
| Traveler's diarrhea (moderate) | Azithromycin 500 mg daily x 3 days OR 1000 mg single dose | Rifaximin 200 mg TID x 3 days (non-invasive) |
| Traveler's diarrhea (severe/dysentery) | Azithromycin 500 mg daily x 3 days | Fluoroquinolone (resistance increasing) |
| Campylobacter (severe) | Azithromycin 500 mg daily x 3 days | Clarithromycin 250-500 mg BID x 5-7 days |
| Shigella (severe) | Ciprofloxacin 500 mg BID x 3 days OR azithromycin | Ceftriaxone (if fluoroquinolone-resistant) |
| Giardia lamblia | Metronidazole 400 mg TID x 7-10 days OR tinidazole 2 g single dose | Nitazoxanide 500 mg BID x 3 days |
| Entamoeba histolytica | Metronidazole 800 mg q8h x 5 days + luminal agent (paromomycin or iodoquinol) | Tinidazole 2 g daily x 3 days + luminal agent |
| Cryptosporidium | Nitazoxanide 500 mg BID x 3 days | Primarily in immunocompromised; HAART in HIV |

## 5.3 Clostridioides difficile Infection (CDI) [^95^] [^102^]

### 5.3.1 Risk Factors
- Antibiotic exposure within preceding 8 weeks (any antibiotic; clindamycin, fluoroquinolones, cephalosporins highest risk)
- Age >65 years
- Hospitalization or long-term care facility residence
- Immunosuppression
- PPI use (modest association)
- Prior CDI (highest risk factor for recurrence)

### 5.3.2 Clinical Classification
| Classification | Criteria |
|---------------|----------|
| **Non-severe** | WBC <15,000 and serum creatinine <1.5 mg/dL |
| **Severe** | WBC >15,000 or serum creatinine >1.5 mg/dL |
| **Severe-complicated/fulminant** | Hypotension, shock, ileus, toxic megacolon |

### 5.3.3 Treatment [^95^] [^102^]

| Episode | First-Line | Alternative |
|---------|-----------|-------------|
| **Initial episode (non-severe or severe)** | Fidaxomicin 200 mg BID x 10 days (preferred) | Vancomycin 125 mg QID x 10 days; Metronidazole 500 mg TID only if above unavailable |
| **First recurrence** | Fidaxomicin 200 mg BID x 10 days OR extended-pulsed fidaxomicin | Vancomycin tapered/pulsed regimen |
| **Second or subsequent recurrence** | Fidaxomicin OR vancomycin taper/pulse + fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) | FMT is most effective for multiple recurrences |
| **Severe-complicated/fulminant** | Vancomycin 500 mg Q6H PO/NG + IV metronidazole 500 mg TID; add vancomycin enemas if ileus | Emergency colectomy if refractory |

> **Key IDSA/SHEA Guideline Updates**: Fidaxomicin is now preferred first-line over vancomycin due to lower recurrence rates. Metronidazole should only be used if fidaxomicin and vancomycin are unavailable. Bezlotoxumab (anti-toxin B monoclonal antibody) can be used as an adjunct to prevent recurrence in high-risk patients. [^99^]

## 5.4 Traveler's Diarrhea [^120^]

### 5.4.1 Definition and Epidemiology
- Most common travel-related illness; affects 30-70% of travelers to high-risk destinations
- High-risk regions: South Asia, sub-Saharan Africa, Central America
- Moderate-risk: Southeast Asia, Middle East, Eastern Europe, Caribbean
- Peak incidence in first 2 weeks of travel

### 5.4.2 Prevention
- "Boil it, cook it, peel it, or forget it"
- Avoid tap water, ice, raw foods, unpasteurized dairy, street vendor food
- Bismuth subsalicylate (2 tablets QID) prophylaxis: ~65% protection (for short trips)
- Routine antibiotic prophylaxis NOT recommended (adverse effects > benefit)

### 5.4.3 Treatment Approach
| Severity | Clinical Features | Treatment |
|----------|------------------|-----------|
| **Mild** | Tolerable, no interference with activities | No antibiotics; loperamide or bismuth; ORS |
| **Moderate** | Distressing, interferes with planned activities | Azithromycin 500 mg daily x 3 days; add loperamide |
| **Severe/Dysentery** | Incapacitating, bloody diarrhea, fever, severe pain | Azithromycin 500 mg daily x 3 days (or 1000 mg single dose); add loperamide if no bloody diarrhea |

## 5.5 Chronic Diarrhea Workup

### 5.5.1 Systematic Diagnostic Approach
For diarrhea persisting >4 weeks, a structured evaluation is essential:

**Step 1: History and Physical Examination**
- Medication review (metformin, PPIs, NSAIDs, laxatives, antibiotics, SSRIs, ACE inhibitors, orlistat)
- Dietary history (sorbitol, mannitol, lactose, caffeine, alcohol)
- Surgical history (cholecystectomy - consider bile acid diarrhea; bowel resections)
- Family history (IBD, celiac, thyroid disease)
- Weight loss assessment (suggests organic/malignant etiology)

**Step 2: Initial Laboratory Testing**
| Test | Purpose |
|------|---------|
| CBC | Anemia (iron deficiency, B12/folate), leukocytosis |
| CMP | Electrolytes, renal function, albumin, liver enzymes |
| CRP or ESR | Inflammatory marker elevation |
| TSH | Hyperthyroidism screening |
| Celiac serology | tTG-IgA with total IgA |
| Fecal calprotectin or lactoferrin | Differentiate inflammatory from non-inflammatory |

**Step 3: Stool Studies**
| Test | Indication |
|------|-----------|
| C. difficile PCR/toxin | Recent antibiotics, healthcare exposure |
| Stool culture | Systemic illness, immunocompromise |
| Ova and parasites x 3 | Travel, immunocompromise, persistent diarrhea |
| Fecal fat or fecal elastase | Steatorrhea (malabsorption, pancreatic insufficiency) |

**Step 4: Directed Advanced Testing**
| Test | Indication |
|------|-----------|
| Colonoscopy with biopsies | Red flags, elevated inflammatory markers, age >50 |
| Upper endoscopy with duodenal biopsies | Positive celiac serology, malabsorption |
| Breath testing (glucose/lactulose hydrogen) | Suspected SIBO or lactose intolerance |
| SeHCAT scan or serum C4 | Post-cholecystectomy diarrhea, suspected bile acid diarrhea |
| CT abdomen/pelvis | Suspicion of malignancy, IBD, or structural cause |
| Capsule endoscopy | Suspected small bowel disease |

## 5.6 Differential Diagnosis: Chronic Diarrhea

| Condition | Key Features | Key Diagnostic Test |
|-----------|-------------|---------------------|
| IBS-D | Rome IV criteria, no alarm features, normal inflammatory markers | Clinical diagnosis |
| IBD | Bloody diarrhea, weight loss, elevated CRP/calprotectin | Colonoscopy with biopsies |
| Celiac disease | Bloating, iron deficiency, dermatitis herpetiformis | tTG-IgA with total IgA; duodenal biopsy |
| Microscopic colitis | Watery diarrhea, older women, normal endoscopy | Colonic biopsies |
| SIBO | Bloating, gas, response to antibiotics | Glucose/lactulose breath test |
| Bile acid diarrhea | Post-cholecystectomy, watery diarrhea | SeHCAT retention <10% |
| Chronic pancreatitis | Steatorrhea, epigastric pain, alcohol history | Fecal elastase <200 mcg/g; CT/MRCP findings |
| Hyperthyroidism | Weight loss, heat intolerance, tremor | TSH, free T4 |
| Lactose intolerance | Symptoms with dairy, bloating, gas | Lactose hydrogen breath test |
| Medication-induced | Temporal relationship to drug initiation | Resolution with drug discontinuation |
| Colorectal cancer | Age >50, weight loss, anemia, change in bowel habits | Colonoscopy |
| Neuroendocrine tumor | Flushing, wheezing, elevated 5-HIAA | Urine 5-HIAA, CT/MRI, somatostatin receptor imaging |

## 5.7 Special Populations

| Population | Key Considerations |
|-----------|-------------------|
| **Pregnancy** | Avoid bismuth subsalicylate in 3rd trimester; avoid fluoroquinolones; azithromycin preferred for traveler's diarrhea; oral rehydration is primary therapy |
| **Pediatrics** | Higher dehydration risk; ORS essential; avoid antimotility agents in young children; avoid bismuth in viral illness (Reye's syndrome risk); rotavirus vaccine prevents severe disease |
| **Elderly** | Higher C. difficile risk; increased dehydration risk; lower threshold for hospitalization; medication review essential |
| **Immunocompromised** | Broader differential including opportunistic infections (CMV, MAC, Cryptosporidium, Isospora, Microsporidia); lower threshold for diagnostic testing and treatment |
| **HIV/AIDS** | CD4 <200: consider MAC, Cryptosporidium, Microsporidia, CMV; CD4 <50: consider CMV colitis, disseminated MAC |

## 5.8 Prevention

- Hand hygiene (soap and water for C. difficile; alcohol-based for other pathogens)
- Safe food and water practices when traveling
- Vaccination: rotavirus (infants), cholera (high-risk travel)
- Judicious antibiotic use
- Avoid unnecessary PPI use (C. difficile risk factor)
- Probiotics: modest evidence for reducing antibiotic-associated diarrhea (Saccharomyces boulardii, Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG)

# Chapter 6: Constipation {#chapter6}

## 6.1 Overview

Constipation is a highly prevalent symptom complex defined by infrequent bowel movements (typically fewer than three per week), hard or lumpy stools, excessive straining, sensation of incomplete evacuation, or anorectal obstruction. It affects approximately 14-16% of the global adult population, with higher prevalence in women, elderly individuals, and those with sedentary lifestyles. Constipation is classified as primary (functional/idiopathic) when no underlying cause is identified, or secondary when attributed to medications, metabolic disorders, neurologic disease, or structural abnormalities.

### 6.1.1 Definition (Rome IV Criteria)
> Must include 2 or more of the following for the last 3 months (symptom onset >6 months prior):
> - Straining during >25% of defecations
> - Lumpy or hard stools (Bristol Types 1-2) in >25% of defecations
> - Sensation of incomplete evacuation in >25% of defecations
> - Sensation of anorectal obstruction/blockage in >25% of defecations
> - Manual maneuvers to facilitate defecation in >25% of defecations
> - Fewer than three spontaneous bowel movements per week

### 6.1.2 Classification

| Category | Description |
|----------|-------------|
| **Normal transit constipation** | Slow colonic movement; stool frequency may be normal but patients perceive constipation |
| **Slow transit constipation** | Markedly reduced colonic motility; infrequent bowel movements; often no urge to defecate |
| **Defecatory disorders (pelvic floor dysfunction)** | Impaired rectal evacuation due to paradoxical anal contraction or inadequate rectal propulsion; excessive straining, digital assistance |

### 6.1.3 Common Secondary Causes

| Category | Common Causes |
|----------|--------------|
| **Medications** | Opioids, anticholinergics, tricyclic antidepressants, SSRIs, calcium channel blockers (verapamil), iron supplements, aluminum/calcium-containing antacids, diuretics |
| **Metabolic/endocrine** | Hypothyroidism, hypercalcemia, hypokalemia, diabetes mellitus, pregnancy, uremia |
| **Neurologic** | Parkinson's disease, multiple sclerosis, spinal cord injury, autonomic neuropathy, stroke |
| **Structural** | Colorectal cancer, strictures, rectocele, sigmoidocele, megacolon |
| **Psychologic** | Depression, anxiety, eating disorders, somatization |
| **Lifestyle** | Low fiber intake, inadequate hydration, sedentary lifestyle, ignoring urge to defecate |

## 6.2 Red Flags

| Feature | Significance | Recommended Action |
|---------|-------------|-------------------|
| New-onset constipation age >50 years | Colorectal cancer risk | Colonoscopy |
| Unintentional weight loss | Malignancy, systemic disease | Comprehensive evaluation |
| Rectal bleeding or melena | Malignancy, IBD, diverticular disease | Urgent colonoscopy |
| Iron-deficiency anemia | Chronic blood loss; malignancy | Colonoscopy + EGD |
| Family history of colorectal cancer or IBD | Increased personal risk | Earlier screening colonoscopy |
| Sudden change in bowel habits | Malignancy until excluded | Colonoscopy |
| Severe abdominal pain or distension | Obstruction, volvulus, perforation | Urgent imaging; surgical consultation |
| Fecal vomiting | Complete bowel obstruction | Emergency evaluation |

## 6.3 Differential Diagnosis

| Condition | Key Distinguishing Features | Diagnostic Test |
|-----------|---------------------------|-----------------|
| IBS-C | Abdominal pain, Rome IV criteria, bloating predominating | Clinical diagnosis |
| Slow transit constipation | Marked infrequency, no urge to defecate, delayed colonic transit | Colonic transit study (Sitz markers or wireless motility capsule) |
| Pelvic floor dysfunction | Excessive straining, digital evacuation, paradoxical anal contraction | Anorectal manometry, balloon expulsion test, defecography |
| Hypothyroidism | Fatigue, weight gain, cold intolerance, elevated TSH | TSH, free T4 |
| Hypercalcemia | Polyuria, polydipsia, confusion, elevated calcium | Serum calcium, PTH |
| Colorectal cancer | Change in bowel habits, weight loss, anemia, rectal bleeding | Colonoscopy |
| Opioid-induced constipation | Recent opioid use, refractory to standard laxatives | Clinical diagnosis |
| Celiac disease | Bloating, diarrhea, iron deficiency, positive serology | tTG-IgA with total IgA |
| Diabetes-related | Autonomic neuropathy, gastroparesis, peripheral neuropathy | Glucose/HbA1c, autonomic testing |
| Hirschsprung disease | Childhood onset, failure to pass meconium, aganglionic segment | Rectal biopsy (children/young adults) |
| Megacolon/megarectum | Chronic stool retention, massively dilated colon on imaging | Abdominal X-ray, barium enema, anorectal manometry |

## 6.4 Diagnostic Workup

### 6.4.1 History and Physical Examination
- Detailed medication review including over-the-counter and supplements
- Stool diary (frequency, consistency, straining, time spent)
- Digital rectal examination: essential to assess resting tone, squeeze, paradoxical contraction
- Neurologic examination if secondary cause suspected

### 6.4.2 Laboratory Testing
| Test | Purpose |
|------|---------|
| CBC | Anemia, leukocytosis |
| CMP | Electrolytes (especially potassium, calcium), renal function |
| TSH | Hypothyroidism screening |
| Fasting glucose/HbA1c | Diabetes screening |
| Fecal calprotectin | If concern for IBD |

### 6.4.3 Specialized Testing (Selected Cases)
| Test | Indication |
|------|-----------|
| Colonic transit study | Suspected slow transit constipation |
| Anorectal manometry | Suspected pelvic floor dysfunction |
| Balloon expulsion test | Screening for defecatory disorders |
| Defecography (MRI or barium) | Structural abnormalities (rectocele, intussusception) |
| Colonoscopy | Red flags, age-appropriate screening, change in bowel habits |

## 6.5 Management: Stepwise Approach

Based on the 2023 AGA/ACG Joint Guideline for Chronic Idiopathic Constipation. [^96^] [^97^] [^101^]

### 6.5.1 Step 1: Lifestyle Modifications
| Intervention | Recommendation | Evidence |
|-------------|----------------|----------|
| Fiber supplementation | Psyllium 3.5 g daily (start low, titrate up) | Conditional recommendation |
| Fluid intake | 1.5-2 L/day unless contraindicated | Expert opinion |
| Physical activity | 30 minutes moderate exercise, 5 days/week | Moderate evidence |
| Toilet routine | Establish regular routine, especially after meals; respond promptly to urge | Expert opinion |
| Positioning | Footstool to elevate knees above hips (squatting position) | Anecdotal/expert opinion |

> **Fiber Caution**: In patients with severe constipation or suspected slow transit, fiber supplementation may worsen bloating and pain. Start at low dose and increase gradually. Psyllium (soluble fiber) is preferred over insoluble fiber.

### 6.5.2 Step 2: Osmotic Laxatives

| Agent | Dose | Recommendation Level | Notes |
|-------|------|---------------------|-------|
| **PEG 3350** (Miralax) | 17 g daily in 8 oz water | **Strong** | First-line osmotic; tasteless powder; excellent safety profile |
| Lactulose | 15-30 mL daily | Conditional | Sweet taste; may cause bloating and gas |
| Magnesium oxide | 1.5-2 g daily | Conditional | Avoid in renal impairment; inexpensive |
| Magnesium citrate | 150-300 mL as needed | Not for daily use | Bowel preparation; avoid in renal disease |

### 6.5.3 Step 3: Stimulant Laxatives (Rescue or Short-Term)

| Agent | Dose | Recommendation Level | Notes |
|-------|------|---------------------|-------|
| **Sodium picosulfate** | 5-10 mg daily | **Strong** | Prodrug activated by colonic bacteria; well-tolerated |
| Bisacodyl | 5-10 mg PO or PR daily/PRN | Strong for rescue | Oral (enteric-coated) or suppository; abdominal cramping |
| Senna | 8.6-17.2 mg at bedtime | Conditional | Anthraquinone; may cause melanosis coli (benign) |

> **Stimulant Laxative Myth**: Long-term use of stimulant laxatives does NOT cause "cathartic colon" or permanent nerve damage. They are safe for chronic use when needed. [^101^]

### 6.5.4 Step 4: Prescription Medications [^96^]

| Drug | Class | Dose (CIC) | Recommendation | Notes |
|------|-------|-----------|----------------|-------|
| **Linaclotide** | Guanylate cyclase-C agonist | 145 mcg daily | **Strong** | Take 30 min before first meal; secretagogue; increases fluid secretion and transit |
| **Plecanatide** | Guanylate cyclase-C agonist | 3 mg daily | **Strong** | Once daily; diarrhea most common side effect |
| **Prucalopride** | 5-HT4 agonist (prokinetic) | 2 mg daily | **Strong** | Highly selective; increases colonic contractility |
| **Lubiprostone** | Chloride channel activator | 24 mcg BID | Conditional | Take with food; nausea may occur |

### 6.5.5 Step 5: Advanced/Procedural Interventions
- **Biofeedback therapy**: First-line for pelvic floor dysfunction (defecatory disorders); superior to laxatives and surgery; 6-8 sessions
- **Sacral nerve stimulation**: For refractory slow transit constipation
- **Surgery (subtotal colectomy with ileorectal anastomosis)**: Last resort for debilitating, refractory slow transit constipation after all other options exhausted; requires thorough preoperative evaluation

## 6.6 Opioid-Induced Constipation (OIC)

### 6.6.1 Pathophysiology
Opioids activate mu-opioid receptors in the GI tract, reducing acetylcholine release, inhibiting propulsive peristalsis, increasing non-propulsive contractions, increasing anal sphincter tone, and reducing GI secretion.

### 6.6.2 Key Features
- Occurs in 40-80% of patients on chronic opioids
- Often refractory to standard laxatives
- Does NOT develop tolerance (persists as long as opioids are used)
- Prophylactic laxative therapy should be initiated when starting chronic opioids

### 6.6.3 Management
| Approach | Details |
|----------|---------|
| **First-line** | PEG 3350 + stimulant laxative (senna or bisacodyl) |
| **Peripheral opioid antagonists** | |
| Methylnaltrexone | 8-12 mg SC every other day (for patients with advanced illness); 450 mg daily PO |
| Naloxegol | 25 mg PO daily (contraindicated with strong CYP3A4 inhibitors) |
| Naldemedine | 0.2 mg PO daily |
| **Lubiprostone** | 24 mcg BID; may be effective specifically for OIC |
| **Switching opioids** | Methadone or buprenorphine (partial agonist) may cause less constipation |

> **Important**: Peripheral opioid antagonists (methylnaltrexone, naloxegol, naldemedine) do NOT cross the blood-brain barrier and do NOT reverse systemic analgesia.

## 6.7 Special Populations

| Population | Considerations |
|-----------|---------------|
| **Pregnancy** | Diet and exercise first-line; PEG 3350, lactulose, and docusate are generally safe; avoid stimulant laxatives in 1st trimester if possible |
| **Pediatrics** | PEG 3350 first-line; lactulose alternative; rectal disimpaction if needed; maintain adequate hydration; daily maintenance dose important |
| **Elderly** | Higher prevalence; medication review essential; avoid magnesium-containing laxatives in renal impairment; monitor for electrolyte disturbances; watch for fecal impaction with overflow diarrhea |
| **Renal impairment** | Avoid magnesium-containing products; PEG generally safe; reduce dosing of some agents; monitor electrolytes |

## 6.8 Follow-up and Monitoring
- Reassess at 2-4 weeks after initiating therapy
- Adjust laxative regimen based on response
- Goal: 1 spontaneous, complete, soft bowel movement daily without straining
- If no response to first-line therapy, evaluate for pelvic floor dysfunction or slow transit
- Refer to gastroenterology for: red flags, refractory symptoms, diagnostic uncertainty, suspected pelvic floor dysfunction

# Chapter 7: Liver Disease {#chapter7}

## 7.1 Overview

This chapter covers the major categories of liver disease encountered in clinical practice: viral hepatitis (hepatitis B and C), nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)/metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), alcohol-related liver disease (ALD), and the complications of cirrhosis (ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, spontaneous bacterial peritonitis). Autoimmune and cholestatic liver diseases are included in the differential diagnosis section.

## 7.2 Hepatitis B Virus (HBV) Infection [^114^] [^115^]

### 7.2.1 Epidemiology and Transmission
- Approximately 296 million people worldwide living with chronic HBV infection
- Transmitted via blood, sexual contact, and perinatal routes
- 5-10% of infected adults develop chronic infection; up to 90% of infected infants
- Leading cause of cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma globally

### 7.2.2 Serologic Markers and Interpretation

| Marker | Indicates | Clinical Significance |
|--------|-----------|----------------------|
| **HBsAg** | Current infection (acute or chronic) | Persistence >6 months = chronic infection |
| **Anti-HBs** | Immunity | Vaccination-induced or recovery from infection |
| **Anti-HBc (IgM)** | Acute/recent infection | Present during acute window period |
| **Anti-HBc (IgG)** | Past or current infection | Isolated anti-HBc = window period, resolved infection, or false-positive |
| **HBeAg** | Active viral replication | High infectivity; predictor of treatment response |
| **Anti-HBe** | Seroconversion (HBeAg to anti-HBe) | May indicate lower replication (but precore/core promoter mutations possible) |
| **HBV DNA (quantitative)** | Viral load | Treatment monitoring; higher levels = more active replication |

### 7.2.3 Phases of Chronic HBV Infection

| Phase | HBeAg | HBV DNA | ALT | Histology | Management |
|-------|-------|---------|-----|-----------|------------|
| **Immune tolerant** | Positive | Very high (>1 million IU/mL) | Normal | Minimal inflammation | Monitor; consider treatment if age >40 or family history of HCC |
| **Immune active (HBeAg-positive)** | Positive | High (>20,000 IU/mL) | Elevated | Active inflammation | Treat |
| **Inactive carrier** | Negative, anti-HBe positive | Low/undetectable (<2,000 IU/mL) | Normal | Minimal inflammation | Monitor; not treatment candidate |
| **HBeAg-negative chronic hepatitis** | Negative | Moderate (>2,000 IU/mL) | Elevated | Active inflammation | Treat |
| **Resolved** | Negative | Undetectable | Normal | Normal or minimal changes | Monitor; HBsAg may rarely reverse |

### 7.2.4 Screening
- **Universal one-time screening**: All adults recommended (CDC/USPSTF 2023) [^115^]
- All pregnant women at every pregnancy (HBsAg)
- High-risk populations: Persons born in endemic regions, household contacts of HBV-positive persons, injection drug users, men who have sex with men, patients on hemodialysis, HIV-positive persons, immunosuppressed patients

### 7.2.5 Treatment [^114^] [^115^]

**AASLD Guideline Recommendations**:

| Clinical Scenario | Treatment |
|------------------|-----------|
| Immune-active chronic hepatitis (HBeAg-positive or negative) | Treat with first-line antiviral |
| Compensated cirrhosis + detectable HBV DNA | Treat regardless of ALT |
| Decompensated cirrhosis | Treat indefinitely regardless of HBV DNA level |
| Acute liver failure | Treat urgently |
| Immunosuppressive therapy (including anti-TNF, chemotherapy) | Prophylactic antiviral if any detectable HBV DNA or positive HBsAg |

**Preferred First-Line Antivirals**:

| Drug | Dose | Advantages | Monitoring |
|------|------|-----------|------------|
| **Tenofovir alafenamide (TAF)** | 25 mg daily | Improved renal and bone safety vs. TDF; potent suppression | HBV DNA q3mo until undetectable, then q3-6mo; renal function annually |
| **Tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF)** | 300 mg daily | Longest track record; potent; no resistance | Same as TAF + more frequent renal monitoring |
| **Entecavir** | 0.5 mg daily (naive); 1 mg daily (lamivudine-resistant) | Potent; low resistance; generic available | Same monitoring |

> **Not Recommended as First-Line**: Lamivudine, telbivudine, adefovir (due to high resistance rates)

**Peg-IFN alfa-2a**: 180 mcg SC weekly for 48 weeks; finite therapy; higher HBeAg/HBsAg seroconversion rates but significant side effects; contraindicated in decompensated cirrhosis.

### 7.2.6 Monitoring on Treatment
- HBV DNA every 3 months until undetectable, then every 3-6 months
- ALT every 3-6 months
- HBeAg/anti-HBe every 6 months (if HBeAg-positive at baseline)
- Renal function annually (more frequently on TDF)
- HCC surveillance: ultrasound +/- AFP every 6 months in all cirrhotic patients and selected high-risk non-cirrhotic patients

### 7.2.7 Prevention
- **HBV vaccine**: 3-dose series (0, 1, 6 months); >95% protective if anti-HBs >10 mIU/mL
- **Post-exposure prophylaxis**: HBIG + initiate vaccine series within 24 hours for percutaneous/mucosal exposure; HBIG + vaccine for infants born to HBsAg-positive mothers
- **Perinatal prevention**: Tenofovir in 3rd trimester if maternal HBV DNA >200,000 IU/mL; infant HBIG + vaccine at birth

## 7.3 Hepatitis C Virus (HCV) Infection [^100^] [^121^]

### 7.3.1 Epidemiology and Transmission
- Approximately 58 million people worldwide with chronic HCV infection
- Transmitted primarily via blood-to-blood contact
- Acute infection usually asymptomatic; chronic infection develops in 75-85%
- Leading cause of cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, and liver transplantation
- **Now a curable disease** with direct-acting antivirals (DAAs) achieving >95% cure rates

### 7.3.2 Screening and Diagnosis

| Test | Purpose |
|------|---------|
| **Anti-HCV antibody** | Screening test; indicates exposure (past or current) |
| **HCV RNA (PCR)** | Confirms active infection; required if antibody positive |
| **HCV genotype** | Less important with pan-genotypic regimens but may guide treatment |
| **Fibrosis assessment** | Non-invasive (FIB-4, FibroScan) or liver biopsy if indeterminate |

**Screening Recommendations** (CDC/USPSTF 2020):
- One-time screening for all adults aged 18-79 years
- Risk-based screening for ongoing risk factors (injection drug use, incarceration, hemodialysis, MSM with HIV, prior blood transfusion before 1992)
- All pregnant women during each pregnancy

### 7.3.3 Direct-Acting Antiviral (DAA) Therapy [^121^]

**Pan-Genotypic Regimens (Recommended)**:

| Regimen | Duration | Population | Dosing |
|---------|----------|------------|--------|
| **Glecaprevir/Pibrentasvir (GLE/PIB)** | 8 weeks | Treatment-naive, with or without compensated cirrhosis | 3 tablets once daily with food |
| **Sofosbuvir/Velpatasvir (SOF/VEL)** | 12 weeks | Treatment-naive, with or without compensated cirrhosis | 1 tablet daily |

**Special Populations**:

| Population | Recommended Regimen |
|-----------|-------------------|
| Decompensated cirrhosis (CTP B or C) | SOF/VEL + low-dose ribavirin x 12 weeks; OR SOF/VEL x 24 weeks if ribavirin contraindicated |
| DAA failure (retreatment) | SOF/VEL/VOX (Vosevi) x 12 weeks |
| GLE/PIB failure | GLE/PIB + SOF + ribavirin x 16 weeks |
| Severe renal impairment (eGFR <30) | Glecaprevir/pibrentasvir (no renal adjustment needed); avoid SOF-based regimens |

> **Critical Safety Point**: Protease inhibitors (glecaprevir, voxilaprevir) are CONTRAINDICATED in decompensated cirrhosis (risk of hepatic decompensation and death). Use SOF/VEL-based regimens only.

### 7.3.4 Treatment Monitoring
- HCV RNA at baseline and week 4 during treatment
- SVR12 (sustained virologic response at 12 weeks post-treatment) = virologic cure
- CBC, CMP, LFTs at baseline and week 4
- Screen for HBV before treatment (risk of HBV reactivation during DAA therapy; provide prophylaxis if needed)
- Continue HCC surveillance if cirrhosis present (even after SVR)

### 7.3.5 Key Treatment Principles
- >95% cure rates with modern DAAs
- Genotype testing not required for pan-genotypic regimens
- Check drug-drug interactions (use hep-druginteractions.org checker; significant interactions with certain statins, anticonvulsants, PPIs)
- No contraindication with renal impairment for GLE/PIB
- Reinfection possible; harm reduction counseling for ongoing risk
- No vaccine available for HCV

## 7.4 NAFLD/MASLD and NASH [^104^]

### 7.4.1 Terminology Update
The term **MASLD (metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease)** has been proposed to replace NAFLD, reflecting the metabolic underpinnings. **NASH** is now referred to as **MASH (metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis)**. Both terms are currently in transition; this chapter uses the traditional terminology.

### 7.4.2 Epidemiology
- Most common chronic liver disease globally, affecting ~25-30% of the world's population
- Strongly associated with metabolic syndrome components: obesity, type 2 diabetes, dyslipidemia, hypertension
- NASH (inflammatory subtype with hepatocellular injury) affects ~5% of the population
- Leading cause of liver transplantation in women in the United States

### 7.4.3 Diagnosis
- Evidence of hepatic steatosis on imaging or histology
- Exclusion of significant alcohol consumption (>21 drinks/week men, >14 drinks/week women)
- Exclusion of other causes of hepatic steatosis (viral hepatitis, medications, autoimmune)

### 7.4.4 Non-Invasive Assessment of Fibrosis [^104^]

| Tool | Calculation/Cutoff | Interpretation |
|------|-------------------|----------------|
| **FIB-4** | (Age x AST) / (Platelets x sqrt(ALT)) | |
| | <1.3 (low risk) | Low risk; follow in primary care |
| | 1.3-2.67 (intermediate) | Secondary testing needed |
| | >2.67 (high risk) | Refer to hepatology |
| **VCTE (FibroScan)** | <8 kPa | Excludes advanced fibrosis |
| | 8-12 kPa | Indeterminate |
| | >12 kPa | Advanced fibrosis/cirrhosis likely |
| **ELF test** | >=11.3 | Predicts liver-related events |

> **Note**: In patients >65 years, use FIB-4 cutoff >2.0. FIB-4 is not validated in patients <35 years.

### 7.4.5 Management [^104^]

| Intervention | Target/Details | Evidence Level |
|-------------|---------------|----------------|
| **Weight loss** | 5% improves steatosis; 7-10% improves NASH; >10% improves fibrosis | High |
| **Mediterranean diet** | Limit refined carbohydrates, saturated fat; high fiber, monounsaturated fats | Moderate |
| **Exercise** | >=150 min/week moderate-intensity | Moderate; benefits independent of weight loss |
| **Coffee** | >=3 cups daily associated with less advanced liver disease | Moderate |
| **Alcohol avoidance** | Complete abstinence recommended | Expert opinion |
| **Diabetes management** | Tight glycemic control; GLP-1 agonists and SGLT2 inhibitors may have liver benefit | Emerging |
| **Resmetirom** | 80-100 mg daily; thyroid hormone receptor-beta agonist; FDA-approved 2024 for NASH with fibrosis | Clinical trial evidence |
| **Pioglitazone** | 30 mg daily (diabetic NASH) | Improves NASH histology; weight gain concern |
| **Vitamin E** | 800 IU daily (non-diabetic, non-cirrhotic NASH) | Improves NASH histology; unclear long-term safety |
| **Bariatric surgery** | For patients meeting metabolic surgery criteria | Resolves NASH in majority; may improve fibrosis |

### 7.4.6 Monitoring and Surveillance
- Stage fibrosis at diagnosis and serially (FIB-4, FibroScan)
- Manage metabolic comorbidities aggressively
- HCC surveillance: ultrasound every 6 months in patients with cirrhosis
- Liver transplant evaluation for decompensated NASH cirrhosis

## 7.5 Alcohol-Related Liver Disease (ALD) [^122^] [^129^]

### 7.5.1 Spectrum and Epidemiology
- Spectrum ranges from simple steatosis (fatty liver) to alcohol-associated hepatitis (AH) to cirrhosis
- Alcohol is one of the leading causes of liver disease globally
- Risk threshold: >21 standard drinks/week for men, >14 for women (approximately >30 g and >20 g ethanol daily)

### 7.5.2 Alcohol-Associated Hepatitis (AH)
- Acute-on-chronic liver injury superimposed on underlying ALD
- Clinical diagnosis: recent heavy alcohol use, jaundice, tender hepatomegaly
- Labs: elevated bilirubin; AST:ALT ratio typically >2:1 (both usually <300 IU/mL in pure AH)

### 7.5.3 Prognostic Scoring
| Score | Components | Interpretation |
|-------|-----------|----------------|
| **Maddrey's Discriminant Function (MDF)** | 4.6 x (PT - control PT) + bilirubin (mg/dL) | >=32 = severe AH; consider steroids |
| **MELD score** | Based on bilirubin, INR, creatinine, sodium | >20 = severe AH |
| **Lille score** | Assessed after 7 days of steroids | >0.45 = non-responder; discontinue steroids |
| **ABIC score** | Age, bilirubin, INR, creatinine | >9 = severe; consider early transplant |
| **Glasgow AH score** | Age, WBC, bilirubin, BUN, PT | >9 = poor prognosis |

### 7.5.4 Management of Severe Alcohol-Associated Hepatitis [^122^]

| Recommendation | Details |
|---------------|---------|
| **Absolute alcohol abstinence** | Essential; multidisciplinary alcohol use disorder treatment |
| **Nutritional support** | 35 kcal/kg body weight, 1.2-1.5 g/kg protein; enteral if oral inadequate |
| **Corticosteroids** | Prednisolone 40 mg daily for 28 days (if MDF >=32 or MELD >20, and no contraindications) |
| **IV N-acetylcysteine** | Adjunct to corticosteroids for first 5 days (improves 1-month survival) |
| **Lille score at day 7** | >0.45 = steroid non-responder; stop steroids |
| **Infection surveillance** | Screen for infections before steroids; monitor during treatment |
| **Contraindications to steroids** | Active untreated infection, GI bleeding, renal failure, acute pancreatitis |
| **Early liver transplant** | Highly selected patients with first AH episode, severe non-responders, no prior sobriety requirement at some centers |

### 7.5.5 Management of Alcohol Use Disorder
- Psychosocial counseling and support groups
- Medications: naltrexone, acamprosate, disulfiram, baclofen (use with caution in liver disease)
- Multidisciplinary care with hepatology and addiction medicine

## 7.6 Cirrhosis and Its Complications

### 7.6.1 Definition and Classification
- **Compensated cirrhosis**: No complications (ascites, variceal hemorrhage, hepatic encephalopathy, jaundice); median survival >12 years
- **Decompensated cirrhosis**: Presence of any complication; median survival ~2 years without transplant

**Child-Pugh Classification**:

| Parameter | 1 Point | 2 Points | 3 Points |
|-----------|---------|----------|----------|
| Bilirubin (mg/dL) | <2.0 | 2.0-3.0 | >3.0 |
| Albumin (g/dL) | >3.5 | 2.8-3.5 | <2.8 |
| INR | <1.7 | 1.7-2.3 | >2.3 |
| Ascites | None | Mild | Moderate-severe |
| Encephalopathy | None | Grade 1-2 | Grade 3-4 |

| Class | Score | 1-Year Survival | 2-Year Survival |
|-------|-------|----------------|-----------------|
| A | 5-6 points | ~100% | ~85% |
| B | 7-9 points | ~80% | ~60% |
| C | 10-15 points | ~45% | ~35% |

**MELD Score**: Used for liver transplant prioritization; based on bilirubin, creatinine, INR, and sodium. Higher score = higher priority.

### 7.6.2 Ascites Management [^107^] [^109^] [^110^]

| Grade | Description | Management |
|-------|-------------|------------|
| **Grade 1** | Mild; detected only by ultrasound | Sodium restriction alone |
| **Grade 2** | Moderate; evident on physical examination | Sodium restriction + diuretics |
| **Grade 3** | Large/tense ascites | Therapeutic paracentesis + albumin |

**Diagnostic Paracentesis**: Must be performed in ALL patients with ascites admitted to hospital. Send for:
- Cell count and differential
- Ascitic fluid culture (at bedside into blood culture bottles)
- Albumin (to calculate serum-ascites albumin gradient, SAAG)
- Total protein

| SAAG | Interpretation |
|------|---------------|
| >=1.1 g/dL | Portal hypertension (cirrhosis, cardiac, Budd-Chiari) |
| <1.1 g/dL | Non-portal hypertension (peritoneal TB, peritoneal carcinomatosis, pancreatic) |

**Medical Management**:
| Aspect | Recommendation |
|--------|---------------|
| Sodium restriction | 2 g/day (88 mmol/day) |
| Fluid restriction | Only if hyponatremia (<125 mEq/L) |
| Diuretics | Spironolactone 100 mg + furosemide 40 mg daily (in 100:40 ratio) |
| Maximum diuretic dose | Spironolactone 400 mg + furosemide 160 mg daily |
| Weight loss goal | 0.5 kg/day (no peripheral edema) or 1 kg/day (with peripheral edema) |

**Refractory Ascites** (diuretic-resistant or diuretic-intractable):
| Treatment | Details |
|-----------|---------|
| Serial large-volume paracentesis | With albumin replacement (6-8 g per liter removed) |
| TIPS (transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt) | Effective for refractory ascites; may worsen HE |
| Liver transplantation | Definitive therapy |

### 7.6.3 Spontaneous Bacterial Peritonitis (SBP) [^106^] [^109^]

| Aspect | Details |
|--------|---------|
| **Diagnosis** | Ascitic fluid PMN >=250 cells/mm3 |
| **Empiric treatment** | Cefotaxime 2 g IV q8h OR ceftriaxone 1-2 g IV daily |
| **Duration** | Minimum 5 days; treat until PMN <250 |
| **Albumin** | 1.5 g/kg on day 1 + 1 g/kg on day 3 (reduces mortality and renal failure) |
| **Secondary prophylaxis** | Daily norfloxacin 400 mg OR ciprofloxacin 500 mg (lifelong) |
| **Primary prophylaxis** | Norfloxacin for high-risk: ascitic protein <1.5 g/dL + advanced liver failure or renal dysfunction |

### 7.6.4 Hepatic Encephalopathy (HE) [^113^] [^118^]

**Definition**: Neuropsychiatric syndrome caused by liver dysfunction and/or portosystemic shunting; results from accumulation of gut-derived neurotoxins (primarily ammonia).

**Grading (West Haven Criteria)**:
| Grade | Clinical Features |
|-------|------------------|
| 0 (Minimal) | Subclinical; only abnormal on psychometric testing |
| 1 | Trivial lack of awareness, shortened attention span, altered sleep pattern |
| 2 | Lethargy, disorientation, inappropriate behavior, asterixis |
| 3 | Somnolent but rousable, marked confusion, incoherent speech |
| 4 | Coma |

**Precipitating Factors** (always evaluate and correct):
| Factor | Mechanism | Management |
|--------|-----------|------------|
| Infection (SBP most common) | Cytokine release, increased ammonia | Treat infection; prophylactic antibiotics if cirrhosis + GI bleed |
| GI bleeding | Protein load in gut | Endoscopic hemostasis; lactulose |
| Constipation | Increased ammonia absorption | Lactulose |
| Electrolyte disturbances (hypokalemia) | Intracellular shift of ammonia | Correct electrolytes |
| Sedatives/opioids | Direct neurotoxicity | Avoid/discontinue |
| Dehydration | Prerenal azotemia | IV fluids |
| TIPS procedure | Portosystemic shunting | Usually transient; prophylactic lactulose |
| Acute liver insult | New hepatic decompensation | Treat underlying cause |

**Treatment** [^113^]:
| Treatment | Details |
|-----------|---------|
| **Lactulose** | First-line; 15-30 mL 2-3 times daily; titrate to 2-3 soft stools/day |
| **Rifaximin** | 550 mg BID; add to lactulose for prevention of recurrent HE |
| **Correct precipitant** | Treat infection, stop bleeding, correct electrolytes |
| **Protein restriction** | NOT recommended; ensure adequate nutrition (1.2-1.5 g/kg/day) |
| **Zinc** | Supplement if deficient (common in cirrhosis) |
| **L-ornithine L-aspartate** | Alternative ammonia-lowering agent (not widely available) |

**Secondary Prevention**: After second overt HE episode, continue lactulose + rifaximin 550 mg BID indefinitely.

### 7.6.5 Hepatorenal Syndrome (HRS)
- Functional renal failure in cirrhosis without intrinsic kidney disease
- Type 1: Rapidly progressive (doubling creatinine in <2 weeks); very poor prognosis
- Type 2: Gradual onset; associated with refractory ascites
- **Treatment**: Terlipressin 0.5-1 mg IV q4-6h + albumin (where available); midodrine + octreotide + albumin as alternative; TIPS if responder; liver transplant is definitive therapy

## 7.7 Differential Diagnosis of Liver Disease

| Condition | Key Distinguishing Features | Key Tests |
|-----------|---------------------------|-----------|
| Autoimmune hepatitis | Young/middle-aged women, elevated IgG, ANA/SMA positive | ANA, SMA, IgG, liver biopsy (interface hepatitis) |
| Primary biliary cholangitis | Middle-aged women, elevated ALP, pruritus, fatigue | AMA, ALP, GGT, liver biopsy |
| Primary sclerosing cholangitis | Young men, IBD association, cholestatic pattern | ANCA, MRCP/ERCP (beading), liver biopsy |
| Wilson disease | Age 5-35, hemolysis, neuropsychiatric symptoms, low ceruloplasmin | Ceruloplasmin, 24h urinary copper, slit-lamp exam, genetic testing |
| Hemochromatosis | Middle-aged men, diabetes, skin bronzing, elevated ferritin | Ferritin, transferrin saturation, HFE genetic testing |
| Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency | Early-onset emphysema, liver disease, PAS-positive globules | Alpha-1 antitrypsin level and phenotype |
| Drug-induced liver injury | Temporal relationship to medication; exclusion of other causes | RUCAM score; dechallenge/rechallenge |
| Budd-Chiari syndrome | Abdominal pain, ascites, hepatomegaly, hypercoagulable state | Doppler ultrasound, CT/MR venography |
| Veno-occlusive disease/SOS | Sinusoidal obstruction; typically post-HSCT or chemotherapy | Clinical diagnosis; elevated bilirubin, weight gain, hepatomegaly |

## 7.8 Red Flags in Liver Disease

| Feature | Significance | Immediate Action |
|---------|-------------|-----------------|
| New-onset jaundice | Hepatic dysfunction; multiple possible causes | Full liver workup; urgent if accompanied by encephalopathy |
| Ascites or peripheral edema | Decompensated cirrhosis | Diagnostic paracentesis; diuretic therapy |
| Confusion or altered mental status | Hepatic encephalopathy; very poor prognostic sign | Lactulose; evaluate for precipitants; admission |
| GI bleeding (hematemesis, melena) | Variceal or non-variceal bleed in cirrhosis | Resuscitation; antibiotics; octreotide; emergency endoscopy |
| Rapidly rising bilirubin or INR | Acute hepatic decompensation | Urgent hepatology consultation |
| Rising creatinine | Hepatorenal syndrome; high mortality | Volume challenge; albumin; terlipressin if available |
| Severe abdominal pain | SBP, biliary obstruction, ischemia, perforation | Diagnostic paracentesis; imaging; urgent evaluation |

## 7.9 Special Populations

| Population | Key Considerations |
|-----------|-------------------|
| **Pregnancy** | TDF safe for HBV in pregnancy; start 3rd trimester if HBV DNA >200,000 IU/mL; ribavirin CONTRAINDICATED in HCV (teratogenic); acute fatty liver of pregnancy and HELLP syndrome are obstetric emergencies requiring delivery |
| **Pediatrics** | All infants receive HBV birth dose vaccine; HAV vaccine at 12-23 months; Wilson disease and autoimmune hepatitis more common in younger patients; NAFLD increasingly prevalent |
| **Elderly** | Higher risk of drug-induced liver injury; age >65: use FIB-4 cutoff >2.0 for NAFLD screening; decreased physiologic reserve; medication review essential |

## 7.10 Prevention Summary

| Strategy | Details |
|----------|---------|
| Vaccination | HAV and HBV vaccines (routine childhood and at-risk adults) |
| Harm reduction | Needle exchange programs, safe injection practices for injection drug use |
| Blood safety | Screen blood products; safe medical practices |
| Alcohol moderation | Abstinence if ALD; moderation for prevention |
| Weight management | Primary prevention for NAFLD |
| Safe food/water | HAV prevention |
| HCC surveillance | Ultrasound every 6 months in all cirrhotic patients and selected high-risk non-cirrhotic patients |

# Chapter 8: Gallbladder Disorders {#chapter8}

## 8.1 Overview

Gallbladder disorders represent a spectrum of conditions ranging from asymptomatic cholelithiasis (gallstones) to life-threatening acute cholangitis. Gallstones affect 10-15% of adults in Western countries, with cholesterol stones predominating. Understanding the clinical presentation, diagnostic criteria, and evidence-based management is essential for optimal patient outcomes.

### 8.1.1 Gallstone Types and Risk Factors

| Type | Composition | Risk Factors |
|------|------------|-------------|
| **Cholesterol stones** (80% in West) | Cholesterol monohydrate | Female gender (estrogen), age >40, obesity, rapid weight loss, pregnancy, gallbladder stasis |
| **Black pigment stones** | Calcium bilirubinate polymer | Chronic hemolysis (sickle cell, hereditary spherocytosis), cirrhosis |
| **Brown pigment stones** | Calcium salts of fatty acids and bilirubin | Biliary infection (East Asia), parasitic infection (Clonorchis), biliary strictures |

**Additional Risk Factors**: Total parenteral nutrition (TPN), certain medications (ceftriaxone, octreotide, clofibrate), ileal disease/resection (Crohn's), diabetes mellitus, Native American ethnicity.

## 8.2 Cholelithiasis (Gallstones)

### 8.2.1 Natural History
- 80% of gallstones remain asymptomatic throughout life
- Annual risk of developing symptoms: 1-4% per year
- Once symptomatic, high likelihood of recurrent symptoms
- Risk of complications (cholecystitis, pancreatitis, cholangitis) increases with symptomatic stones

### 8.2.2 Clinical Features: Biliary Colic
- Episodic RUQ or epigastric pain lasting 30 minutes to 6 hours
- Triggered by fatty meals (stimulates gallbladder contraction against obstructing stone)
- May radiate to right shoulder or back
- NOT relieved by antacids, position change, or bowel movement
- Associated with nausea; may have vomiting
- Between episodes, patient is asymptomatic
- **Important**: The term "colic" is misleading; biliary "colic" is typically constant, not cramping

### 8.2.3 Management
| Scenario | Management |
|----------|------------|
| **Asymptomatic gallstones** | No treatment (watchful waiting) |
| **Asymptomatic but high-risk** | Elective cholecystectomy: porcelain gallbladder, gallbladder polyps >1 cm, stones >3 cm, sickle cell disease, prior to bariatric surgery, transplant candidates |
| **Symptomatic biliary colic** | Elective laparoscopic cholecystectomy (definitive treatment) |
| **Patient declines surgery** | Ursodeoxycholic acid (ursodiol) 8-10 mg/kg/day may dissolve small cholesterol stones over 6-24 months; high recurrence rate after stopping |

## 8.3 Acute Cholecystitis [^103^] [^108^]

### 8.3.1 Definition
Acute inflammation of the gallbladder, most commonly due to cystic duct obstruction by a gallstone, resulting in gallbladder wall inflammation, edema, and potential ischemia.

### 8.3.2 Diagnosis: Tokyo Guidelines 2018

| Component | Criteria |
|-----------|----------|
| **A: Local signs of inflammation** | Murphy's sign (inspiratory arrest with RUQ palpation), RUQ mass/pain/tenderness |
| **B: Systemic signs of inflammation** | Fever, elevated WBC, elevated CRP |
| **C: Imaging findings** | Gallstones, gallbladder wall thickening (>4 mm), pericholecystic fluid, distended gallbladder, sonographic Murphy's sign |

**Definite diagnosis**: A + C OR A + B + C

### 8.3.3 Tokyo Classification of Severity

| Grade | Features | Management |
|-------|----------|------------|
| **I (Mild)** | No organ dysfunction; disease confined to gallbladder; no severe inflammatory changes | Early laparoscopic cholecystectomy (ELC) within 72 hours |
| **II (Moderate)** | WBC >18,000; palpable RUQ mass; symptoms >72 hours; local complications (gangrenous gallbladder, pericholecystic abscess, emphysematous cholecystitis) | Medical optimization then ELC; percutaneous drainage if high surgical risk |
| **III (Severe)** | Organ failure: cardiovascular (hypotension requiring vasopressors), neurologic (altered mental status), respiratory (PaO2/FiO2 <300), renal (creatinine >2.0), hepatic ( INR >1.5), hematologic (platelets <100,000) | Medical stabilization; organ support; percutaneous or endoscopic drainage; delayed cholecystectomy |

### 8.3.4 Clinical Presentation
- Constant RUQ pain (more prolonged than biliary colic; typically >6 hours)
- Fever and chills
- Nausea and vomiting
- Murphy's sign on examination
- Leukocytosis with left shift
- Elevated bilirubin and ALP if common bile duct involvement

### 8.3.5 Management

**Medical Management** (all patients):
| Intervention | Details |
|-------------|---------|
| NPO (nothing by mouth) | Bowel rest |
| IV fluids | Correct dehydration and electrolytes |
| Analgesia | NSAIDs preferred (ketorolac); opioids if needed |
| Antibiotics | Empiric: ceftriaxone + metronidazole OR piperacillin-tazobactam monotherapy |

**Surgical Management**:
| Timing | Indication | Notes |
|--------|-----------|-------|
| **Early laparoscopic cholecystectomy (ELC)** | Preferred; within 72 hours of symptom onset (up to 7-10 days) | Lower conversion rate, shorter hospital stay vs. delayed |
| **Delayed cholecystectomy** | If presentation >10 days, severe inflammation, uncontrolled comorbidities | Wait 6+ weeks for inflammation to resolve |
| **Open cholecystectomy** | Severe adhesions, Mirizzi syndrome, concern for malignancy | Increased morbidity |

**Non-Surgical Alternatives for High-Risk Surgical Candidates**:
- Percutaneous cholecystostomy (gallbladder drainage) - most common
- Endoscopic ultrasound (EUS)-guided gallbladder drainage
- Transpapillary gallbladder drainage via ERCP

### 8.3.6 Emphysematous Cholecystitis
- Severe, necrotizing infection of the gallbladder wall with gas formation
- Higher mortality than uncomplicated acute cholecystitis
- More common in elderly, diabetics
- Clostridium species and E. coli most common pathogens
- Requires emergent cholecystectomy or drainage + broad-spectrum antibiotics

## 8.4 Choledocholithiasis (Common Bile Duct Stones)

### 8.4.1 Definition and Clinical Features
- Gallstones that have migrated into or formed within the common bile duct (CBD)
- May be asymptomatic or cause: jaundice, biliary colic, cholangitis, or pancreatitis
- Labs: elevated conjugated bilirubin, elevated alkaline phosphatase (ALP), elevated GGT; transaminases may be elevated acutely

### 8.4.2 Diagnosis
| Test | Sensitivity | Notes |
|------|------------|-------|
| Transabdominal ultrasound | ~50% for CBD stones | Operator-dependent; limited by bowel gas |
| MRCP (magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography) | >90% | Non-invasive; excellent for CBD evaluation |
| EUS (endoscopic ultrasound) | >95% | Higher sensitivity than MRCP; therapeutic ERCP can follow |
| ERCP (endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography) | ~90% | Diagnostic and therapeutic; procedural risk |

### 8.4.3 Management
- **ERCP with sphincterotomy and stone extraction**: Definitive therapy for CBD stones
- **Laparoscopic cholecystectomy**: Should follow ERCP (usually within 72 hours during same admission) to prevent recurrence
- **Single-stage laparoscopic approach**: Laparoscopic cholecystectomy with intraoperative cholangiography and CBD exploration (experienced centers)
- **Intraoperative ERCP**: "Rendezvous" technique combining laparoscopic and endoscopic approaches

## 8.5 Acute Cholangitis

### 8.5.1 Definition
Bacterial infection of an obstructed biliary tree. Mortality ranges from 5-10% with prompt treatment to >50% if untreated.

### 8.5.2 Clinical Features

| Finding | Frequency | Notes |
|---------|----------|-------|
| **Charcot's triad** (fever + RUQ pain + jaundice) | 50-70% | Classic but not always complete |
| **Reynolds' pentad** (Charcot's + altered mental status + hypotension) | 5-10% | Indicates severe, suppurative cholangitis; high mortality |
| Fever/chills | 90% | May be the only sign in elderly |
| Jaundice | 80% | Variable intensity |
| RUQ pain | 80% | May be mild |
| Hypotension | Severe cases only | Septic shock |
| Altered mental status | Severe cases only | Indicates organ failure |

### 8.5.3 Tokyo Classification and Severity
| Grade | Features |
|-------|----------|
| **Mild** | Responds to initial medical treatment |
| **Moderate** | Any 2 of: age >75, Charcot's triad, WBC >12,000 or <4,000, fever >39C, bilirubin >5 mg/dL, hypoalbuminemia |
| **Severe** | Organ dysfunction (same criteria as severe cholecystitis) |

### 8.5.4 Management

| Phase | Details |
|-------|---------|
| **Initial resuscitation** | IV fluids, correction of electrolytes, blood cultures before antibiotics |
| **Antibiotics** | Empiric broad-spectrum: piperacillin-tazobactam OR ceftriaxone + metronidazole; adjust based on cultures |
| **Urgent biliary drainage** | Within 24 hours for severe cholangitis; within 72 hours for moderate |
| **ERCP** | First-line: sphincterotomy + stone extraction + stenting if needed |
| **Alternative drainage** | Percutaneous transhepatic biliary drainage (PTBD) or EUS-guided drainage if ERCP fails |
| **Definitive therapy** | Cholecystectomy after recovery (usually 4-6 weeks) |

## 8.6 Differential Diagnosis of Gallbladder and Biliary Disease

| Condition | Pain Location | Key Distinguishing Features | Diagnostic Test |
|-----------|--------------|---------------------------|-----------------|
| Biliary colic | RUQ/epigastric | Intermittent, postprandial, no fever, normal labs | Ultrasound (gallstones) |
| Acute cholecystitis | RUQ | Constant pain >6h, fever, elevated WBC, imaging findings | Ultrasound; Tokyo criteria |
| Acute cholangitis | RUQ | Fever + jaundice + RUQ pain, elevated bilirubin/ALP | ERCP/MRCP/EUS |
| Acute pancreatitis | Epigastric (to back) | Elevated lipase >3x ULN, may follow biliary colic | CT, lipase |
| Hepatitis | RUQ (dull ache) | Elevated transaminases >ALP, viral serologies, less pain | Viral serologies, LFTs |
| Right lower lobe pneumonia | RUQ referred pain | Pulmonary symptoms, fever, chest findings | Chest X-ray |
| Myocardial ischemia | Epigastric/chest | Chest pressure, diaphoresis, ECG changes | ECG, troponins |
| Peptic ulcer disease | Epigastric | Responds to PPI, endoscopic findings | Endoscopy |
| Fitz-Hugh-Curtis syndrome | RUQ | Gonococcal/chlamydial perihepatitis in women | PID workup |

## 8.7 Red Flags in Gallbladder Disease

| Feature | Significance | Action |
|---------|-------------|--------|
| Fever with jaundice and RUQ pain | Acute cholangitis | Emergency; urgent biliary drainage |
| Hypotension or altered mental status with biliary disease | Severe cholangitis or sepsis | Resuscitation; emergency drainage |
| Suspected gallbladder perforation | Peritonitis, sepsis | Emergency surgery |
| Emphysematous cholecystitis | High mortality; necrotizing infection | Emergency cholecystectomy or drainage |
| Severe pancreatitis with biliary etiology | Potentially life-threatening | Urgent ERCP if cholangitis present |
| Persistent vomiting with dehydration | Electrolyte disturbances; obstruction | IV fluids; NG decompression; evaluate cause |
| Gallbladder mass | Gallbladder cancer until proven otherwise | Surgical oncology referral |

## 8.8 Special Populations

| Population | Key Considerations |
|-----------|-------------------|
| **Pregnancy** | Ultrasound first-line imaging; MRCP without gadolinium if needed; ERCP with fetal shielding for cholangitis; cholecystectomy ideally in 2nd trimester if needed; laparoscopic approach preferred |
| **Elderly** | Higher mortality with acute cholecystitis; atypical presentations; lower threshold for intervention; higher risk of complications; higher risk of emphysematous cholecystitis |
| **Diabetes** | Higher risk of complications (emphysematous cholecystitis); increased infection severity; more aggressive management warranted |

## 8.9 Prevention

| Strategy | Details |
|----------|---------|
| Gradual weight loss | Avoid rapid weight loss (>1.5 kg/week increases gallstone risk) |
| Regular physical activity | Reduces gallstone risk |
| Moderate fat intake | Very low-fat diets may promote biliary stasis and stone formation |
| Ursodiol prophylaxis | During rapid weight loss (bariatric surgery, very-low-calorie diets) or TPN |

# Chapter 9: Acute Abdomen {#chapter9}

## 9.1 Overview

The acute abdomen refers to the sudden onset of severe abdominal pain requiring urgent evaluation and potential surgical intervention. It represents one of the most challenging presentations in clinical medicine, encompassing a broad differential diagnosis from self-limited conditions to immediately life-threatening surgical emergencies. A systematic approach integrating focused history, physical examination, and targeted investigations is essential. The primary goal is rapid identification of conditions requiring emergent surgical intervention while avoiding unnecessary operations.

### 9.1.1 Initial Assessment Framework

| Priority | Action | Purpose |
|----------|--------|---------|
| **1. Resuscitation** | Airway, breathing, circulation; 2 large-bore IVs; fluid resuscitation; Foley catheter | Stabilize hemodynamically unstable patients |
| **2. History** | OPQRST pain assessment; last meal; prior surgeries; medications; comorbidities; allergies | Narrow differential; assess surgical risk |
| **3. Physical examination** | Inspection, auscultation, percussion, palpation; hernia orifices; rectal/genital if indicated | Identify peritonitis, localization, hernias |
| **4. Laboratory tests** | CBC, CMP, LFTs, lipase, lactate, urinalysis, pregnancy test, type & screen | Assess severity; identify infection, ischemia, organ injury |
| **5. Imaging** | CT abdomen/pelvis with contrast (most cases); ultrasound (pregnancy, gallbladder, AAA); plain X-ray (obstruction, perforation) | Confirm diagnosis; guide management |

### 9.1.2 Key Questions in the Acute Abdomen History
1. **Onset**: Sudden (perforation, rupture, torsion) vs. gradual (inflammation, obstruction)
2. **Character**: Colicky/cramping (obstruction) vs. constant (inflammation, ischemia)
3. **Severity**: "Tearing" (AAA), "burning" (perforated ulcer), "aching" (appendicitis)
4. **Location and radiation**: See differential diagnosis table below
5. ** aggravating/Alleviating factors**: Movement worsens peritonitis; eating worsens pancreatitis, biliary disease
6. **Associated symptoms**: Fever (infection), vomiting (obstruction), jaundice (biliary), hematuria (renal)
7. **Last meal**: Determines surgical timing (aspiration risk)
8. **Prior surgeries**: Adhesion risk for bowel obstruction
9. **Medications**: Anticoagulants (bleeding risk), steroids (masks peritonitis), immunosuppressants

## 9.2 Appendicitis [^116^] [^117^]

### 9.2.1 Overview
Acute appendicitis is the most common surgical emergency, with a lifetime risk of 7-8%. Obstruction of the appendiceal lumen (by fecalith, lymphoid hyperplasia, foreign body, or tumor) leads to bacterial overgrowth, inflammation, venous congestion, ischemia, and potential perforation.

### 9.2.2 Clinical Presentation
- **Classic history**: Periumbilical pain migrating to RLQ (McBurney's point) within 12-24 hours
- **Associated symptoms**: Anorexia (nearly universal), nausea, low-grade fever
- **Physical examination findings**:
  - McBurney's point tenderness (one-third of the distance from anterior superior iliac spine to umbilicus)
  - Rovsing's sign: LLQ pressure causes RLQ pain
  - Psoas sign: Pain with passive right hip extension (retrocecal appendix)
  - Obturator sign: Pain with internal rotation of flexed right hip (pelvic appendix)
  - Peritoneal signs if perforation: guarding, rigidity, rebound tenderness

### 9.2.3 Diagnostic Scoring: Alvarado Score (MANTRELS)

| Component | Points |
|-----------|--------|
| Migration of pain to RLQ | 1 |
| Anorexia | 1 |
| Nausea or vomiting | 1 |
| Tenderness in RLQ | 2 |
| Rebound pain | 1 |
| Elevated temperature (>37.3C) | 1 |
| Leukocytosis (>10,000) | 2 |
| Left shift (>75% neutrophils) | 1 |

| Score | Risk | Management |
|-------|------|------------|
| 1-4 | Unlikely appendicitis | Observe; consider alternative diagnoses |
| 5-6 | Compatible with appendicitis | Imaging indicated |
| 7-8 | Probable appendicitis | Surgical consultation |
| 9-10 | Very probable appendicitis | Proceed to operative management |

### 9.2.4 Imaging
| Modality | Sensitivity | Specificity | Indication |
|----------|------------|-------------|------------|
| CT abdomen with IV contrast | ~94% | ~95% | Diagnostic test of choice in adults |
| Ultrasound | ~75-90% | ~85-95% | First-line in children and pregnant women |
| MRI | ~97% | ~97% | Pregnant women when ultrasound equivocal |
| **CT findings** | | | Dilated appendix >6 mm, wall thickening, periappendiceal fat stranding, appendicolith, abscess |

### 9.2.5 Management [^116^] [^117^]

**Operative Management (Gold Standard)**:
- **Laparoscopic appendectomy**: Preferred approach; lower wound infection rates, shorter hospital stay, faster recovery, lower mortality
- **Open appendectomy**: Indicated for severe peritonitis, extensive adhesions, insufficient laparoscopic expertise, or resource-limited settings
- **Perforated appendicitis**: Laparoscopic appendectomy with peritoneal lavage; postoperative antibiotics 3-7 days; drain if abscess present

**Non-Operative Management (Antibiotics Alone)**:
- Appropriate for selected patients with uncomplicated acute appendicitis
- Regimen: broad-spectrum IV antibiotics (ceftriaxone + metronidazole OR ertapenem OR moxifloxacin)
- 1-year success rate: 58-75%
- Risk of recurrence: ~30% within 1 year
- **Appendicolith increases failure rate**: 48.7% appendectomy rate at 1 year with appendicolith vs. 30.6% without [^117^]

**Postoperative course**: Return to normal activity within 1-2 weeks (laparoscopic); wound care; monitor for complications (abscess, wound infection, ileus)

## 9.3 Diverticulitis

### 9.3.1 Overview
Acute inflammation of colonic diverticula, most commonly affecting the sigmoid colon. Prevalence increases with age, affecting up to 65% of adults >85 years. Most cases (80-85%) are uncomplicated; 15-20% develop complications.

### 9.3.2 Classification
| Type | Features |
|------|----------|
| **Uncomplicated** | Localized inflammation without abscess or perforation |
| **Complicated** | Abscess, perforation, fistula, obstruction, stricture |

### 9.3.3 Clinical Presentation
- LLQ pain (most common); may be RLQ in Asian populations (right-sided diverticulitis)
- Fever
- Leukocytosis
- Change in bowel habits (constipation or diarrhea)
- Nausea (less common than appendicitis)
- Palpable tender mass if phlegmon/abscess

### 9.3.4 Diagnosis
- **CT abdomen with contrast**: Diagnostic test of choice; assesses severity, complications, alternative diagnoses
- **Hinchey Classification** (guides management):

| Stage | Description | Management |
|-------|-------------|------------|
| Ia | Pericolic phlegmon or small abscess | Antibiotics; percutaneous drainage if accessible |
| Ib | Distant abscess amenable to percutaneous drainage | Antibiotics + CT-guided drainage |
| II | Pelvic or retroperitoneal abscess | Antibiotics + drainage; surgery if fails |
| III | Generalized purulent peritonitis | Emergency surgery |
| IV | Generalized fecal peritonitis | Emergency surgery |

### 9.3.5 Management

**Uncomplicated Diverticulitis**:
| Setting | Treatment |
|---------|-----------|
| Outpatient (selected patients)* | Clear liquid diet; oral antibiotics if immunocompromised or significant comorbidities; close follow-up |
| Inpatient | NPO, IV fluids, IV antibiotics (ceftriaxone + metronidazole OR piperacillin-tazobactam OR amoxicillin-clavulanate) |

*Outpatient criteria: reliable patient, tolerating oral intake, no significant comorbidities, able to follow up within 48-72 hours, no immunocompromise.

> **Note**: 2019 IDSA/SHEA guidelines suggest antibiotics may not be necessary in immunocompetent patients with uncomplicated diverticulitis. However, many clinicians still prescribe antibiotics, especially for high-risk patients. [^102^]

**Complicated Diverticulitis**:
- Abscess (Hinchey Ia-II): CT-guided percutaneous drainage + antibiotics
- Free perforation/peritonitis (Hinchey III-IV): Emergency surgery

**Surgical Options**:
- **Laparoscopic sigmoid colectomy with primary anastomosis**: Preferred for most patients
- **Hartmann's procedure** (sigmoid colectomy with end colostomy): For severe contamination, hemodynamic instability, or unsafe anastomosis
- **Laparoscopic peritoneal lavage**: Controversial; may be considered for Hinchey III in select patients

### 9.3.6 Recurrence Prevention
- High-fiber diet (25-35 g/day)
- **Myth debunked**: Nuts, seeds, and popcorn do NOT increase diverticulitis risk; they may actually be protective
- Physical activity
- Weight management
- Mesalamine or rifaximin: Limited evidence; not routinely recommended
- Consider elective sigmoid colectomy after >/=2 recurrent episodes

## 9.4 Bowel Obstruction

### 9.4.1 Overview
Mechanical or functional interruption of normal intestinal transit. Small bowel obstruction (SBO) accounts for 80% of cases; large bowel obstruction (LBO) accounts for 20%.

### 9.4.2 Etiology

| Type | Common Causes |
|------|--------------|
| **SBO** | Adhesions (60-70% in developed countries), hernias, malignancy, Crohn's disease, gallstone ileus, volvulus, intussusception (children) |
| **LBO** | Colorectal cancer (most common), diverticular stricture, volvulus (sigmoid, cecal), fecal impaction, inflammatory stricture |
| **Functional (ileus)** | Postoperative, medications (opioids), electrolyte disturbances, peritonitis, sepsis |

### 9.4.3 Clinical Presentation
**Classic triad**:
1. Abdominal pain (colicky/cramping, periumbilical for SBO)
2. Vomiting (earlier and more prominent in proximal SBO; feculent in distal/complete obstruction)
3. Obstipation (no flatus or stool)

**Additional findings**: Abdominal distension (more prominent in distal obstruction), high-pitched "tinkling" bowel sounds early (absent later), visible peristalsis (children), dehydration.

### 9.4.4 Diagnosis
| Test | Findings |
|------|----------|
| **Plain abdominal X-ray** | Dilated small bowel loops (>3 cm) or large bowel (>6 cm), air-fluid levels, paucity of colonic gas |
| **CT abdomen with contrast** | Diagnostic test of choice; identifies cause, level, transition point, severity, complications |
| **Transition point** | Where dilated bowel meets collapsed bowel = site of obstruction |
| **CT findings of ischemia** | Bowel wall thickening, pneumatosis intestinalis, portal venous gas, absent enhancement |

### 9.4.5 Management

**Initial Management (All Patients)**:
| Intervention | Details |
|-------------|---------|
| NPO | Bowel rest |
| NG tube | Decompression if vomiting or significant distension |
| IV fluids | Aggressive resuscitation; correct electrolytes |
| Foley catheter | Monitor urine output (>0.5 mL/kg/hr goal) |
| Pain control | Opioids (do NOT mask surgical decision-making in modern practice) |
| Antibiotics | If signs of ischemia, perforation, or strangulation suspected |

**Definitive Management**:

| Scenario | Treatment |
|----------|-----------|
| **Partial SBO** | Conservative management; 60-70% resolve; surgery if no improvement in 24-48 hours |
| **Complete SBO** (no gas beyond transition point) | Surgical exploration |
| **Adhesive SBO** | 60-70% resolve conservatively; early surgery if strangulation suspected |
| **Malignant LBO** | Resection with or without stoma; colonic stent as bridge to surgery |
| **Sigmoid volvulus** | Sigmoidoscopic decompression (first-line); elective sigmoid colectomy to prevent recurrence |
| **Cecal volvulus** | Surgical reduction or right hemicolectomy |

**Strangulation Signs** (requires emergent surgery):
- Peritoneal signs (guarding, rigidity, rebound tenderness)
- Fever, tachycardia, leukocytosis with left shift
- Lactic acidosis
- Absent bowel sounds
- CT: pneumatosis intestinalis, portal venous gas, bowel wall thickening with absent enhancement

## 9.5 Acute Pancreatitis [^111^]

### 9.5.1 Overview
Acute inflammatory condition of the pancreas. Most common causes are gallstones (40-70% in Western populations) and alcohol (25-35%). The Revised Atlanta Classification (2012) is the international standard for diagnosis and severity assessment.

### 9.5.2 Etiology (I GET SMASHED Mnemonic)

| Letter | Cause | Notes |
|--------|-------|-------|
| I | Idiopathic | 10-30% after exhaustive workup |
| G | Gallstones | Most common cause; stones pass through ampulla causing obstruction |
| E | Ethanol (alcohol) | Second most common; typically requires heavy, chronic use |
| T | Trauma | Blunt abdominal trauma, post-surgical |
| S | Steroids | Systemic corticosteroids |
| M | Mumps/malignancy | Viral infections; pancreatic or ampullary cancer |
| A | Autoimmune | Type 1 (IgG4-related) or Type 2 (idiopathic duct-centric) |
| S | Scorpion stings/spider bites | Tropical regions (Tityus scorpions) |
| H | Hyperlipidemia/hypertriglyceridemia, Hypercalcemia | TG >1000 mg/dL; hyperparathyroidism |
| E | ERCP, Emboli | Post-ERCP pancreatitis (3-5%); cholesterol embolization |
| D | Drugs | Azathioprine, 6-MP, valproic acid, ACE inhibitors, GLP-1 agonists, thiazides |

### 9.5.3 Diagnosis
Requires **2 of 3 criteria**:
1. Acute onset of persistent, severe epigastric pain (may radiate to back)
2. Serum lipase or amylase at least 3 times the upper limit of normal
3. Characteristic findings on contrast-enhanced CT, MRI, or ultrasound

> **Note**: Lipase is preferred over amylase (higher specificity). Elevated lipase persists longer than amylase.

### 9.5.4 Revised Atlanta Classification - Severity [^111^]

| Grade | Definition | Mortality |
|-------|-----------|-----------|
| **Mild (MAP)** | No organ failure, no local or systemic complications | <1% |
| **Moderately severe (MSAP)** | Transient organ failure (<48 hours) and/or local/systemic complications | ~10% |
| **Severe (SAP)** | Persistent organ failure (>48 hours): respiratory, cardiovascular, or renal | ~30% |

**Organ Failure Definition** (Modified Marshall Scoring System):
- Respiratory: PaO2/FiO2 <300
- Renal: Serum creatinine >/=1.9 mg/dL
- Cardiovascular: Systolic BP <90 mmHg not responsive to fluids

### 9.5.5 Local Complications
| Complication | Timing | Description |
|-------------|--------|-------------|
| Acute peripancreatic fluid collection | <4 weeks | Homogeneous fluid adjacent to pancreas; no encapsulating wall |
| Pseudocyst | >4 weeks | Encapsulated fluid collection; homogeneous; well-defined wall |
| Acute necrotic collection | <4 weeks | Heterogeneous collection containing necrotic tissue and fluid |
| Walled-off necrosis (WON) | >4 weeks | Encapsulated necrotic collection; heterogeneous |
| Infected necrosis | Any time | Gas in collection; clinical deterioration with fever |

### 9.5.6 Management

**Supportive Care (All Patients)**:
| Intervention | Details |
|-------------|---------|
| **Aggressive IV crystalloid** | Lactated Ringer's preferred (evidence for benefit over normal saline); 250-500 mL/hr initially; goal HCT 35-44% |
| **Pain control** | IV opioids (morphine, hydromorphone, fentanyl); patient-controlled analgesia |
| **Anti-emetics** | Ondansetron, prochlorperazine |
| **Nutrition** | Early oral feeding as tolerated (within 24 hours); nasogastric or nasojejunal if unable to eat; **do NOT need bowel rest** |

**Specific Management**:
| Scenario | Treatment |
|----------|-----------|
| **Gallstone pancreatitis** | ERCP within 24 hours if cholangitis or persistent biliary obstruction; cholecystectomy during same admission |
| **Hypertriglyceridemia** | Insulin infusion + heparin + plasmapheresis if TG >1000 mg/dL |
| **Necrosis without infection** | Supportive care only; no intervention needed |
| **Infected necrosis** | Step-up approach: antibiotics first, then percutaneous/endoscopic drainage, then minimally invasive necrosectomy |
| **Severe necrosis (sterile)** | Step-up approach; delay intervention until walled-off (>4 weeks) when possible |

**Antibiotics**: NOT indicated for prophylaxis in acute pancreatitis. Use only for documented infection (culture-positive necrosis, extrapancreatic infection such as UTI or pneumonia).

### 9.5.7 Prognostic Scores

| Score | Components | Interpretation |
|-------|-----------|----------------|
| **BISAP** | BUN >25, Impaired mental status, SIRS, Age >60, Pleural effusion | Score 0-5; >=3 predicts severe disease |
| **Ranson's criteria** | 11 parameters (5 at admission, 6 at 48 hours) | >=3 criteria = severe; mortality 15-40% |
| **APACHE II** | 12 physiologic variables + age + chronic health | >=8 = severe pancreatitis |

## 9.6 Mesenteric Ischemia [^112^]

### 9.6.1 Overview
Insufficient blood supply to the intestine. Acute mesenteric ischemia (AMI) is a surgical emergency with mortality exceeding 50% even with modern treatment. Chronic mesenteric ischemia presents with postprandial pain and weight loss.

### 9.6.2 Classification and Etiology

| Type | Mechanism | Risk Factors |
|------|----------|-------------|
| **Arterial embolism** (~50%) | Cardiac embolus to SMA (usually at origin of middle colic artery) | Atrial fibrillation, valvular disease, recent MI, cardiomyopathy |
| **Arterial thrombosis** (~25%) | Atherosclerotic plaque rupture in SMA | Advanced atherosclerosis; often history of chronic mesenteric ischemia |
| **Non-occlusive mesenteric ischemia** (~20%) | Low-flow state without mechanical obstruction | Sepsis, heart failure, vasopressors, hypovolemia, dialysis |
| **Venous thrombosis** (~5-15%) | Mesenteric venous thrombosis | Hypercoagulable states (factor V Leiden, prothrombin mutation), malignancy, IBD, abdominal trauma, oral contraceptives |

### 9.6.3 Clinical Features
- **Classic triad** (present in <50%): Acute severe abdominal pain **out of proportion to physical examination findings**, absent bowel sounds early, and minimal/no peritoneal signs initially
- **Hallmark**: Pain out of proportion to exam (patient appears in severe distress but abdomen is soft with minimal tenderness)
- **Later findings**: As bowel infarction progresses, peritonitis develops (guarding, rigidity, absent bowel sounds), sepsis, hemodynamic instability
- **Embolic AMI**: Sudden onset maximal pain; history of atrial fibrillation
- **Thrombotic AMI**: More gradual onset; history of chronic postprandial pain, weight loss ("intestinal angina"), atherosclerotic disease

### 9.6.4 Diagnosis
| Test | Findings |
|------|----------|
| **Labs** | Leukocytosis (>15,000), elevated lactate (late finding), metabolic acidosis, elevated D-dimer (sensitive but non-specific), elevated amylase |
| **CT angiography** | **Gold standard**; sensitivity 96-100%, specificity 89-94% |
| **CT findings** | SMA embolus/thrombus, bowel wall thickening, pneumatosis, portal venous gas, absent bowel wall enhancement, SMA filling defect |
| **Angiography** | Historical gold standard; now reserved for therapeutic intervention |
| **Plain X-ray** | Late: thumbprinting, ileus pattern, pneumatosis, portal venous gas |

### 9.6.5 Management [^112^]

| Phase | Details |
|-------|---------|
| **Initial** | Fluid resuscitation, broad-spectrum antibiotics (cover gut flora), systemic anticoagulation (unfractionated heparin) |
| **SMA embolism** | Embolectomy (open or catheter-directed aspiration); assessment of bowel viability; resection of non-viable bowel |
| **SMA thrombosis** | Bypass surgery (antegrade from supraceliac aorta or retrograde from infrarenal aorta) OR endovascular SMA stenting; bowel resection if necrotic |
| **NOMI** | Treat underlying cause; intra-arterial vasodilators (papaverine) via SMA catheter; reduce/eliminate vasopressors |
| **Mesenteric venous thrombosis** | Anticoagulation (heparin); surgery only if bowel infarction suspected |
| **"Second-look" laparotomy** | Planned re-exploration at 24-48 hours if bowel viability uncertain at initial operation |

> **Critical Principle**: Peritonitis or clinical/radiologic evidence of bowel necrosis = emergent laparotomy. Delays in operative intervention dramatically increase mortality.

## 9.7 Differential Diagnosis of the Acute Abdomen

| Condition | Pain Location | Character | Key Features | Key Test |
|-----------|--------------|-----------|-------------|----------|
| Appendicitis | RLQ (migratory) | Constant, aching | Fever, anorexia, leukocytosis, McBurney's tenderness | CT abdomen |
| Diverticulitis | LLQ | Constant, aching | Fever, leukocytosis, older patient, prior episodes | CT abdomen |
| Cholecystitis | RUQ | Constant, colicky | Murphy's sign, fever, elevated WBC, ultrasound findings | Ultrasound |
| Pancreatitis | Epigastric (to back) | Severe, constant | Elevated lipase >3x ULN, risk factors (gallstones, alcohol) | CT, lipase |
| Bowel obstruction | Diffuse, cramping | Colicky, waves | Distension, vomiting, obstipation, prior surgery | CT abdomen |
| Mesenteric ischemia | Diffuse | Severe, out of proportion | Lactic acidosis, atrial fibrillation, vascular risk factors | CT angiography |
| Perforated viscus | Diffuse, sudden | "Knife-like," tearing | Rigid abdomen, free air under diaphragm, board-like rigidity | Upright CXR, CT |
| Ruptured AAA | Back, abdominal | Severe, tearing | Pulsatile mass, hypotension, older male smoker, vascular risk | CT angiography |
| Ectopic pregnancy | Lower abdominal | Unilateral, cramping | Positive pregnancy test, vaginal bleeding, amenorrhea | Beta-hCG, ultrasound |
| Ovarian torsion | Lower abdominal | Sudden, severe | Adnexal mass on exam, nausea, sudden onset | Pelvic ultrasound |
| Nephrolithiasis | Flank to groin | Severe, colicky | Hematuria, prior stones, flank tenderness | CT non-contrast |
| Pyelonephritis | Flank | Constant, aching | Fever, dysuria, CVA tenderness, pyuria | Urinalysis, CT |

## 9.8 Red Flags in the Acute Abdomen

| Feature | Significance | Immediate Action |
|---------|-------------|-----------------|
| Peritonitis (guarding, rigidity, rebound) | Intra-abdominal catastrophe | Emergency surgical consultation |
| Hemodynamic instability (tachycardia, hypotension) | Sepsis, hemorrhage, necrosis | Aggressive resuscitation; emergent intervention |
| Severe sepsis/septic shock | High mortality | Source control + broad-spectrum antibiotics |
| GI bleeding with acute abdomen | Perforated ulcer, ischemic bowel, malignancy | Resuscitation; emergent endoscopy/surgery |
| Free air under diaphragm | Hollow viscus perforation | Emergency surgery |
| Pneumatosis intestinalis | Bowel ischemia/necrosis | Emergency surgery |
| Portal venous gas | Bowel necrosis (advanced) | Emergency surgery; very poor prognosis |
| Acute abdomen in pregnancy | Higher maternal/fetal mortality | Urgent multidisciplinary management |
| Acute abdomen with immunosuppression | Atypical presentations; opportunistic infections | Broad-spectrum coverage; early imaging |

## 9.9 Special Populations

| Population | Key Considerations |
|-----------|-------------------|
| **Elderly** | Atypical presentations common; diminished pain perception; blunted fever response; higher mortality; maintain low threshold for imaging and surgical intervention |
| **Immunocompromised** | Opportunistic infections (CMV colitis, neutropenic enterocolitis/typhlitis, MAC, fungal infections); drug-related complications; atypical presentations; broader differential |
| **Pregnancy** | Appendicitis is most common non-obstetric surgical emergency; physiological leukocytosis confuses interpretation; ultrasound preferred initial imaging; MRI second-line (no gadolinium); avoid CT if possible; elevated gravid uterus displaces appendix superiorly |
| **Pediatrics** | Intussusception (target sign on ultrasound), malrotation with volvulus (bilious vomiting = emergency), Meckel's diverticulitis, appendicitis (perforation more common due to diagnostic delays) |

## 9.10 General Principles of Acute Abdomen Management

1. **Never let the sun set on an acute abdomen without a diagnosis** - Obtain imaging promptly
2. **Peritonitis = operation until proven otherwise** - Do not delay surgical consultation
3. **Elderly patients decompensate quickly** - Lower threshold for intervention
4. **CT with contrast is the workhorse of acute abdomen imaging** - Use in most adults
5. **Always rule out cardiac causes** - MI can present with epigastric pain only
6. **Beta-hCG in all women of childbearing age** - Before any imaging with radiation
7. **Antibiotics for suspected intra-abdominal infection** - Early, broad-spectrum, source control
8. **Document decision-making** - Especially for non-operative management

# Chapter 10: Gastrointestinal Bleeding {#chapter10}

## 10.1 Overview

Gastrointestinal bleeding is classified by anatomic location into upper (proximal to the ligament of Treitz), lower (distal to the ligament of Treitz), and obscure (persistent or recurrent bleeding with negative initial evaluation). It is a common medical emergency with significant morbidity and mortality, particularly in elderly patients and those with comorbidities. Prompt assessment, risk stratification, resuscitation, and targeted intervention are essential for optimal outcomes.

### 10.1.1 Classification by Location and Presentation

| Category | Location | Common Presentation |
|----------|----------|-------------------|
| **Upper GI bleeding** | Proximal to ligament of Treitz | Hematemesis, coffee-ground emesis, melena |
| **Lower GI bleeding** | Distal to ligament of Treitz | Hematochezia (bright red blood per rectum), maroon stools |
| **Obscure GI bleeding** | Any location; negative initial EGD + colonoscopy | Recurrent IDA, positive FOBT, intermittent melena/hematochezia |

> **Important caveat**: Hematochezia with hemodynamic instability may represent a **brisk upper GI bleed** (bleeding from proximal duodenum at rates >200 mL/min can present with bright red blood per rectum). Always consider upper GI source in patients with hematochezia and hemodynamic compromise.

## 10.2 Upper GI Bleeding (UGIB) [^105^]

### 10.2.1 Epidemiology and Etiology
- Annual incidence: 100-180 per 100,000 adults
- Mortality: 2-10% (higher in elderly, those with comorbidities, and variceal bleeding)
- **Most common causes**:
  1. Peptic ulcer disease (40-50%) - H. pylori, NSAIDs
  2. Esophageal/gastric varices (10-30%) - portal hypertension
  3. Mallory-Weiss tear (5-15%) - retching/vomiting
  4. Erosive esophagitis/gastritis (5-15%)
  5. Malignancy (1-5%)
  6. Dieulafoy lesion (1-3%) - large submucosal vessel
  7. Gastric antral vascular ectasia (GAVE) (1-2%) - "watermelon stomach"

### 10.2.2 Risk Stratification Scores [^105^]

| Score | Components | Purpose | Key Cutoff |
|-------|-----------|---------|------------|
| **Glasgow-Blatchford Score (GBS)** | BUN, Hgb, systolic BP, pulse, melena, syncope, hepatic disease, cardiac failure | Predicts need for intervention (transfusion, endoscopy, surgery) | **GBS <=1: Safe for outpatient management** |
| **Rockall Score (pre-endoscopic)** | Age, shock (pulse/BP), comorbidities | Predicts mortality | Score 0-2: Low risk (<5% mortality) |
| **Rockall Score (post-endoscopic)** | Above + endoscopic diagnosis + stigmata of recent hemorrhage | Predicts rebleeding and mortality | Score >5: High risk |
| **AIMS65** | Albumin, INR, mental status, systolic BP, age >65 | Predicts in-hospital mortality | Score >/=2: High mortality risk |

### 10.2.3 Initial Management (All UGIB Patients)

| Step | Action | Details |
|------|--------|---------|
| 1. Resuscitation | 2 large-bore IVs (14-16 gauge); IV fluids | Crystalloid initially; blood products if needed |
| 2. Blood products | Restrictive transfusion strategy | Transfuse when Hgb <7 g/dL (<8 g/dL if cardiovascular disease); target Hgb 7-9 g/dL |
| 3. IV PPI | High-dose before endoscopy | Pantoprazole or omeprazole 80 mg IV bolus + 8 mg/hr infusion; reduces stigmata at endoscopy but does not improve mortality |
| 4. Antibiotics (if cirrhosis) | Prophylactic ceftriaxone | 1 g IV daily for 7 days; reduces infection risk and improves survival |
| 5. Vasoactive agents (if varices) | Octreotide or terlipressin | Octreotide: 50 mcg IV bolus + 50 mcg/hr infusion |
| 6. Endoscopy | Within 24 hours | Diagnostic and therapeutic; sooner if active hemorrhage |
| 7. Airway protection | Intubation if massive bleeding | Protect airway before endoscopy in massive hematemesis |

### 10.2.4 Forrest Classification of Ulcer Stigmata [^105^]

| Classification | Endoscopic Appearance | Rebleeding Risk | Endoscopic Therapy |
|---------------|----------------------|-----------------|-------------------|
| IA | Active spurting hemorrhage | 80-100% | Required (clips, thermal, injection) |
| IB | Active oozing hemorrhage | 10-30% | Required |
| IIA | Non-bleeding visible vessel (pigmented protuberance) | 40-50% | Recommended |
| IIB | Adherent clot | 8-25% | Consider (after clot removal with irrigation) |
| IIC | Flat pigmented spot (hematin) | 0-8% | Not indicated |
| III | Clean base (white fibrin) | 0-3% | Not indicated |

### 10.2.5 Endoscopic Therapy
- **Dual therapy preferred**: Epinephrine injection + second modality (thermal coagulation or mechanical clips)
- **Mechanical clips**: Increasingly preferred as primary therapy for visible vessels
- **Thermal coagulation**: Heater probe or bipolar probe for hemostasis
- **After successful hemostasis**: Continue high-dose IV PPI for 72 hours; repeat endoscopy if rebleeding

### 10.2.6 Variceal Bleeding Management

| Step | Intervention |
|------|-------------|
| 1. Vasoactive agents | Octreotide 50 mcg IV bolus + 50 mcg/hr; terlipressin 2 mg IV q4h (where available) |
| 2. Antibiotics | Ceftriaxone 1 g IV daily x 7 days |
| 3. Endoscopy (within 12 hours) | Esophageal varices: **band ligation** (EVL); Gastric varices: cyanoacrylate injection |
| 4. Balloon tamponade | Sengstaken-Blakemore or Minnesota tube; temporary bridge only; high complication rate |
| 5. TIPS | If endoscopic therapy fails; reduces portal pressure |
| 6. Prevention of rebleeding | Repeat band ligation every 2-4 weeks until obliteration; non-selective beta-blocker (propranolol or nadolol) |

## 10.3 Lower GI Bleeding (LGIB) [^124^] [^125^] [^126^]

### 10.3.1 Epidemiology and Etiology
- Annual incidence: 20-30 per 100,000 adults
- Increases dramatically with age
- **Most common causes**:
  1. Diverticular bleeding (30-40%) - painless, abrupt, large-volume
  2. Angioectasia/vascular ectasia (20-30%) - chronic occult bleeding
  3. Colorectal cancer/polyps (10-20%) - chronic occult or overt bleeding
  4. IBD (5-10%) - bloody diarrhea with mucus
  5. Ischemic colitis (5-10%) - acute onset with abdominal pain
  6. Hemorrhoids (5-10%) - bright red blood coating stool
  7. Anal fissure (5%) - painful rectal bleeding
  8. Post-polypectomy bleeding (2-10%) - delayed bleeding up to 14 days

### 10.3.2 Risk Stratification

| Tool | Purpose | Key Components |
|------|---------|---------------|
| **Oakland Score** | Identifies low-risk patients safe for discharge | Age, sex, digital rectal examination, Hgb, HR, BP, prior LGIB |
| **ACRS** | Predicts severity | Age, comorbidities, vital signs, Hgb |

### 10.3.3 Management (ACG 2023 / ESGE 2021 Guidelines) [^124^] [^125^] [^126^]

**Step 1: Initial Resuscitation**
- IV access, crystalloid fluids
- Blood transfusion: restrictive strategy (Hgb <7 g/dL; <8 g/dL with cardiovascular disease); target post-transfusion Hgb 7-9 g/dL [^124^]
- Reverse anticoagulation if life-threatening: Vitamin K 10 mg IV + 4-factor PCC for warfarin; hold DOACs temporarily
- Type and crossmatch blood

**Step 2: Localization**
- Rule out upper GI source: 15% of patients presenting with hematochezia have UGIB
- NG lavage is NOT recommended (poor sensitivity and specificity)
- Consider urgent EGD if clinical suspicion for brisk UGIB

**Step 3: Definitive Management**

| Scenario | Management |
|----------|------------|
| **Severe ongoing active bleeding** | CT angiography first (sensitivity ~90%); if positive, proceed to transcatheter embolization or surgery |
| **Stable/moderate bleeding** | Colonoscopy (bowel preparation with 4-6 L PEG) |
| **CT angiography positive** | Transcatheter arterial embolization (success rate ~98%) or surgical resection |
| **CT angiography negative** | ~80% will have no rebleeding; elective colonoscopy |

**Colonoscopy**:
- Standard diagnostic test in stable patients
- Perform during hospital stay; bowel preparation essential (4-6 L PEG over 3-4 hours)
- **Urgent colonoscopy (<24 hours) has NOT been shown to improve outcomes** [^126^] [^131^]
- Endoscopic hemostasis: clips (preferred for diverticular bleeding), thermal coagulation, epinephrine injection, band ligation

**Interventional Radiology**:
- Transcatheter arterial embolization for active bleeding not controlled endoscopically
- Ideally performed within 90 minutes of positive CT angiography
- Risk of bowel ischemia (~10-20%)

**Surgery**:
- Segmental colectomy if bleeding source localized and persistent
- Subtotal colectomy with ileorectal anastomosis if unable to localize
- Hartmann's procedure if hemodynamically unstable

### 10.3.4 Post-Discharge Management
- **Resume aspirin for secondary cardiovascular prevention** as soon as bleeding stops (within 3-7 days) to prevent cardiovascular events [^130^]
- **Resume anticoagulation**: Timing individualized; balance thrombotic vs. rebleeding risk; typically resume DOACs at 3-7 days; warfarin at 5-7 days with bridge if needed
- **Avoid NSAIDs**
- **Colonoscopy follow-up**: As indicated; complete polypectomy surveillance; IBD follow-up

## 10.4 Obscure GI Bleeding

### 10.4.1 Definition
Persistent or recurrent GI bleeding with negative initial evaluation with EGD and colonoscopy.

### 10.4.2 Systematic Approach
| Step | Investigation | Yield |
|------|-------------|-------|
| 1. Repeat EGD and colonoscopy | Missed lesions are common (lesions in duodenal bulb, retroflexed views, poor preparation) | 10-35% |
| 2. Capsule endoscopy | First-line for small bowel evaluation | 40-60% diagnostic yield |
| 3. Device-assisted enteroscopy | Push, single-balloon, or double-balloon; for biopsy or therapy of lesions found on capsule | 40-60% yield |
| 4. CT/MR enterography | Small bowel Crohn's, tumors, vascular lesions | Moderate yield |
| 5. Tagged RBC scan | For intermittent bleeding (requires >0.1 mL/min) | Low specificity |
| 6. Angiography | For active bleeding >0.5 mL/min; therapeutic embolization possible | High yield if active bleeding |

### 10.4.3 Common Causes of Obscure Bleeding
| Cause | Characteristics | Diagnostic Test |
|-------|---------------|-----------------|
| Small bowel angioectasia | Elderly, chronic occult bleeding, CKD, aortic stenusis (Heyde syndrome) | Capsule endoscopy, enteroscopy |
| Small bowel tumors | GIST, adenocarcinoma, lymphoma, carcinoid, melanoma | CT/MR enterography, capsule endoscopy |
| Meckel's diverticulum | Younger patients, ectopic gastric mucosa | Technetium-99m pertechnetate scan |
| Crohn's disease (small bowel) | Younger patients, abdominal pain, weight loss | CT/MR enterography, capsule endoscopy |
| NSAID enteropathy | Diaphragm-like strictures, web-like strictures | Capsule endoscopy |

## 10.5 Differential Diagnosis Summary

### Upper GI Bleeding
| Condition | Key Features | Endoscopic Findings |
|-----------|-------------|-------------------|
| Peptic ulcer disease | H. pylori, NSAIDs; most common cause | Round/ovoid ulcer with clean base, visible vessel, or active bleeding |
| Esophageal varices | Portal hypertension, stigmata of liver disease | Dilated submucosal veins in lower esophagus; red wale marks indicate high risk |
| Gastric varices | Splenic vein thrombosis, portal hypertension | Dilated veins in gastric fundus; may occur without esophageal varices |
| Mallory-Weiss tear | History of retching/vomiting; hematemesis after forceful vomiting | Linear mucosal tear at gastroesophageal junction |
| Erosive esophagitis | GERD symptoms; severe erosive disease causes bleeding | Linear erosions, confluent ulcerations in distal esophagus |
| Gastric malignancy | Weight loss, progressive symptoms, older age | Mass lesion, irregular ulcer with heaped-up margins |
| Dieulafoy lesion | Massive acute bleeding; no ulceration around vessel | Large tortuous submucosal artery with minimal surrounding mucosal defect |
| GAVE | Chronic occult bleeding; associated with cirrhosis, autoimmune | "Watermelon stomach" - parallel erythematous stripes radiating from pylorus |

### Lower GI Bleeding
| Condition | Key Features | Management |
|-----------|-------------|------------|
| Diverticular bleeding | Painless, abrupt, large-volume hematochezia; most common cause | Self-limited in 80%; endoscopic clips or band ligation; embolization |
| Angioectasia | Chronic occult bleeding; older patients; cecum/ascending colon | Endoscopic thermal coagulation or clips; treat coagulopathy |
| Colorectal cancer | Chronic occult or overt bleeding; change in bowel habits; weight loss | Resection |
| IBD | Bloody diarrhea, mucus, abdominal pain, tenesmus | Medical therapy for IBD |
| Ischemic colitis | Sudden onset, abdominal pain, "watermelon" colon on endoscopy | Supportive; antibiotics if severe; surgery for gangrene |
| Hemorrhoids | Bright red blood coating stool, on toilet paper, dripping into toilet | Dietary modification, rubber band ligation, sclerotherapy, hemorrhoidectomy |
| Anal fissure | Painful rectal bleeding with defecation; sentinel pile | Topical nitrates, calcium channel blockers, botulinum toxin, lateral internal sphincterotomy |
| Radiation proctitis | History of pelvic radiation; chronic rectal bleeding | Endoscopic argon plasma coagulation, formalin, sucralfate enemas |
| Rectal varices | Portal hypertension, stigmata of liver disease; hemodynamically significant | Endoscopic band ligation; TIPS |
| Meckel's diverticulum | Younger patients, painless bleeding, heterotopic gastric mucosa | Technetium scan; surgical resection |

## 10.6 Medications for GI Bleeding

| Drug | Dose | Indication | Notes |
|------|------|------------|-------|
| Pantoprazole (IV) | 80 mg bolus + 8 mg/hr infusion | UGIB before and after endoscopy | High-dose reduces rebleeding after endoscopic hemostasis |
| Omeprazole (IV) | 80 mg bolus + 8 mg/hr infusion | Alternative PPI | Equivalent efficacy |
| Octreotide | 50 mcg IV bolus + 50 mcg/hr infusion | Variceal bleeding | Reduces splanchnic blood flow |
| Terlipressin | 2 mg IV q4h (1 mg q4h after bleeding controlled) | Variceal bleeding (where available) | Vasoconstrictor; reduces portal pressure |
| Ceftriaxone | 1 g IV daily x 7 days | Antibiotic prophylaxis in cirrhosis with GI bleed | Reduces infection and mortality |
| Vitamin K | 10 mg IV | Warfarin reversal | Takes 6-24 hours to normalize INR |
| 4-factor PCC | 25-50 IU/kg IV | Urgent warfarin reversal | Rapid reversal; preferred over FFP |
| Tranexamic acid | 1 g IV q8h | Fibrinolysis; may reduce mortality in UGIB | CONTROVERSIAL; not routinely recommended |

## 10.7 Special Populations

| Population | Key Considerations |
|-----------|-------------------|
| **Cirrhosis** | All GI bleeds treated as variceal until proven otherwise; antibiotic prophylaxis mandatory; octreotide/terlipressin; early band ligation for varices; high rebleeding (~60% at 1-2 years) and mortality risk |
| **Anticoagulated patients** | Hold DOACs temporarily; reverse warfarin if life-threatening; resume anticoagulation when hemostasis achieved; balance thrombotic vs. rebleeding risk |
| **Elderly** | Higher mortality (10-20%); atypical presentations; broader differential; lower threshold for ICU admission; higher transfusion thresholds may be appropriate |
| **Pediatrics** | Upper endoscopy for melena; Meckel's scan for obscure LGIB; intussusception presents with currant jelly stool; swallowed maternal blood (neonates) |
| **Pregnancy** | Physiologic anemia; increased risk of hemorrhoids; Mallory-Weiss tears; defer non-urgent procedures to 2nd trimester |

## 10.8 Prevention

| Strategy | Target Population | Intervention |
|----------|-------------------|-------------|
| PPI prophylaxis | High-risk patients on NSAIDs or dual antiplatelet therapy | PPI (omeprazole 20 mg daily) |
| H. pylori eradication | All infected patients | Appropriate 14-day regimen + test-of-cure |
| Variceal screening | All cirrhotic patients | EGD; band ligation if medium/large varices with red wale marks or Child-Pugh C |
| Non-selective beta-blockers | Cirrhotic patients with varices not suitable for band ligation | Propranolol 20-40 mg BID; nadolol 20-40 mg daily |
| NSAID avoidance | All patients with history of PUD bleeding | Acetaminophen preferred; lowest NSAID dose if unavoidable + PPI |
| Colorectal cancer screening | Average-risk adults beginning at age 45 | Colonoscopy, FIT, or other approved modalities |
| Smoking cessation | All patients | Reduces risk of PUD, cancer, ischemic colitis |
| Alcohol moderation | All patients | Reduces risk of variceal bleeding, gastritis, Mallory-Weiss tears |
| Anticoagulation optimization | Patients on anticoagulation | Lowest effective dose; regular monitoring; PPI if GI risk |

## 10.9 Discharge Criteria and Follow-up

| Parameter | Criteria |
|-----------|----------|
| Hemodynamic stability | Stable for 24 hours without ongoing transfusion needs |
| Hemoglobin stability | Stable or rising Hgb for 24 hours |
| Successful hemostasis | Documented at endoscopy or no evidence of ongoing bleeding |
| Tolerating diet | Able to tolerate oral intake without recurrent bleeding |
| Outpatient plan | Arranged follow-up (7-14 days), medication reconciliation, H. pylori test-of-cure scheduled |
| Education provided | Avoidance of NSAIDs, alcohol; medication adherence; warning signs |

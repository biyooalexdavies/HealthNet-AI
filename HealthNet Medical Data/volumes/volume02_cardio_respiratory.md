# Chapter 1: Hypertension

## 1.1 Overview

Hypertension (HTN) is defined as sustained elevation of systemic arterial blood pressure. It is the most common preventable risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD), ischemic stroke, hemorrhagic stroke, and chronic kidney disease (CKD). Approximately 46% of adults in the United States have hypertension, and only about 1 in 4 have their blood pressure under control. The 2017 ACC/AHA Guideline introduced a lower threshold for diagnosis compared to the earlier JNC-7 framework, effectively reclassifying millions of Americans as hypertensive and expanding the indication for treatment [^1^] [^23^].

**Pathophysiology (Brief):** Hypertension results from increased cardiac output, increased systemic vascular resistance, or both. Key mechanisms include: (1) dysregulation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS), (2) sympathetic nervous system overactivity, (3) endothelial dysfunction with decreased nitric oxide bioavailability, (4) sodium retention and volume expansion, (5) vascular remodeling and stiffness, and (6) genetic and epigenetic factors. Over time, uncontrolled hypertension leads to target organ damage including left ventricular hypertrophy (LVH), nephrosclerosis, retinopathy, and accelerated atherosclerosis.

**Epidemiology:** HTN affects approximately 1.28 billion adults worldwide. In the US, non-Hispanic Black adults have the highest prevalence (approximately 54%) and develop HTN earlier with greater severity than non-Hispanic White, Hispanic, and Asian adults. Only about 22% of adults with HTN have controlled BP (<130/80 mmHg).

## 1.2 Classification

### 1.2.1 Blood Pressure Categories (2017 ACC/AHA) [^1^] [^23^]

| Category | Systolic BP (mmHg) | | Diastolic BP (mmHg) |
|----------|-------------------|---|---------------------|
| Normal | <120 | and | <80 |
| Elevated | 120-129 | and | <80 |
| Stage 1 Hypertension | 130-139 | or | 80-89 |
| Stage 2 Hypertension | >=140 | or | >=90 |
| Hypertensive Crisis | >=180 | and/or | >=120 |

> **Key Points:** Adults with SBP and DBP in two different categories should be classified according to the higher category. Diagnosis requires an average of >=2 careful readings obtained on >=2 separate occasions. Out-of-office blood pressure monitoring (ambulatory blood pressure monitoring [ABPM] or home blood pressure monitoring [HBPM]) is recommended to confirm diagnosis and evaluate for white coat or masked hypertension.

### 1.2.2 Comparison: JNC-7 vs. 2017 ACC/AHA

| SBP (mmHg) | DBP (mmHg) | JNC-7 Classification | 2017 ACC/AHA Classification |
|------------|-----------|---------------------|----------------------------|
| <120 | <80 | Normal | Normal |
| 120-129 | <80 | Prehypertension | Elevated |
| 130-139 | 80-89 | Prehypertension | Stage 1 HTN |
| 140-159 | 90-99 | Stage 1 HTN | Stage 2 HTN |
| >=160 | >=100 | Stage 2 HTN | Stage 2 HTN |

> **Clinical Impact:** The 2017 ACC/AHA classification increases the prevalence of HTN from 32% (JNC-7) to 46% in US adults, but the number of adults requiring medication only increases modestly (from 31% to 36% of all US adults) because most newly classified patients fall into the Stage 1 category and have low cardiovascular risk, warranting lifestyle modification as first-line therapy.

## 1.3 Clinical Presentation

Hypertension is frequently asymptomatic, earning it the designation "silent killer." When symptoms do occur, they typically indicate target organ damage or a hypertensive emergency:

- **Headache:** Usually occipital, morning headaches; more common with severe hypertension (>=180/120 mmHg)
- **Dizziness:** Often medication-related or due to cerebrovascular involvement
- **Dyspnea:** Suggests left ventricular dysfunction or pulmonary edema
- **Chest pain:** May indicate concurrent CAD or aortic dissection (emergency)
- **Visual changes:** Blurred vision, scotomata (hypertensive retinopathy grade III-IV)
- **Epistaxis:** May occur with markedly elevated BP
- **Symptoms of secondary causes:** Muscle weakness (hypokalemia/aldosteronism), snoring/daytime somnolence (OSA), palpitations/tremor (pheochromocytoma), cold intolerance (hypothyroidism)

## 1.4 Diagnostic Workup

### 1.4.1 Proper Blood Pressure Measurement
- Patient seated quietly for 5 minutes, feet on floor, back supported
- Arm supported at heart level
- Appropriate cuff size (bladder encircling 80% of arm; use large adult cuff if arm circumference >32 cm, thigh cuff if >42 cm)
- No caffeine, exercise, or smoking for 30 minutes prior
- No talking during measurement
- Average of 2-3 readings, 1 minute apart
- Measure in both arms initially; use the higher reading arm for subsequent measurements
- If arm difference >10-15 mmHg, evaluate for subclavian stenosis or aortic coarctation

### 1.4.2 Initial Evaluation

| Component | Details |
|-----------|---------|
| **History** | Duration of HTN, prior readings, symptoms, family history, diet (sodium intake), physical activity, alcohol consumption, smoking, sleep history (snoring, witnessed apneas), medication adherence, all current medications (including OTC and supplements) |
| **Physical Exam** | Fundoscopic exam (arteriolar narrowing, AV nicking, hemorrhages, cotton wool spots, papilledema = grade IV), thyroid examination, cardiac auscultation (S4 gallop suggests LVH), abdominal bruits (renal artery stenosis), peripheral pulses (radio-femoral delay = coarctation), lower extremity edema |
| **Laboratory** | BMP (creatinine, eGFR, electrolytes), fasting glucose/HbA1c, lipid panel, TSH, urinalysis (protein; if positive, obtain albumin-to-creatinine ratio) |
| **Electrocardiogram** | Assess for LVH (Sokolow-Lyon criteria: S in V1 + R in V5 or V6 >35 mm), prior MI, arrhythmias |
| **Additional Testing** | Echocardiogram if concern for LV dysfunction, LVH, or valvular disease; ABPM for white coat/masked HTN evaluation; sleep study if OSA suspected; albumin-to-creatinine ratio if CKD or diabetes |

### 1.4.3 Secondary Hypertension Workup

> **Red Flag:** Secondary hypertension should be suspected when any of the following are present:
> - Onset <30 years without risk factors or obesity
> - Abrupt onset or worsening of previously controlled HTN
> - Resistant hypertension (>=3 drugs including a diuretic at maximally tolerated doses)
> - Target organ damage disproportionate to HTN duration
> - Hypokalemia with or without metabolic alkalosis (primary aldosteronism)
> - Abdominal bruit (renal artery stenosis)
> - Elevated creatinine after ACE-I/ARB initiation (bilateral renal artery stenosis)
> - Family history of kidney disease or early cerebrovascular accident
> - Episodic headache, sweating, and palpitations (pheochromocytoma)

**Common Secondary Causes:**

| Cause | Prevalence | Key Clues | Diagnostic Test |
|-------|-----------|-----------|-----------------|
| Primary aldosteronism | 5-10% of resistant HTN; ~2% of all HTN | Hypokalemia, metabolic alkalosis, muscle weakness | Aldosterone-to-renin ratio (ARR) >20 ng/dL per ng/mL/hr (confirm with salt loading or fludrocortisone suppression test) |
| Renal artery stenosis | 1-5% | Abdominal bruit, flash pulmonary edema, unexplained kidney dysfunction, elevated creatinine with ACE-I/ARB | CTA/MRA renal arteries, duplex ultrasound (peak systolic velocity >180 cm/s or RAR >3.5) |
| Chronic kidney disease | Common (HTN both cause and consequence) | Elevated creatinine, proteinuria, anemia, metabolic acidosis | eGFR, urinalysis, renal ultrasound |
| Obstructive sleep apnea | Very common (50% of resistant HTN) | Snoring, daytime somnolence, obesity, resistant HTN | Polysomnography or home sleep apnea test |
| Pheochromocytoma | Rare (<0.2%) | Episodic headache, sweating, palpitations, tremor, anxiety, pallor | 24-hour urine or plasma metanephrines (sensitivity >95%) |
| Cushing syndrome | Rare | Central obesity, purple striae, proximal myopathy, easy bruising, hirsutism | 1 mg overnight dexamethasone suppression test (cortisol >1.8 mcg/dL = abnormal) |
| Coarctation of aorta | Rare (young patients) | Radio-femoral delay, arm-leg BP discrepancy >20 mmHg, systolic murmur posteriorly | CTA chest, echocardiography, MRA |
| Thyroid disease | Common (hyperthyroidism and hypothyroidism) | Hyperthyroidism: tachycardia, tremor, weight loss, heat intolerance; Hypothyroidism: bradycardia, weight gain, cold intolerance, diastolic HTN | TSH, free T4 |
| Drug-induced | Common | Review all medications and supplements | Medication history |

**Common Culprit Drugs:** NSAIDs (inhibit prostaglandins), oral contraceptives (estrogen activates RAAS), systemic corticosteroids (mineralocorticoid effect), decongestants (pseudoephedrine, phenylephrine are sympathomimetics), stimulants (amphetamines, cocaine), erythropoietin (increases blood viscosity and vascular resistance), calcineurin inhibitors (cyclosporine, tacrolimus cause vasoconstriction and sodium retention), licorice (mineralocorticoid effect), herbal supplements (ephedra, yohimbine).

## 1.5 Management

### 1.5.1 Lifestyle Modifications (All Patients) [^1^]

| Modification | Recommendation | Expected BP Reduction |
|-------------|----------------|---------------------|
| **Weight loss** | Achieve/maintain ideal body weight (BMI 18.5-24.9); aim for 1-2 lb/week loss through caloric deficit | 5-20 mmHg per 10 kg lost |
| **DASH diet** | Emphasize fruits (4-5 servings/day), vegetables (4-5 servings/day), whole grains, low-fat dairy (2-3 servings/day); reduce saturated fat (<6% of calories) and cholesterol (<150 mg/day) | 8-14 mmHg |
| **Sodium restriction** | <2,300 mg/day (ideally <1,500 mg/day); gradual reduction to improve adherence | 2-8 mmHg |
| **Physical activity** | >=150 min/week moderate-intensity aerobic activity (e.g., brisk walking, cycling, swimming) or 75 min/week vigorous; add resistance training 2x/week | 4-9 mmHg |
| **Alcohol moderation** | Men: <=2 standard drinks/day; Women: <=1 drink/day; 1 standard drink = 14 g alcohol | 2-4 mmHg |
| **Smoking cessation** | Absolute requirement; combine behavioral counseling with pharmacotherapy (varenicline, bupropion, NRT); provides immediate CVD risk reduction | Immediate CVD risk reduction |
| **Potassium intake** | 3,500-5,000 mg/day through diet (fruits, vegetables, legumes) if no CKD Stage 3b-5 or hyperkalemia risk | 4-5 mmHg |
| **Stress reduction** | Meditation, yoga, biofeedback, cognitive behavioral therapy, adequate sleep (7-9 hours) | Variable (2-5 mmHg) |

> **Note:** Combined lifestyle modifications can achieve BP reductions comparable to single-drug therapy (10-20 mmHg), making them essential for all patients regardless of pharmacologic treatment needs.

### 1.5.2 Pharmacologic Treatment [^1^] [^23^]

**Treatment Thresholds:**
- **Stage 1 HTN (130-139/80-89):** Initiate pharmacologic therapy if clinical CVD or 10-year ASCVD risk >=10%. If ASCVD risk <10%, start with lifestyle modification for 3-6 months, then reassess.
- **Stage 2 HTN (>=140/90):** Initiate pharmacologic therapy plus lifestyle modification (two first-line agents of different classes preferred for Stage 2).
- **Target BP:** <130/80 mmHg for most adults (if tolerated). For adults >=65 years in the community, SBP target <130 mmHg is reasonable if tolerated.

**First-Line Antihypertensive Agents:**

| Class | Examples | Starting Dose | Target Dose | Key Considerations | Contraindications |
|-------|----------|--------------|-------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| **Thiazide diuretics** | Chlorthalidone 12.5-25 mg daily; HCTZ 12.5-25 mg daily; Indapamide 1.25 mg daily | 12.5 mg | 25-50 mg | Chlorthalidone preferred (longer half-life, better outcomes in ALLHAT); monitor K+, creatinine, glucose, uric acid | Severe electrolyte disturbances, severe hyponatremia |
| **ACE inhibitors** | Lisinopril 5-10 mg daily; Enalapril 5 mg BID | 5 mg | 10-40 mg | First-line with CKD, diabetes; check creatinine and K+ at 1-2 weeks; cough in 5-10%; angioedema in 0.1-0.7% | Pregnancy, bilateral RAS, history of angioedema |
| **ARBs** | Losartan 25-50 mg daily; Valsartan 40-80 mg daily; Olmesartan 20 mg daily | 25 mg | 50-150 mg | Alternative to ACE-I if cough; similar renal protective effects; direct vasodilation of efferent arteriole | Pregnancy, bilateral RAS |
| **Dihydropyridine CCBs** | Amlodipine 2.5-5 mg daily; Nifedipine ER 30 mg daily | 2.5 mg | 5-10 mg | Effective in Black patients; peripheral edema common (dose-dependent); no metabolic adverse effects | HFrEF (relative; may worsen edema) |
| **Non-DHP CCBs** | Diltiazem ER 120-240 mg daily; Verapamil ER 120-240 mg daily | 120 mg | 240-480 mg | Useful with atrial arrhythmias (rate control); negative inotrope and chronotrope | HFrEF, severe bradycardia (<50 bpm), 2nd/3rd degree AV block |

**Compelling Indications (Guideline-Directed Specific Agents):**

| Condition | Recommended Agents | Evidence |
|-----------|-------------------|----------|
| HFrEF | ACE-I/ARB/ARNI + beta-blocker + MRA + SGLT2i | Class I, Level A (2022 ACC/AHA/HFSA) |
| Post-MI | ACE-I + beta-blocker + MRA + high-intensity statin | Class I, Level A |
| CKD with albuminuria | ACE-I or ARB (maximally tolerated dose) | Class I, Level A |
| Diabetes | ACE-I or ARB preferred; add CCB or thiazide as needed | Class I, Level A |
| Coronary artery disease | Beta-blocker + ACE-I + high-intensity statin | Class I, Level A |
| Recurrent stroke prevention | Thiazide + ACE-I (PROGRESS trial) | Class I, Level A |
| Black patients (without CKD/CVD) | Thiazide diuretic or CCB preferred as initial agent | ALLHAT trial demonstrated superiority |

**Combination Therapy:**
- Most patients will require >=2 agents to reach BP target (approximately 75% of hypertensive adults need multiple drugs).
- **Preferred combinations:** ACE-I/ARB + CCB or ACE-I/ARB + thiazide diuretic.
- Single-pill combinations improve adherence by 20-30%.
- **Avoid:** ACE-I + ARB combination (increased adverse events including hyperkalemia and AKI; no outcome benefit per ALTITUDE and VA NEPHRON-D trials).
- If three drugs needed: ACE-I/ARB + CCB + thiazide is preferred triple combination.
- Consider loop diuretics (furosemide) instead of thiazides if eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73m2 (thiazides are ineffective at low GFR).

### 1.5.3 Resistant Hypertension

**Definition:** Blood pressure remains >=130/80 mmHg despite concurrent use of 3 antihypertensive agents of different classes at maximally tolerated doses, one of which is a diuretic. OR controlled BP requiring >=4 agents.

**Systematic Approach:**

1. **Confirm adherence:** Non-adherence is the most common cause of apparent resistant HTN (affects 30-50% of apparent resistant cases). Use pharmacy refill data, direct questioning, and urine/plasma drug levels if available.
2. **Confirm proper BP measurement technique:** Use correct cuff size, position, adequate rest period; consider out-of-office BP monitoring to exclude white coat effect.
3. **Screen for secondary causes:** Primary aldosteronism is the most common (screen with ARR), followed by renal artery stenosis and OSA.
4. **Optimize current regimen:** Ensure diuretic is at adequate dose; switch from HCTZ to chlorthalidone (12.5-25 mg) or indapamide (1.25 mg) if needed; consider using a loop diuretic if eGFR <45.
5. **Add spironolactone:** PATHWAY-2 trial demonstrated spironolactone (25-50 mg daily) is the most effective 4th-line agent (superior to bisoprolol and doxazosin). Monitor K+ and creatinine at 1 week, 1 month, then quarterly.
6. **Other 4th-line options:** Beta-blockers (if not already used), alpha-blockers (doxazosin 1-8 mg daily), central alpha-2 agonists (clonidine 0.1-0.3 mg BID or patch), or direct vasodilators (hydralazine 25-100 mg TID-QID, minoxidil 2.5-40 mg daily - last resort due to hirsutism and fluid retention).
7. **Referral:** To hypertension specialist or nephrologist for confirmed resistant HTN.
8. **Renal denervation:** Consider for select patients with confirmed resistant HTN despite optimal medical therapy (SYMPLICITY HTN-3 and subsequent trials show modest benefit of approximately 5-10 mmHg reduction).

## 1.6 Special Populations

| Population | Considerations | Preferred Agents | Target BP |
|------------|---------------|------------------|-----------|
| **Pregnancy** | Hypertensive disorders complicate 5-10% of pregnancies; monitor for preeclampsia (new-onset HTN + proteinuria + end-organ dysfunction after 20 weeks) | Labetalol, nifedipine ER, methyldopa (safest evidence base) | <140/90 mmHg (acute); <140/90 mmHg (chronic) |
| | **Contraindicated:** ACE-I, ARB, DRI (direct renin inhibitor) - teratogenic (renal agenesis, oligohydramnios, skull hypoplasia) | | |
| **Children/Adolescents** | BP >=95th percentile for age/sex/height; use pediatric cuffs; evaluate for secondary causes (coarctation, renal disease, endocrine) | ACE-I, ARB, CCB, thiazide | <90th percentile for age/sex/height |
| **Elderly (>65)** | Target <130 mmHg SBP if tolerated; increased risk of orthostatic hypotension; "start low, go slow" principle; monitor for falls | Any first-line; avoid orthostatic hypotension; consider deprescribing if symptomatic | <130/80 (if tolerated); avoid SBP <120 |
| **Black adults** | Higher prevalence (54%), earlier onset, greater severity, higher risk of CKD and stroke; thiazide or CCB preferred initial agents; ACE-I/ARB less effective as monotherapy due to lower renin levels | Thiazide, CCB; combination therapy often needed | <130/80 |
| **CKD (eGFR <60)** | ACE-I or ARB if albuminuria (reduces progression); loop diuretics preferred if eGFR <30 (thiazides ineffective); watch for hyperkalemia | ACE-I/ARB (albuminuria), loop diuretics | <130/80 |
| **Diabetes** | ACE-I or ARB preferred (renoprotective); intensive BP control reduces microvascular complications; SGLT2i and GLP-1 RA provide additional CV benefit | ACE-I/ARB + CCB or thiazide | <130/80 |
| **Post-stroke** | Gradual BP reduction over days to weeks; avoid precipitous drops (cerebral autoregulation is impaired); benefit established for secondary prevention | Thiazide + ACE-I (PROGRESS trial) | <130/80 |
| **Coronary artery disease** | Beta-blocker + ACE-I/ARB; avoid excessive DBP lowering (<60 mmHg may compromise coronary perfusion) | Beta-blocker + ACE-I + CCB or thiazide | <130/80 |

## 1.7 Red Flags / Emergency Features

> **CRITICAL: Hypertensive Emergency vs. Urgency**
>
> **Hypertensive Emergency** (BP >=180/120 mmHg WITH acute target organ damage): This is a medical emergency requiring immediate IV therapy and intensive care admission. Acute target organ damage includes:
> - Hypertensive encephalopathy (altered mental status, headache, seizures, visual changes, papilledema)
> - Acute stroke (ischemic or hemorrhagic)
> - Acute coronary syndrome
> - Acute aortic dissection
> - Acute pulmonary edema
> - Acute kidney injury (acute tubular necrosis from malignant HTN)
> - Eclampsia/preeclampsia with severe features
> - Pheochromocytoma crisis
> - Microangiopathic hemolytic anemia (schistocytes on peripheral smear)
>
> **Hypertensive Urgency** (BP >=180/120 mmHg WITHOUT target organ damage): Not a true emergency. Do NOT acutely lower BP in the ED or outpatient setting. Gradual reduction over 24-48 hours with oral agents. Rapid reduction risks cerebral, coronary, and renal hypoperfusion.

**Hypertensive Emergency Treatment Principles:**
- Reduce mean arterial pressure (MAP) by no more than 10-20% in the first hour
- Reduce to approximately 160/100-110 mmHg over the next 2-6 hours
- Normal BP should be achieved over days, not hours
- Avoid precipitous drops (risk of cerebral, coronary, and renal hypoperfusion)

| Agent | Dosing | Indications | Cautions |
|-------|--------|-------------|----------|
| **Nicardipine** | 5 mg/hr IV infusion, titrate by 2.5 mg/hr q5min; max 15 mg/hr | Most hypertensive emergencies (except acute HF) | Tachycardia, headache, flushing; requires arterial line for titration |
| **Clevidipine** | 1-2 mg/hr IV, double q90sec until effect; max 32 mg/hr | Perioperative HTN, aortic dissection, when rapid titration needed | Contraindicated with soy/egg allergy, lipid disorders; requires line changes q12h |
| **Nitroprusside** | 0.3-10 mcg/kg/min IV (start 0.25-0.5 mcg/kg/min) | Aortic dissection, acute pulmonary edema, perioperative HTN | Cyanide/thiocyanate toxicity with prolonged use (>48-72h) or high doses; light-sensitive |
| **Labetalol** | 20 mg IV over 2 min, then 20-80 mg q10min OR 1-2 mg/min infusion | Most emergencies; preferred in pregnancy; perioperative | Bronchospasm (non-selective beta-blockade), bradycardia, heart block; avoid in acute decompensated HF |
| **Esmolol** | 500 mcg/kg IV bolus, then 50-200 mcg/kg/min infusion | Aortic dissection, perioperative HTN; when ultra-short acting needed | Very short half-life (9 min); bradycardia; avoid in HF |
| **Hydralazine** | 10-20 mg IV q4-6h (IM not recommended - unpredictable absorption) | Eclampsia/preeclampsia (with magnesium sulfate) | Reflex tachycardia; unpredictable BP response; less preferred |
| **Fenoldopam** | 0.1-0.3 mcg/kg/min IV | HTN emergency with renal impairment (dopamine-1 agonist - preserves renal blood flow) | Tachycardia, headache, glaucoma (increases intraocular pressure) |

**Condition-Specific IV Agents:**
- **Aortic dissection:** Beta-blockade first (labetalol or esmolol) + vasodilator (nitroprusside or nicardipine) - NEVER use vasodilator alone (increases shear stress on aorta)
- **Acute pulmonary edema:** Nitroglycerin (vasodilation + preload reduction) + loop diuretics; avoid hydralazine
- **Acute ischemic stroke:** Permissive hypertension (SBP up to 220 mmHg for first 48-72 hours unless IV thrombolysis or endovascular therapy planned); if thrombolysis candidate, lower to <185 mmHg systolic
- **Intracerebral hemorrhage:** SBP target 140-160 mmHg (systolic reduction to 140 mmHg is safe and may improve functional outcomes per ATACH-2 trial)
- **Preeclampsia/eclampsia:** Labetalol, hydralazine, or nifedipine; magnesium sulfate for seizure prophylaxis; definitive treatment is delivery

## 1.8 Follow-up and Monitoring

| BP Severity | Initial Follow-up | Evaluation |
|-------------|-------------------|------------|
| Elevated (120-129/<80) | 3-6 months | Lifestyle counseling; reassess need for pharmacotherapy based on ASCVD risk |
| Stage 1 HTN (130-139/80-89) | 1 month after initiating therapy; then every 3-6 months | Check adherence, side effects, home BP readings |
| Stage 2 HTN (>=140/90) | 2-4 weeks after initiating therapy; then every 1-3 months | More frequent monitoring until controlled |
| Resistant HTN | Every 2-4 weeks until controlled; then every 1-3 months | Laboratory monitoring, side effect assessment |

**Laboratory Monitoring:**
- BMP (K+, creatinine) 1-2 weeks after starting ACE-I/ARB or diuretic, then every 3-6 months
- Annual lipid panel, HbA1c, urinalysis
- Consider ABPM if office-home BP discrepancy suspected
- Review medication adherence at every visit

## 1.9 When to Seek Emergency Care

Seek emergency medical attention immediately if:
- Blood pressure >=180/120 mmHg accompanied by symptoms (headache, chest pain, shortness of breath, visual changes, confusion, seizures, severe back pain)
- Blood pressure >=220/140 mmHg regardless of symptoms
- Symptoms of stroke (sudden weakness, difficulty speaking, facial droop)
- Symptoms of heart attack (chest pressure, radiation to arm/jaw, diaphoresis, nausea)
- Pregnancy with blood pressure >=160/110 or symptoms of preeclampsia (severe headache, visual changes, right upper quadrant pain, swelling)

# Chapter 2: Heart Failure

## 2.1 Overview

Heart failure (HF) is a clinical syndrome characterized by structural or functional impairment of ventricular filling or ejection of blood, resulting in symptoms of dyspnea, fatigue, and fluid retention (peripheral edema, pulmonary congestion). It affects approximately 6.5 million Americans aged >=20 years, with prevalence projected to increase 46% by 2030. HF is the leading cause of hospitalization in adults over 65 and carries a significant mortality burden (approximately 50% 5-year mortality in HFrEF without modern therapy) [^19^] [^28^].

**Pathophysiology (Brief):** In HFrEF, myocardial damage (ischemic, toxic, genetic, infectious) leads to reduced contractility, activating compensatory neurohormonal pathways including the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) and sympathetic nervous system (SNS). Chronic activation causes adverse ventricular remodeling (dilation, fibrosis, wall thinning), progressive pump dysfunction, and ultimately end-stage disease. In HFpEF, the primary problem is impaired relaxation and increased stiffness of the left ventricle, often in the setting of hypertension, obesity, diabetes, and aging. Inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and microvascular disease also play important roles in HFpEF.

## 2.2 Classification

### 2.2.1 By Ejection Fraction (Universal Classification) [^19^]

| Classification | LVEF | Description | Prevalence | Key Therapies |
|---------------|------|-------------|------------|--------------|
| **HFrEF** (Heart Failure with reduced Ejection Fraction) | <=40% | Impaired systolic function; myocardial damage leads to decreased contractility | ~50% of HF cases | Quadruple GDMT (ARNI, beta-blocker, MRA, SGLT2i) |
| **HFmrEF** (Heart Failure with mildly reduced Ejection Fraction) | 41-49% | Mildly impaired systolic function; emerging evidence for some HFrEF therapies | ~15-25% of HF cases | SGLT2i; consider ARNI, beta-blocker, MRA |
| **HFpEF** (Heart Failure with preserved Ejection Fraction) | >=50% | Preserved systolic function with impaired relaxation and increased stiffness | ~30-50% of HF cases | SGLT2i; diuretics; comorbidity management |
| **HFimpEF** (Heart Failure with improved Ejection Fraction) | >40% (improved from <=40%) | Previously HFrEF with recovered LVEF | Increasing with GDMT | Continue GDMT (may decompensate if stopped) |

### 2.2.2 NYHA Functional Classification

| Class | Description | Prognostic Significance | Approximate 1-Year Mortality (Untreated HFrEF) |
|-------|-------------|------------------------|-----------------------------------------------|
| **I** | No limitation of physical activity. Ordinary activity does not cause symptoms. | Best prognosis | 5-10% |
| **II** | Slight limitation. Comfortable at rest. Ordinary physical activity causes fatigue, dyspnea, palpitations, or angina. | Moderate prognosis | 10-20% |
| **III** | Marked limitation. Comfortable at rest. Less than ordinary activity causes symptoms. | Poor prognosis | 20-30% |
| **IV** | Unable to carry out any physical activity without symptoms. Symptoms may be present even at rest. | Worst prognosis; highest mortality | 30-50% |

> **Correlation with Objective Measures:**
> - NYHA II roughly corresponds to peak VO2 16-20 mL/kg/min (6-minute walk distance 300-450 m)
> - NYHA III roughly corresponds to peak VO2 10-16 mL/kg/min (6-minute walk distance 150-300 m)
> - NYHA IV roughly corresponds to peak VO2 <10 mL/kg/min (6-minute walk distance <150 m)

### 2.2.3 ACC/AHA Stages of Heart Failure

| Stage | Description | Example Population | Intervention |
|-------|-------------|-------------------|-------------|
| **A** | At high risk for HF but without structural heart disease or symptoms | HTN, CAD, diabetes, obesity, family history of cardiomyopathy, history of cardiotoxic chemotherapy | Risk factor modification |
| **B** | Structural heart disease but without signs or symptoms of HF | Prior MI, LVH, low EF, valvular disease | Prevent progression (GDMT if indicated) |
| **C** | Structural heart disease with prior or current symptoms of HF | Known HF with dyspnea, fatigue, edema | Guideline-directed management |
| **D** | Refractory HF requiring specialized interventions | Inotrope-dependent, recurrent hospitalizations, candidate for transplant/VAD | Advanced therapies, palliative care |

## 2.3 Guideline-Directed Medical Therapy (GDMT) for HFrEF

The cornerstone of HFrEF management is **quadruple therapy** consisting of four foundational drug classes, each independently reducing mortality and morbidity. Current guidelines recommend rapid simultaneous initiation of all four therapies, rather than the historical sequential approach, which delayed therapeutic benefit by months to years [^19^] [^28^].

### 2.3.1 The Four Pillars of HFrEF Therapy

#### Pillar 1: ARNI (Angiotensin Receptor-Neprilysin Inhibitor)
- **Agent:** Sacubitril/Valsartan (Entresto)
- **Mechanism:** Combined angiotensin receptor blockade and neprilysin inhibition (increases natriuretic peptides, bradykinin, adrenomedullin while blocking angiotensin II)
- **Dosing:** Start 24/26 mg or 49/51 mg BID (based on BP and volume status); target 97/103 mg BID
- **Evidence:** PARADIGM-HF trial (2014) demonstrated 20% reduction in cardiovascular death and 21% reduction in first HF hospitalization versus enalapril; NNT ~21 for CV death over 27 months
- **Contraindications:** Concomitant ACE-I (48-hour washout required between stopping ACE-I and starting ARNI), history of angioedema with ACE-I or ARB, aliskiren use with diabetes, pregnancy
- **Monitoring:** Blood pressure, potassium, creatinine at baseline, 1-2 weeks, then monthly during titration
- **Side effects:** Hypotension (most common - manage by adjusting diuretics first), hyperkalemia, angioedema (lower risk than ACE-I), cough
- **Class I recommendation, Level A evidence**

#### Pillar 2: Evidence-Based Beta-Blocker
- **Agents:** Carvedilol, Metoprolol succinate (Toprol XL), Bisoprolol (the only three with proven mortality benefit in HFrEF)
- **Mechanism:** Block sympathetic overactivation, reduce heart rate, improve LV filling, prevent arrhythmias, reverse remodeling
- **Dosing (target doses):**
  - Carvedilol: Start 3.125 mg BID; target 25 mg BID (50 mg BID if weight >85 kg)
  - Metoprolol succinate (NOT tartrate): Start 25 mg daily; target 200 mg daily
  - Bisoprolol: Start 1.25 mg daily; target 10 mg daily
- **Evidence:** COPERNICUS (carvedilol), MERIT-HF (metoprolol succinate), CIBIS-II (bisoprolol) collectively demonstrated 34% reduction in all-cause mortality (NNT ~20-30)
- **Initiation:** Start low, titrate every 2 weeks to target as tolerated (watch for fatigue, dizziness, worsening fluid retention, bradycardia)
- **Contraindications:** Severe bradycardia (<50 bpm), decompensated HF requiring IV inotropes, severe asthma/COPD, 2nd/3rd degree AV block without pacemaker, cardiogenic shock
- **Class I recommendation, Level A evidence**

#### Pillar 3: MRA (Mineralocorticoid Receptor Antagonist)
- **Agents:** Spironolactone, Eplerenone
- **Mechanism:** Block aldosterone effects (myocardial fibrosis, endothelial dysfunction, vascular remodeling, arrhythmogenesis, potassium wasting)
- **Dosing:**
  - Spironolactone: Start 12.5-25 mg daily; target 25-50 mg daily
  - Eplerenone: Start 25 mg daily; target 50 mg daily (more selective; fewer sexual side effects but less potent)
- **Evidence:** RALES (spironolactone in severe HFrEF, 1999), EPHESUS (eplerenone post-MI, 2003) collectively demonstrated 30% reduction in all-cause mortality (NNT ~30)
- **Monitoring:** Potassium and creatinine at 1 week, 1 month, then every 3 months (critical - hyperkalemia can be life-threatening)
- **Contraindications:** K+ >5.0 mEq/L, eGFR <30 mL/min/1.73m2, severe hyperkalemia risk
- **Side effects:** Hyperkalemia, gynecomastia/breast tenderness (spironolactone), menstrual irregularities, metabolic acidosis
- **Class I recommendation, Level A evidence**

#### Pillar 4: SGLT2 Inhibitor
- **Agents:** Dapagliflozin 10 mg daily or Empagliflozin 10 mg daily (both once daily, regardless of meals)
- **Mechanism:** Sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 inhibition in proximal tubule (natriuresis, osmotic diuresis, improved cardiac energetics, reduced inflammation, reverse remodeling)
- **Evidence:** DAPA-HF (2019), EMPEROR-Reduced (2020) trials demonstrated significant reduction in CV death and HF hospitalization; benefits independent of diabetes status
- **Added as Class I recommendation in 2022 ACC/AHA/HFSA guidelines** [^19^]
- **Class I for HFrEF; Class IIa for HFpEF and HFmrEF**
- **Additional benefits:** Renal protection (reduces eGFR decline), modest weight loss, modest BP reduction, reduced risk of gout
- **Side effects:** Genital mycotic infections (higher in uncircumcised males and females with prior infections), volume depletion, rare euglycemic DKA (hold during acute illness/surgery)
- **Monitoring:** Volume status, renal function at baseline and during acute illness; hold if patient is fasting for surgery or has acute severe illness
- **Class I recommendation, Level A evidence**

### 2.3.2 Quadruple Therapy Initiation Strategy

Current evidence supports **rapid simultaneous initiation** of all four foundational therapies [^28^]:

1. **Initiate low doses of all 4 drug classes within 1 week of diagnosis** (do not wait for sequential titration)
2. **Titrate doses every 1-2 weeks** based on tolerance (vital signs, electrolytes, symptoms, renal function)
3. **Target full guideline-recommended doses within 1 month** if tolerated
4. For hospitalized patients: start GDMT before discharge if hemodynamically stable (no hypotension, no acute kidney injury requiring diuretics)
5. Estimated relative risk reduction in all-cause mortality with quadruple therapy: up to 73% versus no therapy; NNT approximately 5-7 for prevention of one death over 2 years

**Practical Initiation Protocol:**
- Day 1: Start carvedilol 3.125 mg BID + spironolactone 12.5 mg daily + dapagliflozin 10 mg daily
- Day 2-3: If BP tolerates (SBP >90 mmHg), add sacubitril/valsartan 24/26 mg BID
- Week 2: Titrate carvedilol to 6.25 mg BID if HR >60 and SBP >90
- Week 3: Increase spironolactone to 25 mg daily if K+ <5.0
- Week 4: Titrate sacubitril/valsartan to 49/51 mg BID or 97/103 mg BID based on BP
- Continue titrating beta-blocker every 2 weeks toward target

## 2.4 Heart Failure with Preserved Ejection Fraction (HFpEF)

### 2.4.1 Diagnostic Criteria (H2FPEF Score)

| Component | Points | Rationale |
|-----------|--------|-----------|
| Heavy (BMI >30 kg/m2) | 2 points | Obesity is strongly associated with HFpEF |
| Hypertensive (>=2 antihypertensive medications) | 1 point | HTN is the strongest risk factor for HFpEF |
| Atrial fibrillation (paroxysmal or persistent) | 3 points | AF and HFpEF frequently coexist and exacerbate each other |
| Pulmonary hypertension (PASP >35 mmHg) | 1 point | Elevated filling pressures cause pulmonary venous HTN |
| Elderly (>60 years) | 1 point | HFpEF prevalence increases dramatically with age |
| Filling pressure (E/e' >=9) | 1 point | Echocardiographic marker of elevated LA pressure |

- **Score 0-1:** Low probability of HFpEF (consider alternative causes of dyspnea)
- **Score 2-5:** Intermediate probability (consider exercise stress testing, invasive hemodynamics)
- **Score 6-9:** High probability of HFpEF (initiate treatment)

### 2.4.2 Management [^19^]

| Intervention | Evidence | Recommendation | Notes |
|-------------|----------|----------------|-------|
| **SGLT2 inhibitors** (dapagliflozin or empagliflozin) | EMPEROR-Preserved (2021), DELIVER (2022) trials | Class IIa, Level B | First therapy to demonstrate robust benefit in HFpEF; reduces HF hospitalizations and CV death; benefits across EF spectrum |
| **Diuretics** | Symptom relief | Class I (symptoms) | Loop diuretics for congestion; lowest effective dose |
| **Blood pressure control** | Multiple observational studies | Class I | Target <130/80 mmHg; ARNI may be particularly beneficial |
| **ARNI** (sacubitril/valsartan) | PARAGON-HF (2019) - overall neutral but benefit in EF <57 and women | Class IIb | Consider in patients with EF <57% or women |
| **Comorbidity management** | Essential component | Class I | AF (rate/rhythm control, anticoagulation), diabetes (SGLT2i/GLP-1 RA), obesity (weight loss), OSA (CPAP), CKD |
| **Exercise training** | Multiple RCTs | Class I | Improves exercise capacity and quality of life |

> **Key Point:** Unlike HFrEF, no single therapy (other than SGLT2i) has demonstrated robust mortality benefit in HFpEF. Management focuses on symptom relief, comorbidity optimization, SGLT2i therapy, and exercise. ACE-I, ARB, and beta-blockers have NOT shown mortality benefit in HFpEF trials (CHARM-Preserved, PEP-CHF, I-PRESERVE, TOPCAT), though they remain useful for BP control and comorbidities.

## 2.5 Acute Decompensated Heart Failure (ADHF) [^85^] [^86^]

ADHF accounts for over 1 million hospitalizations annually in the US and is the most common cause of hospitalization in Medicare beneficiaries. Post-discharge mortality and readmission rates remain high (10-15% mortality, 25-30% readmission at 30 days).

### 2.5.1 Clinical Assessment: The "Warm/Cold, Wet/Dry" Framework

The warm/cold, wet/dry framework classifies patients based on perfusion status and volume status, guiding acute management:

| Profile | Perfusion | Volume | Clinical Features | Initial Management |
|---------|-----------|--------|-------------------|-------------------|
| **Warm and Dry** | Adequate (warm extremities, normal mentation, adequate urine output) | Euvolemic (no JVD, no pulmonary congestion, no peripheral edema) | Stable; may have exertional symptoms only | Continue outpatient GDMT; no diuretics needed |
| **Warm and Wet** | Adequate | Overloaded (JVD, pulmonary crackles, peripheral edema, elevated JVP) | Most common ADHF presentation | IV loop diuretics; may add vasodilators; continue GDMT |
| **Cold and Dry** | Inadequate (cool extremities, decreased urine output, narrow pulse pressure) | Euvolemic | Low cardiac output without congestion | Inotropes if needed; evaluate for advanced therapies; do NOT diurese |
| **Cold and Wet** | Inadequate | Overloaded | Cardiogenic shock + congestion; highest mortality | Inotropes + careful diuresis; consider mechanical circulatory support, transplant evaluation |

### 2.5.2 Management

1. **IV Loop Diuretics:** First-line for all patients with fluid overload
   - Furosemide: 20-160 mg IV (use 2.5x daily oral dose as initial IV dose)
   - If inadequate response: double dose, switch to continuous infusion, or add thiazide-type diuretic (metolazone 2.5-5 mg PO daily - watch for severe electrolyte depletion)
   - Monitor urine output, weight, electrolytes (K+, Mg2+) daily

2. **Vasodilators:** Nitroglycerin (10-200 mcg/min IV) or nitroprusside (0.3-10 mcg/kg/min) for afterload reduction if SBP >110 mmHg and patient is not on PDE-5 inhibitors
   - Contraindicated in severe aortic stenosis, HOCM, SBP <90 mmHg
   - Start low, titrate to effect

3. **Non-Invasive Positive Pressure Ventilation (NIPPV):** BiPAP or CPAP for acute cardiogenic pulmonary edema
   - Reduces work of breathing, improves oxygenation, reduces afterload (increases intrathoracic pressure)
   - Reduces intubation rate by ~50%
   - Start CPAP 10 cm H2O or BiPAP IPAP 10/EPAP 5, titrate to effect

4. **Inotropes:** For cold profiles (cardiogenic shock)
   - Dobutamine: 2-20 mcg/kg/min (beta-1 agonist; increases contractility and heart rate)
   - Milrinone: 0.125-0.75 mcg/kg/min (phosphodiesterase-3 inhibitor; reduces afterload; useful if on beta-blockers but renally cleared)
   - Both increase arrhythmia risk and mortality when used chronically; reserve for short-term bridge

5. **Vasopressors:** Norepinephrine if SBP <90 mmHg despite adequate filling (add to inotropes)

6. **Acetazolamide:** 500-1000 mg IV daily added to loop diuretics may improve decongestion (ADVOR trial showed increased diuresis and natriuresis) [^86^]

7. **Early SGLT2i initiation** during hospitalization may facilitate decongestion and allow earlier GDMT optimization (DICTATE-AHF trial and SOLOIST-WHF trial) [^86^]

### 2.5.3 Discharge Planning (Critical for Reducing Readmissions)

- Continue/optimize all 4 GDMT pillars before discharge (do not wait for outpatient follow-up)
- Early follow-up within 7 days (telephone or clinic visit) - this single intervention reduces 30-day readmissions by 20-30%
- Cardiology follow-up within 2 weeks
- Daily weight monitoring (call provider if weight increases >2-3 lb in 1 day or >5 lb in 1 week)
- Sodium restriction (<2-3 g/day) and fluid restriction (1.5-2 L/day) if hyponatremic
- Medication reconciliation and adherence counseling
- Cardiac rehabilitation referral
- Consider wearable monitoring or remote patient monitoring programs

## 2.6 Red Flags

> **EMERGENCY INDICATORS - SEEK IMMEDIATE CARE:**
> - Acute pulmonary edema (frothy pink sputum, severe dyspnea at rest, oxygen saturation <90%, bilateral diffuse crackles)
> - Cardiogenic shock (cold clammy extremities, altered mental status, decreased urine output <30 mL/hr, hypotension SBP <90 mmHg, lactate elevation)
> - Syncope in HFrEF (suspect ventricular arrhythmia; check if patient has ICD and whether it discharged)
> - ICD shocks (call electrophysiology immediately; any single appropriate shock warrants evaluation; multiple shocks = emergency)
> - Refractory volume overload despite escalating IV diuretic doses (consider ultrafiltration, inotropes, advanced therapies)
> - New sustained ventricular arrhythmia (hemodynamically unstable VT, VF)
> - Acute kidney injury with rising creatinine in setting of aggressive diuresis (cardiorenal syndrome)
> - Acute severe MR (papillary muscle rupture post-MI): flash pulmonary edema, new holosystolic murmur, hemodynamic collapse

## 2.7 When to Refer

| Scenario | Timing | Type of Referral |
|----------|--------|-----------------|
| New diagnosis of HFrEF | Within 1-2 weeks | Cardiology |
| NYHA III-IV symptoms despite optimal GDMT | Urgent (within 1 week) | Advanced HF/cardiac transplant |
| Evaluation for ICD/CRT | After 3-6 months of optimal GDMT (unless post-MI, then 40 days) | Electrophysiology |
| Secondary MR evaluation | After optimization of GDMT | Structural interventional cardiology |
| HFpEF with diagnostic uncertainty | As needed | Cardiology with expertise in HFpEF |
| Recurrent ADHF (>=2 hospitalizations in 6 months) | Urgent | Palliative care consultation (in addition to cardiology) |

# Chapter 3: Coronary Artery Disease

## 3.1 Overview

Coronary artery disease (CAD) represents a continuum from asymptomatic atherosclerosis through chronic stable angina to acute coronary syndromes (ACS). The 2024 ESC Guidelines use the term "Chronic Coronary Syndrome" (CCS), while the 2023 ACC/AHA Guidelines use "Chronic Coronary Disease" (CCD), reflecting the evolving understanding that ischemic symptoms exist on a dynamic spectrum with multiple underlying mechanisms [^78^]. CAD remains the leading cause of death globally, responsible for approximately 17.9 million deaths annually (32% of all-cause mortality). In the United States, someone dies from cardiovascular disease every 33 seconds.

**Pathophysiology (Brief):** Atherosclerosis begins with endothelial dysfunction and subendothelial accumulation of lipoproteins, triggering a chronic inflammatory response. Macrophages engulf oxidized LDL, forming foam cells that coalesce into fatty streaks. Over years, these progress to fibrous plaques with a lipid-rich necrotic core covered by a fibrous cap. Acute coronary syndromes occur when plaque rupture or erosion triggers thrombus formation via platelet activation and coagulation cascade. The degree of coronary obstruction and resultant myocardial oxygen supply-demand mismatch determines the clinical presentation.

## 3.2 Clinical Presentations

**Chronic Coronary Syndrome (CCS) Populations:**
1. Patients with stable angina and suspected/confirmed obstructive CAD
2. Patients with new-onset heart failure or LV dysfunction of ischemic/cardiometabolic origin
3. Asymptomatic patients with known CAD (post-ACS, post-revascularization) in the non-acute phase
4. Patients with angina and coronary vasomotor abnormalities or microvascular angina (ANOCA/INOCA - angina with non-obstructive coronary arteries / ischemia with non-obstructive coronary arteries)
5. Asymptomatic patients with screening abnormalities suggesting CAD

## 3.3 Stable Angina vs. Acute Coronary Syndrome

| Feature | Stable Angina | ACS (NSTEMI/UA) | STEMI |
|---------|-------------|-----------------|-------|
| **Pain character** | Pressure, squeezing, heaviness, tightness | Prolonged pressure, often more severe; may be described as "crushing" | Crushing, "worst ever," "tearing," "like an elephant sitting on my chest" |
| **Typical duration** | 2-10 minutes | >20 minutes; may be persistent for hours | >30 minutes, typically unrelenting |
| **Triggers** | Predictable exertion, emotional stress, cold exposure, heavy meals | Variable; may occur at rest or with minimal exertion; often without clear trigger | Often spontaneous, no clear trigger |
| **Relief** | Rest or sublingual nitroglycerin within 2-5 minutes | Variable or no relief with rest/NTG | Minimal or no relief with NTG |
| **Associated symptoms** | Dyspnea, diaphoresis, nausea (less common) | Diaphoresis, nausea, vomiting, dyspnea, syncope, palpitations | Marked diaphoresis, nausea/vomiting, sense of impending doom, severe dyspnea |
| **ECG** | Normal or stable ST-T wave changes | ST depression >=0.5 mm, T-wave inversion >=1 mm, dynamic ST-T changes, pseudonormalization | ST elevation >=1 mm in 2+ contiguous leads (>=2 mm in V2-V3 for men <40, >=2.5 mm for men >=40, >=1.5 mm for women); new LBBB |
| **Troponin** | Normal | Elevated (NSTEMI) or normal (UA) | Markedly elevated with characteristic rise and fall |
| **Urgency** | Outpatient workup within days to weeks | Urgent evaluation within hours; hospital admission | Emergency; door-to-balloon <90 minutes |

### 3.3.1 Typical vs. Atypical Angina

| Feature | Typical (Definite) Angina | Atypical (Probable) Angina | Non-anginal Chest Pain |
|---------|--------------------------|---------------------------|----------------------|
| Substernal location | Yes | Yes or no | No |
| Provoked by exertion/emotion | Yes | Yes or no | No |
| Relieved by rest or NTG within minutes | Yes | Yes or no | No |
| Clinical significance | High likelihood of obstructive CAD | Intermediate likelihood | Low likelihood |

> **Important Note:** Women, elderly patients (especially >75 years), and patients with diabetes more commonly present with atypical or "angina equivalent" symptoms rather than classic substernal chest pain. These include:
> - Dyspnea on exertion (most common atypical presentation in women)
> - Fatigue, weakness
> - Nausea, epigastric discomfort, indigestion
> - Diaphoresis
> - Pain in the neck, jaw, back, or arm without chest pain
> - Syncope or near-syncope

## 3.4 Acute Coronary Syndrome Management

### 3.4.1 STEMI Protocol

**Goal: Door-to-balloon (D2B) time <90 minutes; Door-to-needle (D2N) time <30 minutes; First medical contact (FMC)-to-device time <120 minutes**

**Immediate Management (First 10 Minutes - "STEMI Alert"):**
1. **Aspirin 325 mg chewed** (if not already given by EMS; give immediately unless true aspirin allergy)
2. **P2Y12 inhibitor loading** (choice depends on strategy and patient factors):
   - Ticagrelor 180 mg PO (preferred; reversible; no genetic variability; used with both PCI and fibrinolysis)
   - Prasugrel 60 mg PO (if proceeding to PCI; most potent; AVOID if prior stroke/TIA, age >75, or weight <60 kg)
   - Clopidogrel 600 mg PO (if ticagrelor/prasugrel contraindicated or unavailable; less potent; genetic variability in metabolism)
3. **Anticoagulation:**
   - Unfractionated heparin 60 U/kg IV bolus (max 4,000 U) + 12 U/kg/hr infusion (max 1,000 U/hr), titrate to aPTT 50-70 seconds
   - Bivalirudin (alternative during PCI; especially if HIT risk)
   - Enoxaparin 1 mg/kg SC q12h (if fibrinolysis planned)
4. **High-intensity statin:** Atorvastatin 40-80 mg PO or rosuvastatin 20-40 mg PO (initiate as early as possible regardless of baseline LDL)
5. **Beta-blocker:** Metoprolol 25-50 mg PO q6h (unless contraindicated: hypotension, acute decompensated HF, heart block, cocaine use, severe reactive airway disease)
6. **Nitroglycerin:** 0.4 mg SL q5min x 3 for pain relief; IV NTG if ongoing pain or hypertension (contraindicated if SBP <90, RV infarct, PDE-5 inhibitor use within 24-48h)
7. **Supplemental oxygen:** Only if SpO2 <90%, respiratory distress, or high-risk features (routine oxygen not beneficial and may be harmful)

**Reperfusion Strategy:**
- **Primary PCI (percutaneous coronary intervention):** Gold standard; goal FMC-to-device <=90 minutes at PCI-capable center, <=120 minutes if transfer required
- **Fibrinolysis:** If PCI not available within 120 minutes of first medical contact; tenecteplase preferred (single weight-based IV bolus: 30 mg if <60 kg, 35 mg if 60-70 kg, 40 mg if 70-80 kg, 45 mg if 80-90 kg, 50 mg if >90 kg)
- **Rescue PCI:** If <50% ST resolution 60-90 minutes after fibrinolysis
- **Complete revascularization:** Routine staging of non-culprit lesions supported by COMPLETE trial (2019)

**Post-PCI Care:**
- DAPT duration: 12 months (aspirin 81 mg daily + P2Y12 inhibitor)
- Consider shortening to 3-6 months in high bleeding risk (PRECISE-DAPT score >=25, ARC-HBR criteria)
- High-intensity statin lifelong (LDL goal <70 mg/dL if very high risk; add ezetimibe if needed)
- ACE-I/ARB (all post-MI patients unless contraindicated)
- Beta-blocker (continue for at least 3 years; indefinitely if LVEF <40%)
- Cardiac rehabilitation referral (initiate during hospitalization)
- Smoking cessation, BP control (<130/80), diabetes management, weight management

### 3.4.2 NSTEMI/UA Protocol

Unlike STEMI, not all NSTEMI patients benefit from immediate invasive strategy. Risk stratification guides timing.

1. **Risk Stratification:** Calculate GRACE score on admission (predicts in-hospital and 6-month mortality)
2. **Immediate Medical Therapy** (same as STEMI except anticoagulation choices differ):
   - Aspirin 325 mg loading, then 81 mg daily
   - P2Y12 inhibitor (ticagrelor preferred; prasugrel if proceeding to early PCI and not excluded; clopidogrel alternative)
   - Anticoagulation: Enoxaparin 1 mg/kg SC q12h, fondaparinux 2.5 mg SC daily (preferred if conservative strategy due to lower bleeding risk), or UFH
   - High-intensity statin
   - Beta-blocker (unless contraindicated)
   - Nitroglycerin for ischemic symptoms
3. **Timing of Coronary Angiography:**
   - **Immediate (<2 hours from arrival):** Refractory angina despite medical therapy, hemodynamic instability, malignant ventricular arrhythmia (VT/VF), acute HF
   - **Early (<24 hours):** GRACE score >140, dynamic ST changes, elevated troponin with ongoing symptoms, new ST depression
   - **Delayed (24-72 hours):** Intermediate-risk patients (GRACE 109-140)
   - **Conservative/ischemia-guided:** Low-risk patients (GRACE <=108) with no recurrent symptoms, negative serial troponins, no dynamic ECG changes; stress testing before discharge

## 3.5 Risk Stratification Scores

### 3.5.1 GRACE Score (for ACS) [^20^]

The GRACE (Global Registry of Acute Coronary Events) score is the most widely validated risk tool for ACS, predicting in-hospital and 6-month mortality.

| Variable | Score Contribution |
|----------|-------------------|
| Age | Linear (higher age = higher score; max contribution) |
| Heart rate | Linear (higher HR = higher score) |
| Systolic BP | Inverse linear (lower BP = higher score) |
| Serum creatinine | Linear (higher creatinine = higher score) |
| Killip class | I=0, II=20, III=39, IV=59 points |
| ST-segment deviation | +28 points if present |
| Cardiac arrest at admission | +39 points |
| Elevated cardiac markers (troponin) | +14 points |

| Risk Category | Score | Predicted In-Hospital Mortality | Management |
|--------------|-------|-------------------------------|------------|
| Low risk | <=108 | <1% | Conservative strategy; early stress testing; discharge with medical therapy |
| Intermediate risk | 109-140 | 1-3% | Early invasive strategy (24-72 hours) |
| High risk | >140 | >3% (up to 20% if >180) | Immediate invasive strategy (<24 hours) |

### 3.5.2 TIMI Score (for NSTEMI/UA) [^20^]

The TIMI (Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction) risk score predicts 14-day risk of death, new/recurrent MI, or severe recurrent ischemia requiring urgent revascularization.

| Risk Factor | Points |
|-------------|--------|
| Age >=65 years | 1 |
| >=3 CAD risk factors (hypertension, diabetes, smoking, family history, hyperlipidemia) | 1 |
| Known CAD (stenosis >=50%) | 1 |
| Aspirin use in past 7 days | 1 |
| Severe angina (>=2 episodes in 24 hours) | 1 |
| ST deviation >=0.5 mm on ECG | 1 |
| Elevated cardiac markers (troponin) | 1 |

| Score | Risk Category | 14-Day Event Rate |
|-------|--------------|-------------------|
| 0-2 | Low risk | 4.7% |
| 3-4 | Intermediate risk | 19.2% |
| 5-7 | High risk | 41.1% |

### 3.5.3 PRECISE-DAPT Score (for Bleeding Risk on DAPT)

Used to guide DAPT duration after PCI. Score >=25 indicates high bleeding risk, supporting shorter DAPT (3-6 months).

| Variable | Points |
|----------|--------|
| Age | 0-14 points (older = more points) |
| Creatinine clearance | 0-7 points (lower = more points) |
| Hemoglobin | 0-11 points (lower = more points) |
| White blood cell count | 0-9 points (higher = more points) |
| Prior spontaneous bleeding | 0-14 points |

### 3.6.3 Revascularization in CCS

Revascularization (PCI or CABG) in stable CAD is indicated for:
- Symptom relief when medical therapy inadequate
- Prognostic benefit in specific anatomic patterns:
  - Left main stenosis >=50%
  - Proximal LAD stenosis >=70%
  - Multivessel disease with diabetes (CABG preferred - FREEDOM trial)
  - Large area of ischemia (>10% of myocardium) on stress testing
  - Three-vessel disease with LV dysfunction

## 3.7 Red Flags

> **EMERGENCY - CALL EMS IMMEDIATELY:**
> - Chest pain/discomfort lasting >20 minutes not relieved by rest or nitroglycerin (possible STEMI/NSTEMI)
> - STEMI on ECG (ST elevation >=1 mm in 2+ contiguous leads, or new LBBB with ischemic symptoms)
> - Cardiogenic shock (hypotension, cool extremities, altered mental status, oliguria)
> - Malignant ventricular arrhythmia (sustained VT, VF)
> - Suspected aortic dissection (tearing pain radiating to back, pulse/BP differential >20 mmHg between arms, new neurologic deficits)
> - Severe dyspnea with pulmonary edema (possible acute MR, free wall rupture)
> - Electrical alternans on ECG with hypotension (cardiac tamponade)

## 3.8 When to Seek Emergency Care

Seek emergency medical attention immediately if you experience:
- Chest pressure, squeezing, or pain lasting more than 5-10 minutes
- Chest pain radiating to the arm, jaw, neck, back, or shoulder
- Chest pain accompanied by shortness of breath, sweating, nausea, or lightheadedness
- Sudden severe chest pain at rest
- Fainting or near-fainting with chest discomfort
- Known CAD with chest pain not relieved by rest or one dose of nitroglycerin within 5 minutes

# Chapter 4: Arrhythmias

## 4.1 Overview

Cardiac arrhythmias represent disorders of cardiac impulse formation, conduction, or both. They range from benign extrasystoles (premature atrial or ventricular contractions) to life-threatening ventricular tachyarrhythmias. Arrhythmias may cause symptoms ranging from palpitations and dizziness to syncope, heart failure, and sudden cardiac death. This chapter covers the most clinically significant arrhythmias encountered in primary and emergency care: atrial fibrillation (the most common sustained arrhythmia), supraventricular tachycardia, ventricular tachycardia, and bradyarrhythmias. Accurate diagnosis and risk stratification are essential for appropriate management.

## 4.2 Atrial Fibrillation (AF)

### 4.2.1 Clinical Overview

Atrial fibrillation is the most common sustained arrhythmia, affecting approximately 2-4% of adults worldwide (rising to >10% in those >80 years). The lifetime risk of developing AF is approximately 1 in 3 for European-descent adults. AF is associated with a 5-fold increased risk of ischemic stroke, 2-fold increased mortality, 3-fold increased risk of heart failure, and significant impairment in quality of life. The prevalence is increasing due to aging populations and rising rates of obesity, hypertension, diabetes, and obstructive sleep apnea. The 2024 ESC and 2023 ACC/AHA Guidelines provide the current evidence-based management framework [^16^].

**Pathophysiology:** AF is initiated by triggered activity (often from pulmonary vein foci) and maintained by re-entry circuits in structurally remodeled atria. Key mechanisms include: (1) electrical remodeling (shortened atrial refractory period), (2) structural remodeling (fibrosis, dilation), and (3) autonomic modulation. Risk factors promote atrial substrate changes that sustain AF once initiated.

### 4.2.2 Classification

| Type | Duration | Management Implications |
|------|----------|------------------------|
| **First-detected** | Initial presentation regardless of duration | Perform comprehensive evaluation; assess stroke risk; determine underlying cause |
| **Paroxysmal** | Terminates spontaneously or with intervention within 7 days | May not require long-term rate/rhythm control if infrequent and minimally symptomatic |
| **Persistent** | Continuous >7 days; requires cardioversion or ablation for sinus rhythm restoration | Requires decision about rhythm control strategy |
| **Long-standing persistent** | Continuous >12 months | Rhythm control still possible but more challenging; higher ablation failure rate |
| **Permanent** | Accepted as ongoing; joint clinician-patient decision not to pursue rhythm control | Focus on rate control and stroke prevention |

> **Important:** The classification scheme is not static. A patient with paroxysmal AF may progress to persistent AF over time ("AF begets AF"). Treatment decisions should not be based solely on AF pattern but on symptoms, stroke risk, comorbidities, and patient preferences.

### 4.2.3 CHA2DS2-VASc Score (Stroke Risk Stratification) [^16^]

The CHA2DS2-VASc score is the standard tool for estimating stroke risk in non-valvular AF and guiding anticoagulation decisions.

| Risk Factor | Points | Rationale |
|-------------|--------|-----------|
| Congestive heart failure (or LVEF <40%) | 1 | Stasis and endothelial dysfunction promote thrombus formation |
| Hypertension | 1 | Vascular remodeling and impaired cerebral autoregulation |
| Age >=75 years | 2 | Stroke risk increases exponentially with age |
| Diabetes mellitus | 1 | Endothelial dysfunction and prothrombotic state |
| Prior Stroke/TIA/systemic thromboembolism | 2 | Highest risk factor for recurrent stroke (risk doubles) |
| Vascular disease (CAD, PAD, aortic plaque) | 1 | Shared atherosclerotic disease burden |
| Age 65-74 years | 1 | Moderate age-related risk increase |
| Sex category (female) | 1 | Modest independent risk, especially in combination with other factors |

**Anticoagulation Recommendations:**

| CHA2DS2-VASc Score | Male | Female |
|-------------------|------|--------|
| 0 | No anticoagulation (low risk; consider if additional risk factors) | No anticoagulation |
| 1 | Consider anticoagulation (shared decision-making) | Consider anticoagulation |
| >=2 | Recommend anticoagulation (strong recommendation) | Recommend anticoagulation |

> **Key Points:**
> - **DOACs (apixaban, rivaroxaban, dabigatran, edoxaban) are preferred over warfarin** for non-valvular AF (Class I recommendation) [^16^]
> - DOACs have lower intracranial hemorrhage risk (approximately 50% reduction), no INR monitoring, fewer drug-food interactions, and comparable or superior efficacy for stroke prevention
> - Warfarin remains preferred for mechanical heart valves (target INR 2.0-3.0 for bileaflet aortic valve; 2.5-3.5 for older valves or mitral position) and moderate-severe rheumatic mitral stenosis (these are "valvular AF" and were excluded from DOAC trials)
> - Anticoagulation decisions should be based on stroke risk (CHA2DS2-VASc), NOT on AF pattern (paroxysmal vs. persistent)
> - Paroxysmal AF carries the same stroke risk as persistent AF for a given CHA2DS2-VASc score
> - All men with score >=2 and all women with score >=3 should receive anticoagulation unless contraindicated

**DOAC Comparison for AF:**

| Drug | Dosing | Renal Adjustment | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|------|--------|-----------------|------------|---------------|
| **Apixaban** | 5 mg BID (2.5 mg BID if 2 of: age >80, weight <60 kg, creatinine >1.5) | Generally none required (avoid if CrCl <15) | Lowest bleeding risk; reduced ICH; no food requirement | Twice daily dosing |
| **Rivaroxaban** | 20 mg daily with food (15 mg if CrCl 15-50) | Reduce dose if CrCl 15-50 | Once daily | Higher GI bleeding; must take with food |
| **Dabigatran** | 150 mg BID (75 mg BID if CrCl 15-30) | Reduce dose if CrCl 15-30 | Direct thrombin inhibitor; reversal agent available (idarucizumab) | Dyspepsia; twice daily; GI bleeding |
| **Edoxaban** | 60 mg daily (30 mg if CrCl 15-50 or weight <=60 kg) | Reduce dose if CrCl 15-50 | Once daily | Less effective if CrCl >95 (high creatinine clearance); use warfarin instead |

### 4.2.4 HAS-BLED Score (Bleeding Risk Assessment) [^16^]

The HAS-BLED score helps identify modifiable bleeding risk factors. It should NOT be used to withhold anticoagulation from patients who would otherwise benefit.

| Risk Factor | Points |
|-------------|--------|
| Hypertension (uncontrolled, SBP >160 mmHg on therapy) | 1 |
| Abnormal renal function (dialysis, transplant, creatinine >2.26 mg/dL or eGFR <30) | 1 |
| Abnormal liver function (cirrhosis, bilirubin >2x ULN, AST/ALT/ALP >3x ULN) | 1 |
| Stroke history | 1 |
| Bleeding history or predisposition (major bleeding, anemia, thrombocytopenia) | 1 |
| Labile INR (if on warfarin; TTR <60%) | 1 |
| Elderly (age >65 years) | 1 |
| Drugs (antiplatelet, NSAIDs) or alcohol excess (>=8 drinks/week) | 1 each (max 2) |

> **Critical Interpretation:**
> - Score >=3 indicates high bleeding risk
> - **HAS-BLED does NOT contraindicate anticoagulation** - it identifies modifiable bleeding risk factors that should be addressed
> - Do NOT withhold anticoagulation based on bleeding risk alone; the net clinical benefit overwhelmingly favors anticoagulation in most patients with CHA2DS2-VASc >=2 (male) or >=3 (female)
> - Address modifiable factors: uncontrolled BP (target <130/80), concurrent NSAIDs (discontinue if possible), excess alcohol, labile INR (switch to DOAC), anemia (treat cause), thrombocytopenia

### 4.2.5 Rate vs. Rhythm Control

The decision between rate control and rhythm control should be individualized based on symptoms, AF burden, patient age, comorbidities, and patient preferences.

#### Rate Control
- **First-line for most patients**, especially asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic
- Target resting heart rate: <110 bpm ("lenient" strategy per RACE II trial) for most patients; <80 bpm ("strict" strategy) if symptoms persist, LVEF <40%, or tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy suspected
- **Agents:**
  - **Beta-blockers** (metoprolol 25-200 mg daily, carvedilol 3.125-25 mg BID, bisoprolol 1.25-10 mg daily): First-line; also beneficial in HF; avoid in acute decompensated asthma
  - **Non-dihydropyridine CCBs** (diltiazem 120-480 mg daily ER, verapamil 120-480 mg daily ER): Effective for rate control; **contraindicated in HFrEF** (negative inotrope may worsen HF)
  - **Digoxin** (0.125-0.25 mg daily): Reinstated as first-line option in 2024 ESC guidelines; useful in sedentary patients, HF, and when beta-blockers/CCBs contraindicated; does NOT control rate during exercise; narrow therapeutic index (monitor levels 0.5-0.9 ng/mL for HF); renal dose adjustment required
- **Avoid AV nodal blockers in pre-excited AF (Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome):** Risk of accelerating conduction through accessory pathway, potentially precipitating ventricular fibrillation

#### Rhythm Control
- Consider for: symptomatic patients despite adequate rate control, younger patients (<70 years), difficulty achieving adequate rate control, tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy (LV dysfunction from rapid AF), patient preference for rhythm restoration, first episode of AF
- **Antiarrhythmic Drugs:**
  - **Flecainide, propafenone:** For structurally normal hearts (no CAD, LVH, or HF); can be used as "pill-in-the-pocket" (single dose of 200-300 mg flecainide or 450-600 mg propafenone at AF onset); avoid if EF <35%, ischemic heart disease, or significant LVH
  - **Amiodarone:** Most effective antiarrhythmic for structural heart disease; significant toxicity including thyroid dysfunction (hyper or hypo), pulmonary fibrosis (baseline and annual PFTs required), hepatotoxicity (baseline and periodic LFTs), corneal deposits (annual eye exam), peripheral neuropathy, photosensitivity, blue-gray skin discoloration; requires baseline TSH, LFTs, CXR, PFTs, ophthalmology exam
  - **Dofetilide:** Requires inpatient initiation (3-day minimum) due to risk of QT prolongation and torsades de pointes; effective in HF
  - **Sotalol:** Requires ECG monitoring during initiation (QT prolongation, torsades risk); both beta-blocker and Class III effects; renal dose adjustment
  - **Dronedarone:** For paroxysmal/persistent non-permanent AF; **contraindicated in permanent AF, NYHA IV or recent decompensated HF** (ANDROMEDA trial showed increased mortality); less effective than amiodarone but better safety profile

**Antiarrhythmic Drug Comparison:**

| Drug | Indication | Typical Dosing | Key Side Effects | Contraindications |
|------|-----------|----------------|------------------|-------------------|
| **Amiodarone** | Structural heart disease, refractory AF/VT, HF | Loading 10 g over 1-2 weeks (IV or PO); maintenance 200-400 mg daily | Hypotension (IV), bradycardia, thyroid dysfunction, pulmonary fibrosis, hepatotoxicity, corneal deposits, photosensitivity, peripheral neuropathy | Severe sinus node disease, 2nd/3rd degree AV block, iodine allergy (relative), pregnancy, thyroid disease |
| **Flecainide** | Normal heart, paroxysmal AF | 50-150 mg BID or 200-300 mg single dose (pill-in-pocket) | Proarrhythmia (VT, increased mortality post-MI in CAST trial), dizziness, visual changes | CAD, LVH (wall thickness >1.4 cm), HF, conduction disease |
| **Propafenone** | Normal heart, paroxysmal AF | 150-300 mg TID or 450-600 mg single dose | Proarrhythmia, metallic taste, constipation, dizziness | CAD, HF, severe COPD, conduction disease |
| **Sotalol** | AF, VT | 80-160 mg BID (initiate in hospital) | Torsades de pointes (QT prolongation), bradycardia, bronchospasm | Prolonged QT (QTc >450 ms men, >470 ms women), hypokalemia, severe renal impairment (CrCl <40) |
| **Dofetilide** | AF, atrial flutter | 125-500 mcg BID (inpatient initiation required) | Torsades de pointes (3-5% incidence) | Prolonged QT, severe renal impairment (CrCl <20), hypokalemia, concurrent verapamil/cimetidine/trimethoprim |
| **Dronedarone** | Paroxysmal/persistent AF (non-permanent) | 400 mg BID | Hepatotoxicity, GI upset, bradycardia, QT prolongation (less than amiodarone) | Permanent AF, NYHA IV or recent decompensated HF, concurrent strong CYP3A inhibitors, severe hepatic impairment |

### 4.2.6 Catheter Ablation for AF

Catheter ablation has evolved from a last-resort option to first-line therapy for selected patients.

**Indications:**
- **Class I (first-line in 2024 ESC):** For paroxysmal AF, especially in younger patients, as an alternative to antiarrhythmic drug therapy
- **Class I:** For AF with HFrEF (tachycardiomyopathy) - improves outcomes including LVEF, quality of life, and possibly mortality (CASTLE-AF, CABANA subgroup analyses)
- **Class IIa:** For persistent AF after failure of one or more antiarrhythmic drugs
- **Class IIa:** For symptomatic AF despite adequate rate control
- **Class IIb:** For long-standing persistent AF (experienced centers)

**Technique:** Pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) is the cornerstone. Cryoballoon ablation (one-shot technique) and radiofrequency point-by-point ablation are both effective. Success rates: 60-80% for paroxysmal AF after single procedure; higher with repeat ablation.

**Risks:** Cardiac tamponade (1%), stroke/TIA (0.5-1%), pulmonary vein stenosis (<1% with modern techniques), atrioesophageal fistula (rare but catastrophic; present with fever, neurologic symptoms, sepsis 2-4 weeks post-procedure), phrenic nerve injury (more common with cryoballoon; usually transient).

### 4.2.7 Cardioversion and Anticoagulation Around Cardioversion

- **Anticoagulate for at least 3 weeks before and 4 weeks after** elective cardioversion (electrical or pharmacological)
- This rule applies regardless of CHA2DS2-VASc score because the risk of thromboembolism is highest around the time of cardioversion due to atrial stunning (mechanical dysfunction persists even after electrical restoration of sinus rhythm)
- **TEE-guided approach:** If transesophageal echocardiography excludes left atrial appendage thrombus, may cardiovert immediately with therapeutic anticoagulation
- **Minimum 4 weeks anticoagulation after cardioversion** even if CHA2DS2-VASc = 0 (2024 ESC update) [^16^]
- Long-term anticoagulation after cardioversion should follow CHA2DS2-VASc score

### 4.2.8 Post-Operative AF

AF occurs in 15-40% of patients after cardiac surgery and 5-15% after non-cardiac surgery. Management includes:
- Rate control (beta-blockers first-line; avoid diltiazem if LV dysfunction)
- Anticoagulation if duration >48 hours or CHA2DS2-VASc indicates long-term need
- Most cases resolve within 6-12 weeks; reassess need for ongoing anticoagulation at 8-12 weeks

## 4.3 Supraventricular Tachycardia (SVT)

### 4.3.1 Types and ECG Features [^29^] [^32^]

| Type | Mechanism | ECG Features | P Wave Appearance |
|------|-----------|--------------|-------------------|
| **AVNRT** (60-70% of SVTs) | Re-entry circuit within AV node | Regular narrow-complex; rate 140-250 bpm; abrupt onset/termination | Buried in QRS (invisible) or retrograde at terminal portion (pseudo R' in V1, pseudo S wave in II, III, aVF) |
| **AVRT** (30% of SVTs) | Accessory pathway-mediated re-entry (bypass tract) | Regular narrow-complex; rate 140-250 bpm | Retrograde P wave after QRS (short RP interval <70 ms); may see delta wave in sinus rhythm if WPW |
| **Atrial tachycardia** | Ectopic atrial focus (automatic or triggered) | Regular narrow-complex; rate 100-250 bpm; warm-up and cool-down | Abnormal P wave morphology (different from sinus P); visible before QRS with long PR |
| **Atrial flutter** | Macro-re-entry circuit in right atrium (cavotricuspid isthmus) | Regular atrial rate ~300 bpm; 2:1 block gives ventricular rate ~150 bpm; 4:1 block gives ~75 bpm | Sawtooth flutter waves (F waves) best seen in II, III, aVF; may be continuous (no isoelectric baseline) |
| **Multifocal atrial tachycardia (MAT)** | Multiple ectopic atrial foci | Irregularly irregular narrow-complex; rate 100-150 bpm | >=3 distinct P wave morphologies with varying PR intervals; often associated with COPD |

### 4.3.2 Acute Management [^29^]

**Step 1: Assess hemodynamic stability**
- **Unstable** (hypotension SBP <90, altered mental status, signs of shock, chest pain suggesting acute ischemia, acute HF): Immediate synchronized cardioversion (50-100 J for SVT, 120-200 J for atrial flutter)
- **Stable:** Proceed to Step 2

**Step 2: Vagal maneuvers (if stable)**
- **Modified Valsalva:** Patient in semi-recumbent position -> strain 15 seconds (against resistance or by blowing into 10 mL syringe to move plunger) -> immediately lay flat and passively raise legs 45 degrees for 15 seconds -> sit up after 60 seconds
- **Carotid sinus massage:** Auscultate for bruits first; avoid if bruit present, recent stroke/TIA (<3 months), known carotid disease, or history of ventricular arrhythmia. Massage carotid bifurcation (at angle of jaw) firmly for 5-10 seconds. **Never massage both sides simultaneously.**
- Success rate: 19-43% (modified Valsalva superior to standard Valsalva at 5-17%) [^29^]

**Step 3: Adenosine (if vagal maneuvers fail)**
- 6 mg rapid IV push through large-bore IV (antecubital preferred) followed immediately by 20 mL saline flush
- If no conversion within 1-2 minutes: 12 mg IV push
- May repeat 12 mg once (maximum total dose 30 mg)
- Brief asystole (6-12 seconds) is expected; warn patient that they will feel "strange," chest tightness, or like they are about to die; have them supine during administration
- **Contraindicated in heart transplant recipients** (denervated heart has exaggerated response; use 1/4 dose if absolutely needed)
- **Avoid in wide-complex tachycardia of uncertain origin** (may precipitate VF in VT; adenosine is for diagnostic purposes only in stable wide-complex tachycardia)
- Almost all AVNRT and orthodromic AVRT will terminate with adenosine [^29^]

**Step 4: Alternative agents (if adenosine fails or is contraindicated)**
- Diltiazem 15-20 mg IV over 2 minutes (then 5-15 mg/hr infusion) - avoid in WPW with AF
- Verapamil 2.5-5 mg IV over 2 minutes (repeat 5-10 mg in 15-30 min if needed; max 20 mg) - avoid in WPW with AF
- Beta-blockers IV (metoprolol 5 mg IV q5min x 3 doses; esmolol 500 mcg/kg bolus then 50 mcg/kg/min infusion) - avoid in WPW with AF
- Synchronized cardioversion if becomes unstable at any point

### 4.3.3 Chronic Management [^29^]

| Strategy | Details | Recommendation |
|----------|---------|----------------|
| **Catheter ablation** | Curative for AVNRT (>95% success, low recurrence) and AVRT (>95% success for manifest pathways); first-line for recurrent symptomatic SVT | Class I |
| **Oral medications** | Beta-blocker (metoprolol), diltiazem, or verapamil for recurrent SVT (without pre-excitation); suppress but do not cure | Class IIa |
| **Pill-in-the-pocket** | Flecainide 200-300 mg or propafenone 450-600 mg single dose for infrequent, well-tolerated episodes in patients with structurally normal hearts | Class IIa |
| **Patient education** | All patients should learn vagal maneuvers for self-termination of future episodes | Essential |

### 4.3.4 WPW and Pre-excited AF

- In patients with Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome (short PR, delta wave), AF can conduct rapidly down the accessory pathway, causing very fast ventricular rates (250-300+ bpm) and potentially degenerating into VF
- **CRITICAL: Do NOT use AV nodal blockers (adenosine, calcium channel blockers, digoxin, beta-blockers, amiodarone) in pre-excited AF** - these block the AV node but facilitate conduction through the accessory pathway, potentially accelerating ventricular rate and precipitating VF
- **Treatment:** Procainamide or ibutilide (preferentially block accessory pathway); electrical cardioversion if unstable; catheter ablation of accessory pathway for definitive treatment

## 4.4 Ventricular Tachycardia (VT) [^54^] [^59^]

### 4.4.1 Classification

| Type | Features | Common Causes |
|------|----------|--------------|
| **Monomorphic VT** | Single QRS morphology; wide-complex, usually regular; rate 100-250 bpm | Prior MI with scar (most common), cardiomyopathy (dilated, hypertrophic), arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy (ARVC), channelopathies, idiopathic (RVOT VT, LVOT VT, fascicular VT) |
| **Polymorphic VT** | Changing QRS morphology from beat to beat; irregular; rate often >200 bpm | Myocardial ischemia, electrolyte abnormalities (hypokalemia, hypomagnesemia, hypocalcemia), QT prolongation (congenital or acquired), inherited channelopathies (Brugada, CPVT), catecholaminergic surge |
| **Torsades de Pointes** | Specific type of polymorphic VT with QRS complexes appearing to "twist" around the baseline; rate 200-250 bpm; always associated with prolonged QT interval | Acquired QT prolongation (drugs: antiarrhythmics, antibiotics, antipsychotics; electrolyte disturbances), congenital long QT syndrome |

### 4.4.2 Acute Management (ACLS Algorithm)

**Pulseless VT:** Treat as ventricular fibrillation - cardiac arrest protocol
- Immediate unsynchronized defibrillation (200 J biphasic, 360 J monophasic)
- High-quality CPR (30 compressions:2 breaths, rate 100-120/min, depth 5-6 cm)
- Epinephrine 1 mg IV/IO every 3-5 minutes
- Amiodarone 300 mg IV/IO push for refractory VF/pulseless VT (second dose 150 mg if persistent)
- Consider lidocaine 1-1.5 mg/kg IV if amiodarone unavailable
- Treat reversible causes (Hs and Ts: Hypoxia, Hypovolemia, Hydrogen ion/acidosis, Hypo/hyperkalemia, Hypothermia, Tension pneumothorax, Tamponade, Toxins, Thrombosis-pulmonary/coronary)

**Stable VT with Pulse:**
1. **Verify diagnosis:** Wide-complex regular tachycardia; if uncertain whether SVT with aberrancy vs. VT, assume VT (treat as VT - safer; most wide-complex tachycardias in adults are VT)
2. **Wide-complex regular:** Consider adenosine 6 mg rapid IV (diagnostic, not therapeutic; if transient AV block reveals atrial flutter or if it terminates = SVT; if no effect = supports VT)
3. **IV antiarrhythmics:**
   - **Amiodarone:** 150 mg IV over 10 minutes; may repeat every 10 minutes as needed; maintenance 1 mg/min x 6 hours, then 0.5 mg/min x 18 hours
   - **Procainamide:** 20-50 mg/min IV until arrhythmia suppressed, hypotension occurs, QRS widens >50% from baseline, or maximum 17 mg/kg given; then 1-4 mg/min infusion
   - **Sotalol:** 100 mg (1.5 mg/kg) IV over 5 minutes (avoid if prolonged QT)
4. **Synchronized cardioversion** if patient becomes unstable at any point (100 J for regular wide-complex, 200 J for irregular)

**Polymorphic VT:**
- Check and immediately correct: Potassium (target >4.0 mEq/L), Magnesium (target >2.0 mg/dL), Calcium, pH
- **IV magnesium sulfate** 2 g over 10-15 minutes for Torsades de Pointes (even if magnesium level is normal)
- Defibrillation (unsynchronized) if pulseless or unstable
- Consider overdrive pacing or isoproterenol infusion for bradycardia-dependent Torsades
- Discontinue any QT-prolonging medications immediately
- Beta-blockade for congenital long QT and CPVT

## 4.5 Bradyarrhythmias

### 4.5.1 Types and Management

| Condition | ECG Features | Clinical Significance | Management |
|-----------|-------------|----------------------|------------|
| **Sinus bradycardia** | Rate <60 bpm; normal P waves before each QRS with normal PR interval | Normal variant in athletes and during sleep; can be drug-induced (beta-blockers, digoxin, calcium channel blockers, amiodarone) | Treat underlying cause; atropine if symptomatic (hypotension, syncope, angina); rarely requires pacing |
| **Sinus node dysfunction (sick sinus syndrome)** | Sinus pauses >3 seconds, sinus arrest, chronotropic incompetence (failure to increase HR with exertion), bradycardia-tachycardia syndrome (alternating bradycardia and SVT/AF) | Fatigue, syncope, dizziness, exercise intolerance; bradycardia-tachycardia variant particularly challenging (need anticoagulation for AF but bradycardia limits rate control agents) | Permanent pacemaker if symptomatic; review and adjust medications; anticoagulation for AF component |
| **First-degree AV block** | PR interval >200 ms (5 small boxes) | Usually benign; no progression in most patients; may be normal variant or drug-related | Observation; no specific treatment; monitor if progressive |
| **Second-degree AV block Mobitz I (Wenckebach)** | Progressive PR prolongation until dropped QRS complex; then cycle repeats; grouped beating pattern | Usually benign; often at AV node level (inferior MI, drugs, increased vagal tone); usually transient | Observation if asymptomatic; pacemaker if symptomatic (syncope, HF) or if block below AV node (wide QRS escape rhythm) |
| **Second-degree AV block Mobitz II** | Fixed PR interval with intermittent non-conducted P waves without progressive PR prolongation | Risk of progression to complete heart block; usually infranodal (His-Purkinje system); often associated with wide QRS | **Permanent pacemaker regardless of symptoms** (high risk of progression to complete heart block and asystole) |
| **Third-degree (complete) AV block** | Complete AV dissociation; P waves and QRS complexes march independently at their own rates; atrial rate > ventricular rate | Bradyarrhythmia-related syncope risk; risk of asystole; escape rhythm may be unreliable (narrow = junctional, reliable; wide = ventricular, unreliable) | **Permanent pacemaker** unless reversible cause (Lyme disease - treat with antibiotics; drugs - discontinue; hyperkalemia - correct; inferior MI - may resolve) |
| **Bundle branch blocks** | RBBB (rsR' in V1, wide S in I/V6); LBBB (broad monophasic R in V6, deep S in V1) | May cause symptoms if bilateral (alternating BBB) or if associated with AV block; new LBBB with symptoms concerning for ACS | Pacemaker if associated with symptomatic bradycardia or alternating bundle branch block |

### 4.5.2 ACLS for Symptomatic Bradycardia [^54^]

1. **Atropine** 0.5 mg IV push every 3-5 minutes (maximum 3 mg total = complete vagal blockade)
   - Effective for sinus bradycardia and AV nodal block (Mobitz I)
   - Generally ineffective for infranodal blocks (Mobitz II, third-degree with wide QRS escape)
   - Do NOT delay transcutaneous pacing for transvenous pacing if atropine ineffective

2. **Transcutaneous pacing** (external pads) if atropine ineffective or contraindicated
   - Start at 60-80 bpm; increase output until electrical capture (then confirm mechanical capture by pulse check)
   - Painful in conscious patients; use sedation (benzodiazepines or opioids) if mental status allows
   - Bridge to transvenous or permanent pacing

3. **Dopamine infusion** 2-20 mcg/kg/min (titrated to effect) or **epinephrine infusion** 2-10 mcg/min as bridge to transvenous pacing

4. **Transvenous pacing** for persistent symptomatic bradycardia unresponsive to atropine and transcutaneous pacing

5. **Permanent pacemaker** for irreversible conditions (Mobitz II, complete heart block, symptomatic sinus node dysfunction)

### 4.5.3 Indications for Permanent Pacemaker

| Condition | Class I Indication (Definite Benefit) |
|-----------|--------------------------------------|
| Third-degree AV block | Symptomatic bradycardia; or escape rhythm <40 bpm; or pauses >3 seconds; or post-AV junction ablation; or neuromuscular disease (myotonic dystrophy, Kearns-Sayre) |
| Mobitz II | Regardless of symptoms (high risk of progression) |
| Sinus node dysfunction | Documented symptomatic bradycardia including bradycardia-tachycardia syndrome requiring antiarrhythmic drugs that cause bradycardia |
| Bifascicular block | Intermittent third-degree AV block or Mobitz II; or alternating BBB |
| Carotid sinus hypersensitivity | Recurrent syncope with cardioinhibitory response (asystole >3 seconds) |

## 4.6 Red Flags

> **EMERGENCY INDICATORS - SEEK IMMEDIATE CARE:**
> - AF with rapid ventricular response causing hypotension (SBP <90), acute pulmonary edema, or ongoing ischemic chest pain
> - AF with pre-excitation (WPW) and wide QRS complex: Do NOT use AV nodal blockers (adenosine, calcium channel blockers, digoxin, beta-blockers) - risk of precipitating VF
> - Pulseless VT or VF: Immediate defibrillation and CPR
> - Symptomatic bradycardia with syncope, hypotension, or hemodynamic compromise
> - New-onset AF in the setting of acute MI, acute PE, or thyrotoxic storm (treat underlying cause urgently)
> - Torsades de Pointes with hemodynamic compromise
> - Complete heart block with wide QRS escape rhythm or hemodynamic instability
> - Drug overdose causing arrhythmia (tricyclic antidepressants, digoxin, beta-blocker, calcium channel blocker)

## 4.7 When to Refer

| Scenario | Urgency | Referral |
|----------|---------|----------|
| New AF (first episode) | Within 1-2 weeks | Cardiology or electrophysiology |
| Symptomatic AF despite rate control | Elective | Electrophysiology (ablation evaluation) |
| AF with tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy | Urgent | Electrophysiology (ablation - Class I) |
| Recurrent SVT | Elective | Electrophysiology (ablation - curative) |
| Sustained VT | Emergency | Cardiology/electrophysiology |
| Indication for ICD/CRT | Elective | Electrophysiology |
| Syncope with suspected arrhythmic cause | Urgent | Cardiology/electrophysiology (consider loop recorder) |
| Complete heart block or Mobitz II | Urgent/emergent | Electrophysiology (permanent pacemaker) |
| Long QT syndrome, Brugada, CPVT | Elective | Electrophysiology (genetic arrhythmia specialist) |

# Chapter 5: Valvular Heart Disease

## 5.1 Overview

Valvular heart disease (VHD) affects approximately 2.5% of the US population, with prevalence increasing dramatically with age (up to 13% in those >=75 years). Rheumatic heart disease remains the most common cause globally (affecting 40 million people), while degenerative calcific disease dominates in developed nations. Other causes include congenital abnormalities (bicuspid aortic valve), infective endocarditis, connective tissue disorders (Marfan syndrome, Ehlers-Danlos), radiation, and drug-induced (anorectic drugs, ergot alkaloids). The 2025 ESC/EACTS and 2020 ACC/AHA Guidelines provide comprehensive frameworks for evaluation and management [^17^]. Untreated severe VHD carries significant morbidity and mortality; however, timely intervention can restore near-normal life expectancy.

## 5.2 Aortic Stenosis (AS)

### 5.2.1 Clinical Features

Aortic stenosis is the most common valvular lesion requiring intervention in developed countries, primarily due to age-related calcification of a tricuspid valve (present in 2-4% of adults >65 years) or earlier presentation in congenital bicuspid aortic valve (present in 1-2% of the population).

**Classic Triad** (each symptom indicates severe disease and warrants urgent intervention):
1. **Angina** (approximately 35% of patients at presentation; average survival 5 years without intervention)
2. **Syncope** (typically exertional; average survival 3 years without intervention)
3. **Dyspnea/Heart failure** (indicates very advanced disease; average survival 1-2 years without intervention)

**Physical Examination Findings:**
- Harsh crescendo-decrescendo systolic ejection murmur at the right upper sternal border (second intercostal space)
- Radiation to both carotid arteries (right > left due to direction of aortic jet)
- Delayed and diminished carotid upstrokes (pulsus parvus et tardus - "parvus" = small, "tardus" = delayed; this is the most specific physical finding for severe AS)
- Soft or absent A2 component of S2 (calcified immobile valve cannot close properly)
- Paradoxical splitting of S2 in very severe AS (A2 occurs after P2 due to prolonged LV ejection time)
- Ejection click may be present early in disease (bicuspid aortic valve opening snap)
- Narrow pulse pressure in severe disease
- Sustained apical impulse (LV hypertrophy)
- S4 gallop (atrial contraction against stiff hypertrophied LV)

### 5.2.2 Severity Assessment [^17^]

Echocardiography is the gold standard for severity assessment. Multiple parameters should be evaluated together.

| Parameter | Mild | Moderate | Severe |
|-----------|------|----------|--------|
| Aortic valve area (AVA) | >1.5 cm2 | 1.0-1.5 cm2 | <1.0 cm2 |
| Indexed AVA (AVA/body surface area) | >0.85 cm2/m2 | 0.60-0.85 cm2/m2 | <0.60 cm2/m2 |
| Mean transvalvular gradient | <20 mmHg | 20-40 mmHg | >=40 mmHg |
| Peak jet velocity | <3.0 m/s | 3.0-4.0 m/s | >4.0 m/s |

**Special Subsets:**
- **Low-flow, low-gradient severe AS:** Reduced stroke volume (e.g., severe LV dysfunction) results in lower gradients despite severe stenosis. Differentiate true severe AS (AVA <1.0 cm2 with high gradient on dobutamine stress echo) from pseudo-severe AS (AVA increases to >1.0 cm2 with dobutamine - the valve is not truly stenotic, just not opening fully due to low flow).
- **Paradoxical low-flow, low-gradient severe AS:** Severe AS with preserved LVEF (>50%) but low stroke volume (small LV cavity, severe concentric hypertrophy, diastolic dysfunction). Often seen in elderly women with hypertension. Prognosis is poor without intervention.
- **Normal-flow, low-gradient AS:** AVA <1.0 cm2 but mean gradient <40 mmHg with normal stroke volume. May represent measurement error or anatomic variant. Re-measure AVA using 3D or planimetry.

### 5.2.3 Natural History
- Asymptomatic severe AS: Risk of sudden death approximately 1% per year; risk of symptom development 4-6% per year
- Once symptoms appear, survival is dramatically reduced without intervention (see classic triad above)
- Average rate of hemodynamic progression: increase in jet velocity 0.3 m/s per year

### 5.2.4 Management [^17^]

**Indications for Intervention:**

| Clinical Scenario | Recommendation | Class | Level of Evidence |
|------------------|----------------|-------|-------------------|
| Symptomatic severe AS (any symptom: dyspnea, angina, syncope, decreased exercise tolerance) | Intervention indicated regardless of LVEF | Class I | A |
| Asymptomatic severe AS with LVEF <50% (without other cause) | Intervention indicated | Class I | B |
| Asymptomatic severe AS with high gradient, normal LVEF, AND low surgical/interventional risk | Intervention should be considered as alternative to surveillance | Class IIa | A |
| Asymptomatic severe AS with exercise-induced symptoms | Intervention indicated | Class I | B |
| Asymptomatic severe AS with rapid hemodynamic progression (increase in jet velocity >0.3 m/s/year) | Intervention should be considered | Class IIa | B |
| Moderate AS undergoing cardiac surgery for other reasons | Consider concomitant AVR | Class IIa | C |

**Intervention Options:**

| Procedure | Best Candidates | Advantages | Considerations/Limitations |
|-----------|----------------|------------|---------------------------|
| **SAVR (Surgical Aortic Valve Replacement)** | Low surgical risk (STS-PROM <4%), younger patients (<65-70 years), longer life expectancy (>10-15 years), small aortic annulus, concomitant cardiac surgery needed (CABG, ascending aorta repair, other valve surgery), bicuspid valve with unfavorable anatomy for TAVI | Gold standard with proven long-term durability (bioprosthetic 10-15 years, mechanical >30 years); allows complete removal of diseased valve; concomitant procedures possible | Sternotomy (though minimally invasive approaches available); longer recovery (6-8 weeks); prosthetic valve choice (mechanical requires lifelong warfarin; bioprosthetic will eventually require reintervention) |
| **TAVI/TAVR (Transcatheter Aortic Valve Implantation/Replacement)** | High/intermediate surgical risk (STS-PROM >=4%), elderly (>=70-75 years), prior cardiac surgery, prohibitive surgical risk, native valve anatomy suitable for device delivery | Minimally invasive (transfemoral approach most common); no sternotomy; shorter recovery (3-5 days hospitalization); no general anesthesia required in most cases; can be performed in patients who are not surgical candidates | Vascular access complications (1-5%); paravalvular leak (mild-moderate in 5-10% with newer-generation devices, much improved from first generation); permanent pacemaker requirement (5-10% due to conduction system injury); limited long-term durability data beyond 10 years; higher cost; coronary access considerations for future PCI |

**Decision Framework:** The Heart Team approach (interventional cardiologist, cardiac surgeon, imaging specialist, anesthesiologist, geriatrician, and patient) is recommended for all patients with severe AS being considered for intervention. Key decision factors include:
- Age and life expectancy
- Surgical risk (STS-PROM score, frailty)
- Valve anatomy (annulus size, calcification pattern, coronary height)
- Patient preference (avoiding warfarin, desire for least invasive approach)
- Need for concomitant procedures
- Access site quality (femoral artery size, tortuosity, calcification)

**Valve Choice:**
- **Mechanical valve:** For patients <50-60 years who can tolerate warfarin (excellent durability; lifelong anticoagulation required with target INR 2.0-3.0 for aortic position)
- **Bioprosthetic valve:** For patients >65 years or those who cannot/will not take warfarin (no anticoagulation required after initial 3 months; limited durability 10-15 years; valve-in-valve TAVI possible if degeneration occurs)
- **Shared decision-making:** For patients 50-65 years, individualized decision based on patient preference, lifestyle, ability to adhere to anticoagulation, and willingness to accept future reintervention

## 5.3 Aortic Regurgitation (AR)

### 5.3.1 Clinical Features

Aortic regurgitation can be acute (infective endocarditis, aortic dissection, trauma) or chronic (bicuspid valve, rheumatic, degenerative, connective tissue disease, syphilis, inflammatory).

**Physical Examination:**
- High-pitched, blowing decrescendo diastolic murmur at the left sternal border (third to fourth intercostal space), best heard with patient sitting up, leaning forward, at end-expiration
- Wide pulse pressure (systolic hypertension with diastolic hypotension due to regurgitant flow)
- **Peripheral signs of hyperdynamic circulation:**
  - Quincke pulses (capillary pulsations in fingernail bed visible with light pressure)
  - de Musset sign (head bobbing with each heartbeat)
  - Duroziez sign (systolic-diastolic murmur over femoral artery with compression)
  - Hill sign (popliteal SBP exceeds brachial SBP by >60 mmHg in severe AR)
  - Corrigan pulse (water-hammer pulse - rapid rise and fall)
  - Traube sign (pistol shot sounds over femoral arteries)
- Austin Flint murmur: Functional mitral stenosis murmur (mid-diastolic rumble at apex) caused by AR jet impinging on anterior mitral leaflet, preventing full opening

### 5.3.2 Management [^17^]

**Acute Severe AR:** This is a surgical emergency. Medical therapy (IV vasodilators, inotropes) is a bridge to emergency surgery. Causes include aortic dissection, infective endocarditis, trauma, prosthetic valve dysfunction.

**Chronic AR Medical Therapy:**
- Vasodilators (dihydropyridine CCBs, ACE-I/ARB, hydralazine) for afterload reduction in chronic severe AR with hypertension or symptoms
- **Avoid beta-blockers in acute severe AR** (prolong diastole, increase regurgitant time, worsen hemodynamics)
- Beta-blockers may be used cautiously in chronic AR with associated HTN or CAD

**Surgical Indications (Chronic Severe AR):**

| Indication | Class | Notes |
|-----------|-------|-------|
| Symptomatic severe AR (any symptom) | Class I | Regardless of LV function |
| Asymptomatic severe AR with LV dysfunction (LVESD >50 mm or LVESDi >25 mm/m2, or LVEF <50%) | Class I | Key thresholds for intervention |
| Asymptomatic severe AR undergoing other cardiac surgery | Class I | Concomitant ARR |
| Asymptomatic severe AR with progressive LV dilation approaching surgical thresholds (LVESD approaching 50 mm) | Class IIa | Close monitoring with serial echoes every 6 months |

**SAVR is the treatment of choice for chronic severe AR.** TAVI for pure native AR is investigational (not FDA approved) but may be considered for inoperable patients with suitable anatomy (some dedicated AR TAVI devices in clinical trials).

## 5.4 Mitral Regurgitation (MR)

### 5.4.1 Classification

| Feature | Primary MR (Degenerative/Organic) | Secondary MR (Functional) |
|---------|----------------------------------|--------------------------|
| **Pathology** | Structural abnormality of mitral apparatus (leaflets, chords, annulus, papillary muscles) | Normal valve leaflets; MR due to ventricular and/or atrial remodeling |
| **Etiologies** | Degenerative/myxomatous (prolapse, Barlow disease), fibroelastic deficiency, rheumatic heart disease, infective endocarditis, chordal rupture (spontaneous or trauma), papillary muscle dysfunction/rupture (ischemic), congenital cleft, drugs (anorectics, ergot) | Ventricular functional MR (LV dilation/dysfunction causing annular dilation, papillary muscle displacement, tethering); Atrial functional MR (LA dilation causing annular enlargement, often with AF) |
| **Management focus** | Valve intervention (repair strongly preferred over replacement when feasible) | Treat underlying cause first (GDMT for HF); intervene if persistent despite optimal medical therapy |

### 5.4.2 Primary MR Management [^17^]

- **Surgical mitral valve repair is preferred over replacement** whenever feasible (Class I recommendation)
- Repair preserves LV function, avoids prosthetic valve complications, maintains normal leaflet physiology, and improves long-term survival compared to replacement
- Durability of repair: 90-95% freedom from reoperation at 10 years in experienced centers
- **Indications for intervention in primary MR:**
  - Symptomatic severe primary MR (Class I)
  - Asymptomatic severe primary MR with LV dysfunction (LVESD >=40 mm, LVESDi >=22 mm/m2, or LVEF <=60%) (Class I)
  - Asymptomatic severe primary MR with preserved LV function (LVEF >60%, LVESD <40 mm) AND high likelihood of durable repair (>95% repair rate at experienced center) (Class IIa)
  - Asymptomatic severe primary MR with preserved LV function and new-onset AF or resting pulmonary hypertension (PASP >50 mmHg) (Class IIa)

**TEER (Transcatheter Edge-to-Edge Repair) / MitraClip:**
- For high-surgical-risk patients with suitable anatomy (Class IIa)
- Creates a "double-orifice" mitral valve by clipping together the anterior and posterior leaflets at the site of maximal regurgitation
- Not suitable for all anatomies (requires adequate leaflet length and coaptation depth)

### 5.4.3 Secondary MR Management [^17^]

**Stepwise Approach:**

1. **Optimize GDMT first:** ACE-I/ARB/ARNI, beta-blocker, MRA, SGLT2i. Approximately 40% of patients with secondary MR show significant improvement after 1-3 months of optimal medical therapy. Do NOT rush to intervention before optimizing medical therapy.
2. **CRT (Cardiac Resynchronization Therapy):** If indicated (LVEF <=35%, LBBB with QRS >=150 ms, or >=130 ms with non-LBBB and NYHA II-IV despite GDMT). CRT can reduce MR by improving LV geometry and synchronizing papillary muscle contraction.
3. **M-TEER (MitraClip) for secondary MR:** Recommended in symptomatic patients on optimal GDMT (including CRT if indicated) without CAD needing revascularization, who meet COAPT trial criteria:
   - LVEF 20-50%
   - LVESD <=70 mm
   - Elevated pulmonary artery pressure (PASP >30 mmHg)
   - Suitable anatomy (adequate leaflet length >=2 mm, coaptation length >=2 mm, coaptation depth <11 mm)
   - (Class I for COAPT-like patients)
   
   > **Important:** The MITRA-FR trial showed no benefit, highlighting that patient selection is critical. The difference between COAPT (positive) and MITRA-FR (negative) relates to MR severity relative to LV dimensions, with COAPT patients having disproportionately severe MR for their LV size.

4. **Surgical MV repair/replacement:** If concomitant CAD requiring CABG, if TEER not anatomically suitable, or if failed TEER

### 5.4.4 Acute Severe MR
- **Causes:** Papillary muscle rupture (post-MI, usually inferoposterior), chordal rupture (myxomatous valve, endocarditis), infective endocarditis with leaflet perforation
- **Presentation:** Acute pulmonary edema, shock, new holosystolic murmur (may be soft due to equalization of pressures)
- **Management:** Afterload reduction (IV nitroprusside, IABP), emergency surgery (valve repair or replacement)

## 5.5 Prosthetic Valves

### 5.5.1 Antithrombotic Therapy [^17^]

**Mechanical Valves:**
- **Lifelong VKA (warfarin) required** - DOACs are contraindicated in mechanical valves
- RE-ALIGN trial showed harm with dabigatran in mechanical valves (increased thrombosis and bleeding)
- Target INR: 2.5 (range 2.0-3.0) for bileaflet aortic valves; 3.0 (range 2.5-3.5) for mitral position or older generation aortic valves
- Add low-dose aspirin (75-100 mg daily) for all mechanical valves (reduces thromboembolism and mortality)
- Ensure therapeutic INR before any invasive procedures; bridge with LMWH or UFH if interruption required

**Bioprosthetic Valves:**
- VKA for 3 months post-implantation (target INR 2.5, range 2.0-3.0) - until endothelialization is complete
- Transition to aspirin 75-100 mg daily after 3 months
- DOACs (apixaban or rivaroxaban) may be used as alternative to VKA for the initial 3 months (growing evidence; some centers prefer DOACs due to lower bleeding risk)
- Lifelong aspirin thereafter
- If AF is present, anticoagulation should follow CHA2DS2-VASc score (DOACs acceptable in bioprosthetic valves with AF)

### 5.5.2 Valve-Related Complications

| Complication | Presentation | Management |
|-------------|-------------|------------|
| **Structural valve deterioration (bioprosthetic)** | Gradual return of symptoms, new murmur, increased gradients on echo | Reintervention (reoperation or valve-in-valve TAVI for aortic) |
| **Valve thrombosis** | Acute dyspnea, embolic event, new murmur, increased gradients, impaired leaflet motion on echo | VKA optimization; fibrinolysis if hemodynamically unstable; surgery if refractory |
| **Prosthetic valve endocarditis** | Fever + new murmur + embolic phenomena; positive blood cultures (Staphylococcus, Streptococcus, Enterococcus) | Antibiotics (6 weeks); surgery if HF, uncontrolled infection, large vegetations, extension (abscess, fistula) |
| **Paravalvular leak** | Hemolysis (anemia, schistocytes, elevated LDH, low haptoglobin), new murmur | Reoperation if severe hemolysis, HF, or significant leak; transcatheter closure in selected cases |
| **Patient-prosthesis mismatch** | Persistent symptoms despite "successful" surgery; elevated gradients | Prevention (calculate projected effective orifice area at time of surgery); medical management; reoperation if severe |

### 5.5.3 Surveillance

| Timing | Action |
|--------|--------|
| Post-implantation (before discharge) | Baseline transthoracic echocardiogram (gradients, valve function, ventricular function) |
| Annually | Clinical evaluation; echocardiography for bioprosthetic valves (assess for structural deterioration) |
| If new symptoms | Urgent echocardiography; evaluate for valve dysfunction, thrombosis, endocarditis, paravalvular leak |
| Ongoing | INR monitoring for mechanical valves (target TTR >70%); patient education on endocarditis prophylaxis |

**Endocarditis Prophylaxis:**
- Recommended only for patients with prosthetic valves (mechanical or bioprosthetic), prior infective endocarditis, unrepaired cyanotic congenital heart disease, or cardiac transplant recipients with valvulopathy
- Prophylaxis indicated only for dental procedures involving manipulation of gingival tissue or periapical region, or perforation of oral mucosa
- Single dose of amoxicillin 2 g PO (or ampicillin 2 g IV/IM) 30-60 minutes before procedure; clindamycin 600 mg if penicillin-allergic
- NOT recommended for genitourinary or gastrointestinal procedures solely for endocarditis prophylaxis

## 5.6 Red Flags

> **EMERGENCY INDICATORS - SEEK IMMEDIATE CARE:**
> - Syncope in aortic stenosis (indicates critical stenosis with high risk of sudden death; urgent valve intervention needed)
> - Acute severe MR (papillary muscle rupture post-MI): Flash pulmonary edema, new holosystolic murmur, hemodynamic collapse
> - Acute severe AR (aortic dissection, endocarditis): Sudden dyspnea, hypotension, wide pulse pressure, new diastolic murmur
> - Prosthetic valve thrombosis: Sudden onset dyspnea, embolic event (stroke, limb ischemia), new murmur, increased gradients on echo
> - Infective endocarditis: Fever + new murmur + peripheral embolic phenomena (Janeway lesions, Osler nodes, Roth spots, splinter hemorrhages)
> - Aortic dissection with AR: Tearing chest pain radiating to back, pulse/BP differential, new neurologic deficits

## 5.7 When to Refer

| Scenario | Timing | Referral |
|----------|--------|----------|
| Severe AS (any etiology) | Urgent (within 1-2 weeks if asymptomatic; emergent if symptomatic) | Cardiac surgery or TAVI team (Heart Team evaluation) |
| Moderate AS with symptoms | Semi-urgent | Cardiology (exercise testing, dobutamine echo) |
| Severe AR with symptoms or LV dysfunction | Urgent | Cardiac surgery |
| Severe primary MR | Urgent (symptomatic) to semi-urgent (asymptomatic with LV dilation) | Cardiac surgery (valve repair specialist) |
| Secondary MR on optimal GDMT | Elective after GDMT optimization | Structural interventional cardiology (TEER evaluation) or cardiac surgery |
| Prosthetic valve dysfunction | Urgent | Cardiac surgery |
| Suspected endocarditis | Urgent (within 24 hours) | Cardiology, infectious disease, cardiac surgery |
| Bicuspid aortic valve with ascending aorta dilation | Semi-urgent | Cardiac surgery (aortic root replacement if ascending aorta >5.5 cm, or >5.0 cm if risk factors) |

# Chapter 6: Chest Pain Evaluation

## 6.1 Overview

Chest pain accounts for approximately 6-8 million emergency department visits annually in the United States, making it the second most common complaint in US emergency departments. The primary clinical goal is to rapidly identify life-threatening causes - acute coronary syndrome (ACS), aortic dissection, pulmonary embolism, tension pneumothorax, cardiac tamponade, and esophageal rupture - while avoiding unnecessary admissions, testing, and radiation exposure for low-risk patients. A structured, evidence-based approach using validated risk scores is essential for safe and efficient care [^20^] [^31^].

**Key Principles:**
1. Not all chest pain is cardiac; not all cardiac chest pain presents as "pain" (may be pressure, tightness, burning, indigestion)
2. Atypical presentations are common in women, elderly, and diabetics
3. A normal ECG does NOT exclude ACS (especially early in presentation)
4. A single negative troponin does NOT exclude MI (serial testing required)
5. Use validated risk scores to guide disposition decisions
6. Document shared decision-making for intermediate-risk patients

## 6.2 HEART Score [^20^] [^31^]

The HEART score is the most widely validated risk stratification tool for chest pain patients in the emergency department. It was designed specifically for undifferentiated chest pain (not just ACS-suspected patients) and has been prospectively validated in multiple international cohorts.

### 6.2.1 Components

| Component | 0 Points | 1 Point | 2 Points | Rationale |
|-----------|----------|---------|----------|-----------|
| **H**istory | Slightly or non-suspicious for ACS | Moderately suspicious for ACS | Highly suspicious for ACS | Typical chest pressure, radiation, diaphoresis, nausea = 2 points; atypical but possibly cardiac = 1 point; clearly non-cardiac = 0 points |
| **E**CG | Normal | Nonspecific repolarization disturbance (ST depression <0.5 mm, T-wave inversion <2 mm, LVH, LBBB, paced rhythm) | Significant ST depression >=0.5 mm or elevation >=1 mm | Degree of ECG abnormality correlates with acute ischemia |
| **A**ge | <=45 years | >45 to <65 years | >=65 years | Age is the strongest independent predictor of CAD |
| **R**isk factors | No known risk factors | 1-2 risk factors (hypertension, diabetes, smoking, family history of premature CAD, hyperlipidemia, obesity) | >=3 risk factors OR history of atherosclerotic disease (CAD, stroke, PAD) | Cumulative risk factor burden |
| **T**roponin | <=Normal limit (below 99th percentile) | 1-3x normal limit (slightly elevated) | >=3x normal limit (markedly elevated) | Troponin elevation indicates myocardial injury |

### 6.2.2 Risk Stratification and Management

| Score | Risk Category | 6-Week MACE Rate* | Recommended Management |
|-------|--------------|-------------------|----------------------|
| **0-3** | Low risk | 1.7% (0.9-1.7%) | Safe for early discharge or outpatient management; arrange primary care follow-up within 72 hours; provide return precautions |
| **4-6** | Moderate risk | 16.6% (12.3-20.3%) | Observation unit admission; serial troponins (0, 3, 6 hours from presentation); functional testing (stress ECG, stress echo, coronary CTA) before discharge |
| **7-10** | High risk | 50.1% (41.4-63.6%) | Immediate cardiology consultation; admission; invasive management likely (coronary angiography) |

*MACE = Major Adverse Cardiovascular Events (death, myocardial infarction, need for urgent revascularization)

### 6.2.3 HEART Pathway

The HEART pathway combines the HEART score with serial troponins at 0 and 3 hours (using contemporary high-sensitivity troponin assays):

- **HEART score 0-3 AND 0-hour and 3-hour troponins both negative:** Safe for discharge without stress testing (0.9% MACE at 30 days)
- This approach has been shown to reduce cardiac observation unit admissions by 20-40% without missing MACE
- Reduces healthcare costs, length of stay, and patient radiation exposure

### 6.2.4 Key Points and Limitations
- Pooled sensitivity: 96%; specificity: 50% [^20^]
- Superior to GRACE score for predicting MACE in undifferentiated chest pain [^20^]
- The HEART score should NOT be used as the sole determinant of disposition; clinical judgment remains essential
- Low-risk patients (score 0-3) with 0-hour troponin below assay threshold may be safely discharged with close follow-up and explicit return precautions
- Patients with score 0-3 but elevated troponin (>=1 point) should be admitted for serial testing
- The score does NOT apply to patients with known CAD who present with typical angina (these patients should be evaluated for ACS regardless of score)
- The score may underestimate risk in women (who more commonly present with atypical features)

## 6.3 TIMI Score [^20^]

The TIMI risk score was originally designed for NSTEMI/UA patients enrolled in clinical trials. It is less sensitive for all-comer chest pain compared to HEART but remains useful for risk stratification within the ACS population.

| Risk Factor | Points |
|-------------|--------|
| Age >=65 years | 1 |
| >=3 CAD risk factors (hypertension, diabetes, smoking, family history, hyperlipidemia) | 1 |
| Known CAD (stenosis >=50%) | 1 |
| Aspirin use in past 7 days (suggests refractory symptoms despite antiplatelet therapy) | 1 |
| Severe angina (>=2 episodes in 24 hours) | 1 |
| ST deviation >=0.5 mm on ECG | 1 |
| Elevated cardiac markers (troponin) | 1 |

| Score | Risk Category | 14-Day Event Rate (Death/MI/Urgent Revascularization) |
|-------|--------------|-----------------------------------------------------|
| 0-2 | Low risk | 4.7% |
| 3-4 | Intermediate risk | 19.2% |
| 5-7 | High risk | 41.1% |

**Clinical Use:** The TIMI score is primarily used within the ACS population to guide intensity of antithrombotic therapy and timing of invasive strategy. It is less useful than HEART for undifferentiated chest pain because it requires known CAD history and aspirin use as variables.

## 6.4 Additional Risk Scores

### 6.4.1 GRACE Score
- More comprehensive than TIMI; includes vital signs, renal function, Killip class
- Originally designed for ACS patients (not undifferentiated chest pain)
- Predicts in-hospital and 6-month mortality
- Inferior to HEART for MACE prediction in undifferentiated chest pain [^20^]
- Best used for guiding invasive strategy timing in confirmed ACS (see Chapter 3)

### 6.4.2 EDACS (Emergency Department Assessment of Chest Pain Score)
- Designed to identify low-risk patients safe for early discharge
- Uses 13 variables including age, sex, risk factors, pain characteristics
- A score <16 combined with negative troponin identifies low-risk patients
- Less widely used than HEART but validated in multiple cohorts

## 6.5 Differential Diagnosis Framework

### 6.5.1 Life-Threatening Causes (Must Not Miss)

| Priority | Condition | Key Clinical Features | Key Diagnostic Test | Time-Critical Action |
|----------|-----------|----------------------|---------------------|---------------------|
| **1** | **Acute Coronary Syndrome** (STEMI/NSTEMI/UA) | Pressure/crushing substernal pain, radiation to arm/jaw/back, diaphoresis, nausea, dyspnea; may be atypical in women/elderly/diabetics | Serial troponins (0, 3, 6h), ECG (look for ST changes), coronary CTA or invasive angiography | STEMI: Door-to-balloon <90 min; NSTEMI: Risk stratify and treat based on GRACE |
| **2** | **Aortic Dissection** | "Tearing" or "ripping" pain radiating to back, BP differential >20 mmHg between arms, pulse deficits (subclavian, carotid, femoral), new neurologic deficits (stroke from carotid involvement, paraplegia from spinal cord ischemia), syncope | CTA chest/abdomen/pelvis (gold standard); TEE (bedside, evaluates aortic valve); D-dimer may be very elevated | Immediate IV beta-blockade (labetalol or esmolol) BEFORE vasodilators; emergent surgery (Type A) or TEVAR/endovascular (Type B with complications) |
| **3** | **Pulmonary Embolism** | Pleuritic chest pain, dyspnea, tachypnea (>20), tachycardia (>100), risk factors for DVT (recent surgery, immobilization, cancer, hormonal therapy), syncope (indicates massive PE), hemoptysis | Wells score, D-dimer (if low-intermediate probability), CTPA (gold standard), V/Q scan (if CTPA contraindicated), lower extremity ultrasound | Anticoagulation; systemic thrombolysis if massive (hypotension); embolectomy if contraindicated |
| **4** | **Tension Pneumothorax** | Sudden unilateral pleuritic pain, severe respiratory distress, hypotension, tracheal deviation AWAY from affected side, absent breath sounds, hyperresonance, JVD | Clinical diagnosis (do NOT wait for imaging if suspected); confirmatory CXR shows mediastinal shift | **Immediate needle decompression** (14-gauge angiocatheter, 2nd intercostal space midclavicular line on affected side) followed by chest tube |
| **5** | **Cardiac Tamponade** | Beck's triad (hypotension, JVD, muffled heart sounds - present in only 10-40%), pulsus paradoxus >10 mmHg (exaggerated drop in SBP with inspiration), dyspnea, fatigue; may have history of malignancy, tuberculosis, autoimmune disease, recent cardiac procedure | Bedside transthoracic echocardiography (pericardial effusion with RV collapse, respiratory variation in mitral/tricuspid inflow) | **Urgent pericardiocentesis** (echocardiography-guided preferred; if hemodynamically unstable, perform blindly or via ED resuscitative thoracotomy); pericardial window if recurrent/loculated |
| **6** | **Esophageal Rupture (Boerhaave Syndrome)** | Severe chest/upper abdominal pain after forceful vomiting (retching without vomiting), subcutaneous emphysema (crepitus in neck/chest), Hamman crunch (mediastinal crackle with heartbeat), fever, sepsis, mediastinal air on CXR | Contrast esophagram (water-soluble contrast first; if negative but high suspicion, use barium); CT chest with oral contrast (mediastinal air, fluid, contrast extravasation) | Broad-spectrum antibiotics; NPO; emergent surgical repair (high mortality if >24 hours from rupture); drainage |

### 6.5.2 Non-Life-Threatening Causes

| Category | Conditions | Distinguishing Features | Diagnostic Approach |
|----------|-----------|------------------------|---------------------|
| **Cardiac** | Stable angina, myocardialitis, pericarditis, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM), mitral valve prolapse, coronary vasospasm (Prinzmetal angina) | Pericarditis: sharp pleuritic pain, worse supine better leaning forward, friction rub, diffuse ST elevation, PR depression; HCM: harsh systolic murmur at LLSB radiating to apex, increased with Valsalva/decreased with squatting, syncope with exertion; Myocarditis: recent viral illness, diffuse ST changes, elevated troponin with non-obstructive coronaries | ECG, troponin, echocardiogram, cardiac MRI (for myocarditis), coronary angiography (to exclude obstructive CAD) |
| **Pulmonary** | Pneumonia, pneumothorax (simple), pleurisy, COPD/asthma exacerbation, pulmonary hypertension, interstitial lung disease | Fever, cough, sputum production, focal findings on auscultation (crackles, egophony, bronchial breath sounds), pleuritic pain worsened by inspiration | CXR, CT chest, sputum culture, spirometry |
| **Gastrointestinal** | GERD, esophageal spasm (nutcracker esophagus), peptic ulcer disease, biliary colic, pancreatitis, esophageal foreign body | Relation to meals, relief with antacids, epigastric tenderness, burning quality, radiation to back (pancreatitis), right upper quadrant pain (biliary), dysphagia (esophageal) | Trial of PPI for GERD; upper endoscopy for esophageal spasm/ulcer; RUQ ultrasound for biliary; lipase for pancreatitis |
| **Musculoskeletal** | Costochondritis (Tietze syndrome), rib fracture, muscle strain, slipping rib syndrome, thoracic spine pathology | Reproducible chest wall tenderness (diagnostic of musculoskeletal cause if present); pain worsens with movement, deep breathing, or specific positions; history of trauma or overuse | Physical exam (palpate costochondral junctions, rib cage); chest X-ray if fracture suspected |
| **Psychogenic** | Panic attack, anxiety disorder, somatization | Paresthesias (perioral and digital), hyperventilation, chest tightness with normal vitals, sense of doom, history of anxiety, normal cardiac workup, response to reassurance/anxiolytics | Diagnosis of exclusion after ruling out cardiac/pulmonary causes; GAD-7 or PHQ-9 screening |
| **Other** | Herpes zoster (pre-eruptive), breast pathology (mastitis, abscess), sickle cell crisis (acute chest syndrome), fibromyalgia | Vesicular rash in dermatomal distribution (herpes zoster appears days after pain begins), breast mass/tenderness, history of sickle cell disease with chest pain/fever/hypoxia/new infiltrate | Clinical diagnosis for zoster; breast ultrasound/mammography for breast pathology; CXR for acute chest syndrome |

| Feature | ACS | PE | Aortic Dissection | Pericarditis | Pneumothorax |
|---------|-----|-----|-------------------|-------------|-------------|
| **Pain quality** | Pressure, squeezing, heaviness, tightness | Pleuritic, sharp, stabbing | Tearing, ripping, "worst ever" | Sharp, stabbing, pleuritic | Sudden, sharp, pleuritic |
| **Pain location** | Substernal, epigastric | Pleuritic, lateral chest | Anterior chest radiating to back, interscapular | Substernal, precordial | Unilateral, lateral |
| **Radiation** | Left arm, jaw, neck, back, right arm | None (may have pleuritic shoulder pain from diaphragmatic irritation) | Back, abdomen, neck (carotid dissection) | Trapezius ridge (phrenic nerve irritation) | Ipsilateral shoulder |

| **Vital signs** | Variable (may be normal); diaphoresis | Tachycardia, tachypnea; may have low-grade fever | BP differential between arms >20 mmHg; pulse deficits | Friction rub; pulsus paradoxus if tamponade | Tachycardia; hypotension if tension |

| **Key imaging** | Coronary CTA/angiography | CTPA | CTA chest (sensitivity and specificity >95%) | Echocardiogram (effusion, tamponade) | Upright CXR (visible visceral pleural line); CT if uncertain |

## 6.6 Clinical Decision Pathways

### 6.6.1 For Emergency Department Clinicians

1. **First 10 minutes:** ECG, vitals, targeted history and physical
   - If STEMI on ECG: Activate catheterization lab immediately
   - If hemodynamically unstable: Resuscitate first; consider tamponade, tension pneumothorax, massive PE, aortic dissection
2. **First 30 minutes:** Troponin, CXR, basic labs (CBC, BMP)
   - Calculate HEART score
   - Consider PERC/Wells for PE if presentation suggests it
3. **Serial troponins:** At 0, 3, and 6 hours from symptom onset (or from arrival if symptom onset time unclear)
   - Use high-sensitivity troponin assays if available (allow earlier rule-out with 0/1-hour or 0/2-hour protocols)
4. **Risk stratification:**
   - HEART 0-3 + negative serial troponins: Consider discharge with close follow-up
   - HEART 4-6: Observation + stress test or coronary CTA
   - HEART 7-10: Admission + cardiology consultation
5. **Functional testing** (for intermediate-risk patients): Exercise stress test, pharmacologic stress with imaging, or coronary CTA
   - Coronary CTA preferred for low-intermediate risk patients (excellent negative predictive value; radiation comparable to nuclear stress test)
   - Stress testing preferred if known CAD (assesses ischemia) or renal insufficiency (avoids contrast)

### 6.6.2 For Primary Care / Outpatient Settings

1. **Immediate ECG** for any patient presenting with chest pain to outpatient clinic
2. **If ECG shows STEMI or patient is hemodynamically unstable:** Call EMS immediately; do NOT attempt to transport patient in private vehicle
3. **If stable and low suspicion:** Office-based evaluation with careful history, physical exam, risk factor assessment
4. **If intermediate suspicion or risk factors:** Urgent ED referral (same day) for serial troponins and risk stratification
5. **If very low suspicion:** Outpatient stress testing or coronary CTA within 72 hours for patients with risk factors
6. **Do NOT attribute chest pain to anxiety without first excluding cardiac causes** in patients with risk factors or atypical presentations

## 6.7 Red Flags

> **IMMEDIATE EMERGENCY ACTIVATION REQUIRED - CALL EMS:**
> - STEMI on ECG (ST elevation >=1 mm in 2+ contiguous leads, or new LBBB with symptoms, or posterior MI with ST depression in V1-V3 with tall R waves)
> - Hemodynamic instability (SBP <90 mmHg, altered mental status, signs of shock)
> - Suspected aortic dissection with pulse/BP differential or new neurologic deficits
> - Tension pneumothorax (immediate needle decompression before imaging - do NOT wait for X-ray)
> - Cardiac tamponade (Beck's triad, pulsus paradoxus >10 mmHg)
> - Esophageal rupture (subcutaneous emphysema, severe pain post-emesis, Hamman crunch)
> - Chest pain with syncope (consider PE, VT, aortic stenosis, HCM)
> - Chest pain in patient with known severe aortic stenosis (could represent acute coronary syndrome or worsening AS)

## 6.8 When to Seek Emergency Care (Patient-Facing Guidance)

Call emergency services immediately if you experience:
- Chest pressure, squeezing, fullness, or pain that lasts more than a few minutes or comes and goes
- Chest discomfort that spreads to the shoulders, neck, jaw, back, or arms
- Chest discomfort accompanied by shortness of breath, sweating, nausea, vomiting, or lightheadedness
- Sudden severe chest pain unlike anything experienced before
- Chest pain that occurs at rest or wakes you from sleep
- Fainting or near-fainting associated with chest discomfort
- Chest pain after significant trauma
- Chest pain with known heart disease that is not relieved by rest or prescribed nitroglycerin within 5 minutes

Do NOT drive yourself to the hospital if you are experiencing chest pain. Call emergency services so that treatment can begin in transit if needed.

# Chapter 7: Asthma

## 7.1 Overview

Asthma is a chronic inflammatory disorder of the airways characterized by variable symptoms of wheeze, shortness of breath, chest tightness, and cough, together with variable expiratory airflow limitation. The inflammatory process involves mast cells, eosinophils, T-lymphocytes, macrophages, and neutrophils, leading to bronchial hyperresponsiveness, mucus hypersecretion, and in severe or long-standing disease, airway remodeling with basement membrane thickening and smooth muscle hypertrophy. Asthma affects approximately 262 million people globally and causes approximately 450,000 deaths annually. Despite availability of effective therapies, asthma remains poorly controlled in a significant proportion of patients. The GINA (Global Initiative for Asthma) 2024 guidelines represent the current standard of care and have introduced a paradigm shift in treatment approach [^21^] [^65^].

**Pathophysiology:** Most asthma (approximately 70-80%) is driven by Type 2 (T2) inflammation, characterized by elevated levels of IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13, leading to IgE production, eosinophilic infiltration, mucus hypersecretion, and bronchial hyperresponsiveness. T2-high asthma can be further classified as allergic (elevated IgE, positive skin tests, early onset) and eosinophilic (elevated blood/sputum eosinophils). Non-T2 (neutrophilic or paucigranulocytic) asthma is less responsive to corticosteroids and represents an area of unmet need. T2-low asthma may be driven by IL-17, TNF-alpha, or other non-T2 pathways.

**Epidemiology:** Asthma affects approximately 8% of adults in the US (25 million people), with higher prevalence in children (9.4%). Risk factors include atopy, family history, obesity, urbanization, allergen exposure, respiratory infections in early life, and occupational exposures. Asthma mortality has declined in developed countries but remains significant, particularly among Black Americans and those with severe disease.

## 7.2 Diagnosis

### 7.2.1 Clinical Features
- Episodic symptoms: Wheeze (expiratory, musical), shortness of breath, chest tightness, cough (especially nocturnal or early morning, exercise-induced, or with laughter)
- Symptoms vary over time and in intensity, with periods of exacerbation and remission
- Triggers: Exercise, allergens (dust mites, pollen, pet dander, mold), respiratory infections (viral, particularly rhinovirus), cold air, irritants (tobacco smoke, strong odors, air pollution), emotions/stress, medications (aspirin/NSAIDs in AERD, beta-blockers), gastroesophageal reflux
- Personal or family history of atopy (eczema, allergic rhinitis, food allergies)
- Seasonal variation in symptoms (suggestive of allergic triggers)

### 7.2.2 Objective Confirmation
Diagnosis requires **documented variable expiratory airflow limitation** - this is essential and distinguishes asthma from COPD and other causes of dyspnea:

| Test | Diagnostic Criterion | Notes |
|------|---------------------|-------|
| **Spirometry with bronchodilator reversibility** | FEV1 increases >12% and >200 mL from baseline after inhaled SABA (400 mcg albuterol/salbutamol) | Most commonly used test; perform when patient symptomatic or after withholding bronchodilators; post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC <0.70 indicates obstruction |
| **Peak Expiratory Flow (PEF) variability** | Excessive variability: diurnal variation >10% (twice daily over 2 weeks) or improvement >20% after bronchodilator or >20% diurnal variation on >3 days in a week | Useful for monitoring; less reliable than spirometry; PEF meters vary in accuracy |
| **Bronchoprovocation testing** | PC20 methacholine <8 mg/mL (or <16 mg/mL if pre-test probability intermediate); or positive exercise challenge (FEV1 drop >=10% after 6-8 minutes of exercise) | Used when baseline spirometry is normal but asthma strongly suspected; contraindicated if FEV1 <70% predicted |
| **Fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO)** | >=20 ppb supports T2 inflammation (eosinophilic/allergic asthma); >=50 ppb strongly suggests steroid-responsive asthma | Adjunct test; NOT diagnostic on its own; influenced by smoking (reduces levels), atopy, recent respiratory infection; useful for phenotyping and monitoring ICS response |

**Spirometric Criteria:**
- FEV1/FVC < lower limit of normal (<0.70 in adults; use LLN for children to avoid overdiagnosis)
- Significant bronchodilator response: FEV1 increase >12% and >200 mL (highly suggestive of asthma)
- Degree of airflow limitation determines severity: FEV1 >80% predicted = mild; 60-80% = moderate; <60% = severe obstruction

> **Important:** A normal spirometry does NOT exclude asthma. Asthma is variable by definition, and patients may have normal lung function between exacerbations. If clinical suspicion is high and spirometry is normal, proceed to bronchoprovocation testing or repeat spirometry during symptomatic periods.

### 7.2.3 Differential Diagnosis

| Condition | Distinguishing Features | Key Test |
|-----------|------------------------|----------|
| **COPD** | Age >40, smoking history, persistent (not variable) airflow limitation, poor bronchodilator response, emphysema on CT | Spirometry (post-BD FEV1/FVC <0.70 with limited reversibility), CT chest |
| **Heart failure** | Orthopnea, JVD, peripheral edema, S3 gallop, BNP elevation | Echocardiogram, BNP/NT-proBNP |
| **Vocal cord dysfunction** | Inspiratory stridor, throat tightness, voice changes, normal spirometry, symptoms out of proportion to objective findings | Laryngoscopy (paradoxical vocal cord adduction during inspiration) |
| **Central airway obstruction** | Monophonic wheeze (not polyphonic), fixed airflow limitation on flow-volume loop | CT chest, bronchoscopy |
| **Bronchiectasis** | Chronic productive cough, hemoptysis, recurrent infections, coarse crackles | CT chest (bronchial dilation), sputum culture |
| **Allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA)** | Central bronchiectasis, fleeting infiltrates, elevated IgE, eosinophilia, positive Aspergillus-specific IgE/IgG | CT chest, specific IgE to Aspergillus, total IgE |
| **Eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA)** | Asthma + eosinophilia + systemic vasculitis (neuropathy, skin lesions, renal involvement) | ANCA (p-ANCA/MPO positive in 40%), tissue biopsy |
| **Hyperventilation syndrome** | Paresthesias, anxiety, normal examination and spirometry, symptoms reproduced with voluntary hyperventilation | Diagnosis of exclusion |
| **Cystic fibrosis** | Recurrent infections, pancreatic insufficiency, digital clubbing, positive newborn screen | Sweat chloride test (>60 mmol/L), CFTR genetic testing |

## 7.3 GINA 2024 Stepwise Therapy [^21^] [^65^]

> **Critical Update (GINA 2024): SABA-only reliever therapy is NO LONGER recommended as Step 1 for adults and adolescents.** All patients with asthma should use ICS-containing inhalers. This paradigm shift was driven by overwhelming evidence that as-needed ICS-formoterol reduces severe exacerbations by 60-64% compared to SABA-only, while also reducing mortality. SABA-only treatment is associated with increased risk of exacerbations and death due to undertreated airway inflammation.

### 7.3.1 Treatment Steps (Adults and Adolescents >=12 years)

| Step | Controller | Reliever | Clinical Scenario | Key Evidence |
|------|-----------|----------|-------------------|-------------|
| **1** | Low-dose ICS-formoterol taken **as-needed only** (no daily maintenance) | Same inhaler (as-needed only) | Mild/occasional symptoms; typically <2 days/week; no exacerbation history | COMPASS trial: As-needed budesonide-formoterol comparable to daily ICS + SABA for exacerbation prevention |
| **2** | Low-dose ICS-formoterol maintenance (1-2 puffs BID) + as-needed reliever | Same inhaler (as-needed) | Persistent symptoms requiring daily controller; previous exacerbation | Maintain daily ICS for persistent symptoms |
| **3** | Low-dose ICS-formoterol maintenance and reliever (SMART/MART) - same inhaler for both | Same inhaler (as-needed reliever) | Maintenance + reliever in one device; reduces exacerbations vs. separate reliever | SMART trials: 30-50% reduction in severe exacerbations |
| **4** | Medium-dose ICS-formoterol maintenance and reliever (SMART) | Same inhaler (as-needed) | Poor control on Step 3; ongoing symptoms or exacerbations | Increase ICS dose; evaluate adherence and technique |
| **5** | High-dose ICS-formoterol + LAMA + **biologic** + maintenance oral corticosteroids if needed | Same inhaler (as-needed) | Severe asthma; refer to specialist | Biologic selection based on phenotype (see below) |

### 7.3.2 SMART/MART (Single Maintenance and Reliever Therapy)
- Uses a single inhaler containing low-dose ICS-formoterol as both daily controller and as-needed reliever
- Key advantage: Every reliever use delivers anti-inflammatory medication
- Reduces severe exacerbations by 30-50% compared to traditional higher-dose ICS/LABA maintenance + SABA reliever
- Simplifies regimen (one inhaler for both purposes), potentially improving adherence
- NOT applicable to ICS/salmeterol or other non-formoterol combinations (salmeterol has slow onset and is not suitable for reliever use)
- Evidence base: COMPASS, SMILE, STAY, and other trials support SMART as preferred approach for Steps 3-5

### 7.3.3 Track 1 (Preferred) vs. Track 2 (Alternative)

| | Track 1 (Preferred) - Anti-Inflammatory Reliever | Track 2 (Alternative) - SABA Reliever + Daily ICS |
|--|--------------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------|
| **Reliever** | As-needed low-dose ICS-formoterol | SABA (albuterol/salbutamol) |
| **Advantage** | Anti-inflammatory reliever; lower exacerbation risk; simpler regimen | Familiar to patients and providers; lower cost in some settings |
| **Step 1** | As-needed low-dose ICS-formoterol only | SABA + daily low-dose ICS (separate inhalers) |
| **Step 2** | Daily low-dose ICS-formoterol + as-needed | SABA + daily low-dose ICS |
| **Step 3** | Low-dose ICS-formoterol SMART (maintenance + reliever) | SABA + daily medium-dose ICS/LABA |
| **Step 4** | Medium-dose ICS-formoterol SMART | SABA + daily high-dose ICS/LABA |
| **Step 5** | High-dose ICS-formoterol + LAMA + biologic | SABA + high-dose ICS/LABA + LAMA + biologic |

> **Key Message:** Regardless of Track, patients should NOT use SABA alone without ICS. Every patient prescribed SABA for rescue should also be on regular ICS. If a patient uses SABA >2 times per week (excluding pre-exercise), this indicates inadequate control and the need to step up therapy.

### 7.3.4 Step-Up and Step-Down
- **Step up** if: symptoms on >=2 days/week, any night waking due to asthma, any exacerbation requiring oral corticosteroids, or reliever use >2 times/week (excluding pre-exercise)
- **Step down** after 3 months of good control: Reduce ICS dose by 25-50% every 3 months to lowest effective dose
- Do NOT completely discontinue ICS in patients who required it for control (risk of rebound exacerbations)

## 7.4 Pharmacologic Agents

### 7.4.1 Controller Medications

| Class | Examples | Mechanism | Key Notes |
|-------|----------|-----------|-----------|
| **Inhaled corticosteroids (ICS)** | Budesonide (Pulmicort), fluticasone propionate (Flovent), fluticasone furoate (Arnuity), mometasone (Asmanex), beclomethasone (Qvar) | Suppress airway inflammation; reduce eosinophils, mast cells, mucus production; decrease bronchial hyperresponsiveness | Cornerstone of asthma therapy; most effective anti-inflammatory; onset of benefit 1-2 weeks; rinse mouth after use to prevent oral candidiasis and dysphonia; use spacer with MDI |
| **ICS/LABA combinations** | Budesonide-formoterol (Symbicort), fluticasone-salmeterol (Advair), fluticasone-vilanterol (Breo), mometasone-indacaterol | Anti-inflammatory + long-acting bronchodilation | Formoterol has rapid onset (1-3 minutes) allowing use as reliever; salmeterol onset 15-30 minutes (not suitable as reliever); LABA should never be used without ICS (safety signal from SMART trial) |
| **LAMA** | Tiotropium (Spiriva Respimat), glycopyrrolate | Long-acting anticholinergic bronchodilation | Add-on for Steps 4-5; LABA-naive patients may use as add-on to ICS; improves lung function and reduces exacerbations; NOT a substitute for ICS |
| **LTRA** | Montelukast (Singulair) | Leukotriene receptor antagonist; blocks bronchoconstriction and inflammation | Alternative controller (less effective than ICS); once-daily oral dosing; FDA black box warning for neuropsychiatric effects (agitation, depression, suicidal ideation - rare but serious); use if ICS intolerance or preference for oral therapy |
| **Theophylline** | Various formulations | Phosphodiesterase inhibition; weak bronchodilator and anti-inflammatory | Third-line only; narrow therapeutic index (therapeutic range 5-15 mcg/mL); multiple drug interactions (CYP1A2); requires serum level monitoring; toxicity includes seizures, arrhythmias |
| **Systemic corticosteroids** | Prednisone, prednisolone, methylprednisolone | Potent anti-inflammatory | For acute exacerbations (see below); maintenance low-dose prednisone (2.5-10 mg daily) only in severe asthma despite biologics; significant adverse effects with chronic use |

### 7.4.2 Reliever Medications

| Class | Examples | Role | Key Notes |
|-------|----------|------|-----------|
| **ICS-formoterol (as-needed)** | Budesonide-formoterol 100/6 or 200/6 mcg | Preferred reliever (Track 1) | Anti-inflammatory reliever; reduces exacerbations vs. SABA; used as both controller and reliever in SMART |
| **SABA** | Albuterol/salbutamol (Ventolin, ProAir), levalbuterol (Xopenex) | Rescue only (Track 2) | Rapid onset (5 minutes); duration 4-6 hours; should never be sole therapy; overuse (>2 canisters/month or >200 puffs/month) indicates poor control and increased mortality risk |
| **SAMA** | Ipratropium bromide (Atrovent) | Acute exacerbations | Added to SABA in severe exacerbations (emergency department); anticholinergic mechanism complements beta-agonist |

## 7.5 Biologics for Severe Asthma [^53^] [^57^] [^60^] [^64^]

Severe asthma is defined as asthma that remains uncontrolled despite high-dose ICS-LABA (Step 4) or that requires maintenance oral corticosteroids to prevent uncontrolled disease. Severe asthma affects approximately 3-10% of all asthma patients but accounts for disproportionate healthcare utilization (50% of asthma-related costs). Before escalating to biologics, confirm:
1. Correct diagnosis (exclude mimics: VCD, bronchiectasis, CHF, COPD)
2. Adequate inhaler technique (observe patient using their device)
3. Good adherence (pharmacy refill data, direct questioning)
4. Management of comorbidities (rhinosinusitis, obesity, GERD, anxiety, OSA)
5. Elimination of ongoing allergen/trigger exposure

### 7.5.1 Biologic Comparison Table

| Biologic | Target | Mechanism | Phenotype | Age | Dosing | Key Considerations |
|----------|--------|-----------|-----------|-----|--------|--------------------|
| **Omalizumab** (Xolair) | Anti-IgE (monoclonal antibody) | Neutralizes free IgE; downregulates Fc-epsilon-RI on mast cells and basophils | Allergic asthma (elevated IgE 30-1500 IU/mL, positive skin prick test or specific IgE to aeroallergens) | >=6 years | SC q2-4 weeks (dosing based on weight and baseline IgE level) | First biologic approved for asthma; anaphylaxis risk 0.1-0.2% (observe 2 hours after first 3 injections, then 30 minutes); carry epinephrine auto-injector; may reduce allergic rhinitis symptoms; onset 4-8 weeks |

| **Depemokimab** | Anti-IL-5 (monoclonal antibody) | Same as mepolizumab but ultra-long-acting (higher binding affinity, modified Fc region) | Eosinophilic asthma | Adults | SC q6 months | 2025 FDA approval; ultra-long-acting convenience; first asthma biologic with twice-yearly dosing; may improve adherence |

### 7.5.2 Biologic Selection Algorithm [^53^]

| Phenotype | First-Line Biologic | Alternatives | Rationale |
|-----------|-------------------|-------------|-----------|
| **T2-high allergic asthma** (early onset <40 years, elevated IgE, positive skin test/RAST to aeroallergens, comorbid allergic rhinitis/atopic dermatitis) | Omalizumab | Dupilumab, Tezepelumab | Omalizumab targets IgE-mediated pathway; most cost-effective for this phenotype |
| **T2-high eosinophilic asthma** (blood eosinophils >=150/μL, may or may not have allergies, often adult-onset) | Mepolizumab or Benralizumab | Dupilumab, Tezepelumab, Depemokimab | IL-5 pathway blockade is most specific for eosinophilic inflammation; benralizumab offers most convenient dosing |
| **T2-high with moderate eosinophilia or raised FeNO** (not clearly allergic or eosinophilic) | Dupilumab (dual IL-4/IL-13 blockade) | Tezepelumab, Mepolizumab | Dual blockade addresses both IgE-mediated and eosinophilic pathways |
| **T2-low or mixed phenotype** (normal eosinophils, normal IgE, normal FeNO, corticosteroid-resistant) | Tezepelumab (TSLP blockade works across phenotypes) | Consider non-biologic optimization first; may add azithromycin or bronchial thermoplasty | TSLP is upstream of both T2 and non-T2 pathways |
| **Corticosteroid-dependent** (requiring maintenance OCS) | Dupilumab or Tezepelumab (potent steroid-sparing effect) | Benralizumab, Mepolizumab | These biologics have strongest evidence for OCS reduction (75-100% able to eliminate OCS) |
| **All phenotypes - prioritizing convenience** | Depemokimab (q6 months) or Benralizumab (q8 weeks) | | Ultra-long-acting dosing improves adherence and reduces healthcare visits |

### 7.5.3 Response Assessment and Monitoring
- **Assess response at 16-24 weeks** after biologic initiation
- **Targets for adequate response:**
  - >=50% reduction in annualized severe exacerbation rate
  - >=10 ppb reduction in FeNO (if elevated at baseline)
  - >=0.1 L improvement in FEV1
  - Reduction in maintenance oral corticosteroid dose (if applicable)
  - Improvement in ACQ (Asthma Control Questionnaire) or ACT (Asthma Control Test) scores
- **Consider switching biologic class** if inadequate response after appropriate trial (e.g., from anti-IL-5 to anti-IL-4Rα or anti-TSLP)
- **Continue ICS-containing controller** alongside biologic therapy (biologics are add-on, not replacement)

## 7.6 Asthma Exacerbation Management

### 7.6.1 Severity Assessment

| Parameter | Mild | Moderate | Severe | Life-Threatening |
|-----------|------|----------|--------|-----------------|
| **Able to speak** | Complete sentences | Phrases | Single words only | Unable to speak |
| **Respiratory rate** | Increased | Increased | >30/min | Bradypnea (respiratory muscle fatigue) |
| **Heart rate** | Increased | 100-120 bpm | >120 bpm | Bradycardia (pre-arrest sign) |
| **PEF** | >80% predicted or personal best | 60-80% | <60% | <33% |
| **SpO2** | >95% on room air | 91-95% | <90% | <90% despite supplemental O2 |
| **Accessory muscle use** | Absent | Usually present | Present, prominent | Paradoxical thoracoabdominal movement (see-saw breathing) |
| **Mental status** | Normal | Normal | Agitated, anxious | Drowsy, confused, altered consciousness |
| **ABG (if obtained)** | Not usually needed | Not usually needed | PaO2 <60 mmHg, PaCO2 normal or low (35-45) | PaO2 <60 mmHg, PaCO2 >45 mmHg (respiratory acidosis with pH <7.35) |

> **Life-threatening features:** Silent chest (no wheeze due to severely reduced airflow - ominous sign), cyanosis, bradycardia, hypotension, exhaustion, altered consciousness, PaCO2 elevation.

### 7.6.2 Treatment (Emergency Department / Acute Care Setting)

1. **Oxygen:** Target SpO2 93-95% (give 2-4 L NC or higher as needed). In patients at risk of hypercapnia (COPD overlap, obesity hypoventilation syndrome, severe asthma with previous hypercapnia), target SpO2 88-92%.

2. **SABA (Short-Acting Beta-Agonist):** Albuterol/salbutamol
   - MDI with spacer: 4-8 puffs every 20 minutes for first hour, then every 1-4 hours as needed
   - Nebulizer: 2.5-5 mg every 20 minutes for first hour
   - Continuous nebulization (10-15 mg/hr) may be used in severe exacerbations
   - MDI with spacer is as effective as nebulizer in most patients and reduces side effects

3. **Systemic corticosteroids:**
   - Prednisone/prednisolone 40-50 mg PO daily for 5-7 days (adults)
   - OR hydrocortisone 100-200 mg IV q6-8h if unable to take oral
   - **No taper needed** for short courses (<=2 weeks)
   - Early administration (within first hour) reduces relapse and hospitalization
   - Equivalent bioavailability of oral and IV corticosteroids

4. **Ipratropium bromide (SAMA):** 0.5 mg nebulized, added to SABA for severe exacerbations
   - Reduces hospitalization rates when added to SABA in first 2-4 hours
   - Dose: 0.5 mg q20min x 3 doses in first hour

5. **Magnesium sulfate:** 2 g IV over 20 minutes
   - For severe exacerbations (FEV1 <25-30% predicted after initial therapy)
   - Bronchodilator effect via calcium channel antagonism and smooth muscle relaxation
   - Reduces hospitalization in severe exacerbations (3MAG trial)

6. **Non-invasive ventilation (BiPAP):** If progressive hypercapnia, exhaustion, or altered mental status
   - Reduces intubation rate and improves gas exchange
   - Settings: IPAP 10-15 cm H2O, EPAP 5 cm H2O, titrate to patient comfort and tidal volume

7. **Intubation and mechanical ventilation:** If progressive respiratory failure despite above measures
   - Use largest possible ETT (8.0 mm or larger) to reduce airway resistance
   - Low tidal volume strategy (6-8 mL/kg ideal body weight)
   - Prolonged expiratory time (decrease respiratory rate, increase I:E ratio)
   - Deep sedation +/- paralysis may be needed to prevent breath stacking
   - Permissive hypercapnia (avoid excessive tidal volumes)

8. **Avoid:** Sedatives (unless intubated), mucolytics, chest physiotherapy (not evidence-based in acute asthma), antibiotics (unless evidence of bacterial infection such as fever with purulent sputum, infiltrate on CXR)

### 7.6.3 Discharge Criteria
- SABA can be spaced to every 4 hours (or as-needed)
- PEF >60-70% personal best or predicted
- Patient can use inhaler correctly (spacer technique verified with direct observation)
- Written asthma action plan provided (green zone/yellow zone/red zone instructions)
- Follow-up arranged within 1 week (primary care or pulmonology)
- Oral corticosteroid prescription for 5-7 days if moderate-severe exacerbation
- Controller medication adjusted/stepped up
- Return precautions reviewed

### 7.6.4 Written Asthma Action Plan (Essential Component)
Every patient with asthma should have a written action plan including:

| Zone | Criteria | Action |
|------|----------|--------|
| **Green (Doing Well)** | No symptoms; PEF >80% personal best; normal activities; sleeping through night | Continue daily controller; use reliever as needed before exercise |
| **Yellow (Getting Worse)** | Cough, wheeze, chest tightness, or shortness of breath; PEF 60-80%; waking at night; needing reliever >2 times/week | Increase reliever use; double ICS dose for 7-14 days; contact healthcare provider within 24-48 hours |
| **Red (Medical Alert)** | Severe symptoms; PEF <60%; cannot speak in full sentences; lips/fingernails blue; no improvement after reliever; difficulty walking | Take reliever immediately; start oral corticosteroids if prescribed; call emergency services |

## 7.7 Special Populations

| Population | Key Considerations |
|-----------|-------------------|
| **Pregnancy** | Uncontrolled asthma poses greater risk to mother and fetus than asthma medications. Budesonide is preferred ICS (most pregnancy safety data - Category B). SABA (albuterol) is safe. LABA (salmeterol, formoterol) is safe. Montelukast and biologics generally considered safe (Category B). Systemic corticosteroids may be used for exacerbations (risk of oral cleft with first-trimester exposure is small and outweighed by benefit). Theophylline NOT recommended. Target excellent control (FEV1 maintained >80% predicted). |
| **Children** | Low-dose ICS first-line; step up cautiously; monitor growth velocity with moderate-high dose ICS (1 cm growth suppression in first year, effect attenuates thereafter); spacers essential for MDI use; nebulizers generally not superior to MDI+spacer; leukotriene receptor antagonists less effective than ICS but better adherence (oral); biologics available >=6 years (omalizumab, mepolizumab, dupilumab); avoid LABA monotherapy. |
| **Elderly** | Higher risk of ICS side effects (pneumonia, osteoporosis, cataracts, glaucoma, skin thinning); use lowest effective ICS dose; check inhaler technique (arthritis, cognitive impairment may affect ability); watch for drug interactions with beta-blockers (may worsen asthma); consider LAMA add-on rather than high-dose ICS; ensure vaccinations (influenza, pneumococcal). |
| **Aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease (AERD) / Samter's Triad** | Classic triad: asthma + nasal polyps + sensitivity to aspirin/COX-1 inhibitors. Presents with severe bronchoconstriction, rhinorrhea, flushing after aspirin/NSAID ingestion. Avoid all COX-1 inhibitors (ibuprofen, naproxen, aspirin). COX-2 inhibitors (celecoxib) may be tolerated. Desensitization to daily aspirin (81-325 mg) may be beneficial - reduces polyp recurrence, improves asthma control, but must be performed in monitored setting. Add leukotriene modification (montelukast or 5-lipoxygenase inhibitor). |
| **Occupational asthma** | Caused by workplace sensitizers (isocyanates, flour, wood dust, latex, cleaning agents) or irritants. Key clue: improvement during time away from work (weekends, vacations). Early identification and elimination of exposure are essential (may require job change). Can progress to persistent asthma even after exposure ceases. Legal/compensation implications. |
| **Exercise-induced bronchoconstriction (EIB)** | Bronchospasm during or after exercise; may be only manifestation of asthma. Pre-treatment: SABA or ICS-formoterol 5-15 minutes before exercise. If frequent EIB (>3 times/week), daily controller ICS indicated. Warm-up period may reduce EIB. Cold, dry air worsens EIB (swimming often better tolerated). |
| **Smokers with asthma** | Reduced response to ICS (smoking induces corticosteroid resistance via oxidative stress). Higher dose ICS or LTRA may be needed. Smoking cessation is essential. Increased risk of accelerated lung function decline. |

## 7.8 Red Flags

> **EMERGENCY INDICATORS - SEEK IMMEDIATE CARE:**
> - Life-threatening exacerbation: unable to speak, cyanosis (blue lips/fingernails), silent chest (no audible wheeze due to severely reduced airflow), altered consciousness (confusion, drowsiness), exhaustion
> - PEF <33% predicted or personal best despite initial treatment
> - Rising PaCO2 or falling pH on arterial blood gas (indicates impending respiratory failure and need for mechanical ventilation)
> - Need for mechanical ventilation
> - Pneumothorax complicating asthma (sudden worsening, unilateral decreased breath sounds, pleuritic chest pain)
> - Status asthmaticus (severe asthma persisting >24 hours despite treatment)
> - Cardiac arrhythmias (hypoxia and beta-agonist toxicity can precipitate atrial fibrillation, VT)

## 7.9 Prevention and Long-Term Management

- **Allergen immunotherapy:** Consider for patients with allergic asthma and confirmed sensitization to indoor allergens (dust mites, pet dander, molds) who have incomplete control despite pharmacotherapy; 3-5 year course; reduces symptoms and medication requirements; may prevent progression to more severe asthma
- **Vaccinations:** Annual influenza, pneumococcal (PCV20 or PCV15+PPSV23), COVID-19 as recommended
- **Smoking cessation:** Essential for all smokers with asthma; smoking reduces ICS efficacy and accelerates lung function decline
- **Weight management:** Obesity is associated with worse asthma control; weight loss improves symptoms and lung function
- **Physical activity:** Regular exercise improves asthma control and cardiovascular fitness; pre-treat with reliever if EIB
- **Environmental control:** Reduce indoor allergens (dust mite covers, HEPA filters, humidity control), avoid tobacco smoke exposure, minimize outdoor activity during high pollen/air pollution days
- **Self-management education:** Written asthma action plan, inhaler technique training, PEF monitoring for severe asthma, recognition of deteriorating control

# Chapter 8: COPD

## 8.1 Overview

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a common, preventable, and treatable disease characterized by persistent respiratory symptoms and airflow limitation due to airway and/or alveolar abnormalities, usually caused by significant exposure to noxious particles or gases. The defining feature of COPD is that the airflow limitation is not fully reversible, distinguishing it from asthma. The most common cause is cigarette smoking (responsible for 85-90% of cases in developed countries), though biomass fuel exposure (a major cause in developing countries), occupational dusts and chemicals, and genetic factors (alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency) also contribute. COPD affects approximately 384 million people globally and is the third leading cause of death worldwide, responsible for approximately 3 million deaths annually. The GOLD (Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease) 2024 and 2026 reports provide current guidance [^56^] [^61^] [^62^].

**Pathophysiology:** Chronic inhalational injury triggers an exaggerated inflammatory response in the lungs, involving neutrophils, macrophages, and CD8+ T-lymphocytes. This leads to: (1) small airway disease (chronic bronchitis with mucus hypersecretion, airway wall thickening, fibrosis, and luminal narrowing), and (2) parenchymal destruction (emphysema with loss of alveolar walls, elastic recoil, and capillary bed). The resulting airflow limitation is largely irreversible, though some bronchodilator reversibility may be present (particularly in patients with asthma-COPD overlap). Gas exchange abnormalities (hypoxemia, hypercapnia) develop in advanced disease due to ventilation-perfusion mismatch and reduced diffusing capacity.

**Definition of COPD Exacerbation:** An acute worsening of respiratory symptoms beyond normal day-to-day variation, leading to a change in medication. Exacerbations are classified by severity:
- **Mild:** Treated with short-acting bronchodilators only (SABA/SAMA)
- **Moderate:** Treated with SABA/SAMA plus oral corticosteroids and/or antibiotics
- **Severe:** Requires hospitalization or emergency department visit; may need non-invasive or invasive ventilation

## 8.2 Diagnosis and Assessment

### 8.2.1 Diagnostic Criteria
COPD should be considered in any patient >=40 years with:
- Dyspnea (progressive, worse with exertion, persistent)
- Chronic cough (may be intermittent, may be unproductive)
- Chronic sputum production
- History of exposure to risk factors (tobacco smoke, occupational dusts/chemicals, biomass fuels, family history)

### 8.2.2 Spirometric Criteria
- **Post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC <0.70** confirms persistent airflow limitation (required for diagnosis)
- Post-bronchodilator testing required (400 mcg albuterol/salbutamol or equivalent); wait 15-20 minutes after bronchodilator
- The FEV1/FVC ratio is the key diagnostic parameter; FEV1 percent predicted determines severity
- Degree of severity:

| GOLD Stage | FEV1 % Predicted | Clinical Description |
|-----------|-----------------|---------------------|
| GOLD 1 (Mild) | >=80% | Often asymptomatic or minimal symptoms; may not seek medical attention |
| GOLD 2 (Moderate) | 50-79% | Dyspnea on moderate exertion; first stage where most patients seek care |
| GOLD 3 (Severe) | 30-49% | Significant dyspnea on minimal exertion; repeated exacerbations; reduced exercise capacity |
| GOLD 4 (Very Severe) | <30% | Severe dyspnea; very limited exercise capacity; risk of respiratory failure; cor pulmonale |

> **Note:** The FEV1/FVC <0.70 fixed ratio may overdiagnose COPD in elderly patients (due to age-related decline in FEV1) and underdiagnose in young patients. Using the lower limit of normal (LLN, based on reference equations) may be more accurate in these populations, but the fixed ratio remains the GOLD standard for simplicity and consistency.

### 8.2.3 Comprehensive Assessment: ABE Groups (GOLD 2024) [^56^] [^61^]

The ABE assessment combines symptom burden and exacerbation history to guide initial pharmacologic treatment. This replaces the older ABCD classification.

| Group | Exacerbation History (Previous 12 Months) | Symptoms (mMRC/CAT) | Initial Pharmacotherapy |
|-------|------------------------------------------|---------------------|------------------------|
| **A** | 0-1 moderate exacerbation AND no severe exacerbations (no hospitalization) | mMRC 0-1 OR CAT <10 | Single long-acting bronchodilator (LAMA or LABA) |
| **B** | 0-1 moderate exacerbation AND no severe exacerbations | mMRC >=2 OR CAT >=10 | Dual bronchodilation (LAMA + LABA) |
| **E** | >=2 moderate exacerbations OR >=1 severe exacerbation (hospitalization) | Any symptom level | LAMA + LABA; consider adding ICS if blood eosinophils >=300/μL |

**Symptom Assessment Tools:**
- **mMRC (Modified Medical Research Council) Dyspnea Scale:**
  - Grade 0: Breathless only with strenuous exercise
  - Grade 1: Breathless when hurrying on level ground or walking up a slight hill
  - Grade 2: Walks slower than people of the same age on level ground due to breathlessness, or has to stop for breath when walking at own pace
  - Grade 3: Stops for breath after walking approximately 100 meters or after a few minutes on level ground
  - Grade 4: Too breathless to leave the house, or breathless when dressing/undressing

- **CAT (COPD Assessment Test):** 8-item questionnaire (0-40 scale); >=10 indicates significant symptom burden

> **GOLD 2026 Update:** Groups C and D have been consolidated into Group E (Exacerbation-prone). Any patient with >=1 moderate or severe exacerbation in the previous year is considered high risk and should initiate LABA/LAMA dual bronchodilation [^61^]. This reflects evidence that exacerbation history is the strongest predictor of future exacerbations and mortality.

### 8.2.4 Blood Eosinophil Count (BEC) and ICS Response

Blood eosinophil count is the most useful biomarker for predicting ICS response in COPD:

| BEC (cells/μL) | ICS Response Prediction | Recommendation |
|----------------|------------------------|----------------|
| >=300 | Strong predictor of benefit from ICS; greater reduction in exacerbations | Initiate triple therapy (LABA/LAMA/ICS) when indicated; ICS beneficial even without frequent exacerbations |
| 100-300 | Possible benefit; individualize based on exacerbation history and other features | Consider if >=2 moderate exacerbations or >=1 hospitalization |
| <100 | Unlikely to benefit; ICS may increase pneumonia risk without clear benefit | Avoid ICS; use LABA/LAMA dual therapy |

- Obtain 2-3 BEC measurements before initiating ICS due to natural variability (can vary 20-30% between measurements)
- BEC tends to be stable over time in individual patients
- Higher BEC also predicts greater benefit from roflumilast and macrolide prophylaxis
- BEC does NOT predict bronchodilator response

### 8.2.5 Additional Assessment

| Assessment | Purpose | Tool/Method |
|-----------|---------|-------------|
| **Alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency screening** | In all patients with COPD <45 years, basilar emphysema, family history, or unclear etiology | Serum AAT level and phenotype (Pi typing) or genotype |
| **Exercise capacity** | Prognosis, disability assessment, treatment response | 6-minute walk test (6MWD), cardiopulmonary exercise testing |
| **Body composition** | Prognosis; low BMI and muscle wasting predict mortality | BMI, mid-arm circumference, bioelectrical impedance |
| **Comorbidities** | COPD rarely exists in isolation | Screen for CVD, osteoporosis, depression/anxiety, lung cancer (annual LDCT if meets criteria), diabetes, GERD, OSA |
| **Lung volumes and DLCO** | Characterize emphysema vs. chronic bronchitis phenotype | Body plethysmography, diffusion capacity |
| **CT chest** | Emphysema pattern, bronchiectasis, lung cancer screening, surgical evaluation | High-resolution CT if alpha-1, disproportionate dyspnea, or surgical candidate |

## 8.3 Pharmacologic Treatment [^56^] [^62^]

### 8.3.1 Initial Pharmacotherapy by Group

| Group | Preferred Initial Therapy | Rationale |
|-------|--------------------------|-----------|
| **A** | Single long-acting bronchodilator (LAMA or LABA) | Monotherapy improves symptoms, exercise capacity, and reduces exacerbations compared to placebo; LAMA preferred over LABA as initial monotherapy due to better exacerbation reduction |
| **B** | LAMA + LABA (dual bronchodilation) | Superior symptom relief, lung function improvement, and exacerbation reduction vs. either agent alone; no ICS initially unless BEC >=300 |
| **E** | LAMA + LABA; add ICS if BEC >=300/μL (triple therapy) | Dual bronchodilation foundation; ICS for T2-high exacerbators; triple therapy has strongest evidence for mortality reduction in frequent exacerbators |

### 8.3.2 Bronchodilator Classes

| Class | Mechanism | Examples | Dosing | Key Considerations |
|-------|-----------|----------|--------|-------------------|
| **LAMA** | Blocks acetylcholine at M3 muscarinic receptors; prevents bronchial smooth muscle contraction | Tiotropium (Spiriva HandiHaler/Respimat), umeclidinium (Incruse), glycopyrrolate (Seebri Neohaler), aclidinium (Tudorza Pressair) | Once or twice daily depending on agent | First-line bronchodilator in COPD; anticholinergic side effects (dry mouth ~15%, urinary retention, constipation); caution in narrow-angle glaucoma (use mask instead of mouthpiece if concern); tiotropium Respimat showed mortality benefit in TIOTROP trial |
| **LABA** | Beta-2 adrenergic agonism; relaxes bronchial smooth muscle | Salmeterol (Serevent), formoterol (Foradil), indacaterol (Arcapta), vilanterol (in Breo), olodaterol (Striverdi Respimat) | Once or twice daily depending on agent | Never use without ICS in asthma (safety signal); safe in COPD as monotherapy or combination; faster onset with formoterol and indacaterol |
| **SABA** | Short-acting beta-2 agonism | Albuterol/salbutamol, levalbuterol | q4-6h PRN | Rescue only; high usage (>2 canisters/month) indicates inadequate control and need to step up maintenance therapy |
| **SAMA** | Short-acting anticholinergic | Ipratropium bromide (Atrovent) | q6h PRN | Added to SABA for acute exacerbations; also used in combination SABA/SAMA rescue inhalers |

### 8.3.3 Inhaled Corticosteroids and Triple Therapy

**GOLD 2024/2026 strongly prefer triple therapy (LABA + LAMA + ICS) over LABA/ICS dual therapy** when ICS is indicated [^56^]:

- **Evidence from IMPACT and ETHOS trials:**
  - Reduced moderate/severe exacerbations by 13-15% vs. LABA/ICS dual therapy
  - Reduced all-cause mortality by 42-49% vs. LABA/LAMA (in ETHOS, high-dose ICS component)
  - Reduced hospitalizations vs. dual bronchodilation
  - Benefits most pronounced in patients with BEC >=300/μL and >=2 exacerbations/year

**When to Initiate Triple Therapy:**
- Group E with BEC >=300/μL (strong indication)
- Group E with frequent exacerbations despite LABA/LAMA
- Group B or E with asthma-COPD overlap
- On LABA/ICS with persistent exacerbations (step up by adding LAMA rather than increasing ICS)

**Fixed Triple Therapy Options (Single Inhaler):**

| Product | Components | Dosing | Notes |
|---------|-----------|--------|-------|
| **Trelegy Ellipta** | Fluticasone furoate/umeclidinium/vilanterol | 1 inhalation daily | Once daily; DPI; proven in CAPTA and ETHOS trials |
| **AstraZeneca triple** | Budesonide/glycopyrrolate/formoterol | 2 inhalations BID | MDI; proven in ATHOS and SUNSET trials |
| **Other combinations** | Varies | Various | Availability varies by region |

### 8.3.4 Other Pharmacologic Therapies

| Agent | Class | Indication | Dosing | Evidence/Notes |
|-------|-------|-----------|--------|---------------|
| **Roflumilast** | PDE4 inhibitor | Severe COPD (GOLD 3-4), chronic bronchitis phenotype, FEV1 <50% predicted, history of exacerbations despite bronchodilators | 500 mcg PO daily | Reduces moderate exacerbations by ~15%; anti-inflammatory; side effects: diarrhea, weight loss (average 2 kg), nausea, headache, insomnia, depression; monitor weight |
| **Azithromycin** | Macrolide (immunomodulatory) | Frequent exacerbators (>=3/year) despite optimal inhaled therapy; former smokers | 250-500 mg PO 3x/week (Monday, Wednesday, Friday) | Reduces exacerbations by ~20% (AZALEA and MACRO trials); mechanism: anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial; risks: hearing loss (baseline and annual audiology), QT prolongation (check baseline ECG), macrolide resistance; avoid if resting QTc >450 ms |
| **Alpha-1 antitrypsin augmentation** | Protein replacement | Confirmed AAT deficiency (PiZZ, PiSZ, PiNull) with emphysema and FEV1 35-60% predicted | Weekly IV infusion | Slows FEV1 decline; expensive; confirm AAT level <11 μM and phenotype; does NOT benefit non-deficient patients |
| **Mucolytics** | Carbocysteine, N-acetylcysteine (NAC) | Select populations | Variable | Limited and inconsistent evidence; may reduce exacerbations in some populations (not recommended routinely by GOLD); high-dose NAC (600 mg BID) showed benefit in HIACE study |

### 8.3.5 COPD Medications to Avoid

| Medication | Reason to Avoid | Alternative |
|------------|----------------|-------------|
| **SABA monotherapy** | No anti-inflammatory effect; inadequate control; increased mortality risk | Add LAMA or LABA/LAMA controller |
| **ICS monotherapy** | Not recommended in COPD (no bronchodilation; insufficient evidence) | Use LAMA or LABA/LAMA |
| **Long-term oral corticosteroids** | Severe adverse effects (osteoporosis, diabetes, adrenal suppression, myopathy, cataracts, infection) | Optimize inhaled therapy; add biologics if severe eosinophilic; short courses only for exacerbations |
| **Theophylline** | Narrow therapeutic index; multiple drug interactions; limited efficacy | LAMA/LABA preferred |
| **Chronic opioid cough suppressants** | Respiratory depression; increased mortality in COPD | Treat underlying cause of cough |
| **Antitussives** | Cough is protective in COPD (clears secretions) | Airway clearance techniques |

## 8.4 Non-Pharmacologic Management [^62^]

### 8.4.1 Smoking Cessation
The single most important intervention in COPD management. Benefits include:
- Reduces rate of FEV1 decline by approximately 50% (slows progression from ~60 mL/year to ~30 mL/year)
- Reduces mortality
- Reduces exacerbation frequency
- Improves response to inhaled therapies
- Combined behavioral counseling with pharmacotherapy (varenicline, bupropion, nicotine replacement therapy) achieves the highest quit rates (20-35% at 6 months)
- Every patient with COPD who smokes should be offered cessation intervention at every visit

### 8.4.2 Vaccinations
| Vaccine | Recommendation | Notes |
|---------|---------------|-------|
| Influenza | Annually for all COPD patients | Reduces exacerbations and hospitalizations by ~50% |
| Pneumococcal | PCV20 once, OR PCV15 followed by PPSV23 1 year later | Age >=65 or 19-64 with risk conditions |
| COVID-19 | Per current recommendations | Reduces severe illness |
| RSV | Shared decision-making for age >=60 | AREXVY or ABRYSVO available |
| Tdap | One-time, then Td or Tdap every 10 years | Pertussis protection |
| Shingles | 2 doses recombinant zoster vaccine (Shingrix) | Age >=50 or >=19 with immunocompromise |

### 8.4.3 Pulmonary Rehabilitation
- Recommended for symptomatic patients (Groups B and E), post-exacerbation, and pre/post-lung surgery
- Components: Exercise training (aerobic and resistance), nutritional counseling, education, psychosocial support, smoking cessation
- Benefits: Improves exercise capacity (6MWD increases 40-80 meters), reduces dyspnea, improves quality of life, reduces hospitalizations and mortality
- Post-exacerbation pulmonary rehabilitation initiated within 4 weeks of hospital discharge reduces readmissions
- Minimum 8-week program; longer programs produce greater benefits

### 8.4.4 Long-term Oxygen Therapy (LTOT)
- Indicated for chronic resting hypoxemia: PaO2 <=55 mmHg or SpO2 <=88% on room air; OR PaO2 56-59 mmHg (SpO2 89%) with evidence of pulmonary hypertension, polycythemia (Hct >55%), or cor pulmonale
- Must be used >=15 hours/day (including during sleep) to improve survival (NOTC trial)
- Also indicated for exertional desaturation (SpO2 <88% with ambulation) or nocturnal desaturation
- Reassess after smoking cessation, acute illness recovery, and annually
- Goal: SpO2 >90% during rest, sleep, and exertion

### 8.4.5 Non-Invasive Ventilation (NIV)
- Indicated for chronic hypercapnic respiratory failure (PaCO2 >=52 mmHg)
- Nocturnal bilevel positive airway pressure (BiPAP) reduces PaCO2, improves sleep quality, reduces hospitalizations, and may improve survival
- Settings typically: IPAP 12-18 cm H2O, EPAP 4-5 cm H2O, titrated to reduce PaCO2 by 20% or achieve PaCO2 <48 mmHg
- Requires careful patient selection and adherence support

### 8.4.6 Surgical and Procedural Interventions

| Intervention | Indication | Expected Outcome |
|-------------|-----------|-----------------|
| **Lung volume reduction surgery (LVRS)** | Upper lobe-predominant emphysema, FEV1 20-45%, low exercise capacity after pulmonary rehab | NETT trial: Improved survival, exercise capacity, and quality of life in selected patients |
| **Bronchoscopic lung volume reduction** | Endobronchial valves, coils, or thermal vapor ablation for severe emphysema | Reduces hyperinflation; improves FEV1, 6MWD, and dyspnea |
| **Bullectomy** | Giant bulla occupying >=1/3 hemithorax, compression of adjacent lung | Improves lung function and dyspnea |
| **Lung transplantation** | BODE index >7, FEV1 <25%, severe dyspnea, or frequent exacerbations; limited life expectancy | Median survival ~6 years post-transplant; 5-year survival ~50-60% |

## 8.5 Acute Exacerbation Management [^62^]

**Definition:** Acute worsening of respiratory symptoms over <14 days beyond normal day-to-day variation, leading to a change in medication. Common triggers: viral respiratory infections (40-60%), bacterial infections, environmental pollutants, non-adherence to therapy.

### 8.5.1 Assessment of Severity

| Feature | Mild (Outpatient) | Moderate (ED/Observation) | Severe (Hospitalization) | Very Severe (ICU) |
|---------|------------------|--------------------------|------------------------|-------------------|
| **Dyspnea** | Increased on exertion | At rest, speaking in phrases | At rest, speaking in words | Exhaustion, obtundation |
| **Mental status** | Normal | Normal | Anxious, alert | Confused, drowsy |
| **Respiratory rate** | <20 | 20-30 | >30 | Variable |
| **Heart rate** | <100 | 100-120 | >120 | Bradycardia (pre-terminal) |
| **Use of accessory muscles** | No | Sometimes | Yes | Paradoxical chest movement |
| **SpO2** | >92% | 90-92% | <90% | <85% despite O2 |
| **ABG (on room air)** | Normal | PaO2 60-80, PaCO2 <45 | PaO2 <60, PaCO2 >45 | PaO2 <40, PaCO2 >60, pH <7.25 |

### 8.5.2 Treatment

1. **Bronchodilators:**
   - Short-acting beta-agonist +/- anticholinergic (albuterol + ipratropium nebulizers) every 4-6 hours
   - May administer continuously or every 20 minutes for first hour in severe cases
   - Continue long-acting bronchodilators during exacerbation (do not stop)

2. **Systemic corticosteroids:**
   - Prednisone 40 mg PO daily for 5 days (evidence supports 5 days = 10-14 days for most patients - REDUCE trial)
   - IV methylprednisolone 40 mg q24h if unable to take oral
   - No taper needed for 5-day course
   - Begin within first hour of presentation
   - Reduces treatment failure, shortens hospitalization, improves FEV1

3. **Antibiotics:**
   - **Indicated if bacterial infection suspected** (Anthonisen criteria):
     - Increased sputum purulence + increased dyspnea, OR
     - Increased sputum purulence + increased sputum volume, OR
     - All three (increased purulence + increased dyspnea + increased volume)
   - Also indicated if: need for mechanical ventilation (invasive or non-invasive), pneumonia on CXR
   - **Options:** Amoxicillin/clavulanate 875/125 mg BID, doxycycline 100 mg BID, azithromycin 500 mg day 1 then 250 mg daily, or respiratory fluoroquinolone (levofloxacin 750 mg daily, moxifloxacin 400 mg daily) for 5 days
   - Reserve fluoroquinolones for beta-lactam allergy or failure of first-line agents (tendon rupture risk, QT prolongation, resistance)

4. **Oxygen:**
   - Target SpO2 88-92% (lower target than in most other conditions)
   - **Avoid hyperoxia** in chronic CO2 retainers (loss of hypoxic ventilatory drive can precipitate hypercapnic respiratory failure)
   - Titrate carefully; obtain ABG 30-60 minutes after starting oxygen
   - Venturi masks provide more precise FiO2 than nasal cannula

5. **Non-invasive ventilation (BiPAP):** First-line for acute hypercapnic respiratory failure
   - Indications: pH <7.35 + PaCO2 >45 mmHg despite supplemental oxygen and medical therapy
   - Settings: IPAP 10-15 cm H2O, EPAP 4-5 cm H2O, titrate to RR <25, tidal volume 6-8 mL/kg
   - Benefits: Reduces work of breathing, improves gas exchange, reduces intubation rate by ~50%, improves survival, reduces hospital LOS
   - Contraindications: Respiratory arrest, unable to protect airway, hemodynamic instability, facial trauma, recent upper GI surgery, severe agitation, copious secretions

6. **Ventilatory support:** Intubation and mechanical ventilation if NIV fails or is contraindicated

### 8.5.3 Indications for Hospitalization

- Severe symptoms (dyspnea at rest, altered mental status, cyanosis)
- Failure of outpatient therapy
- Significant comorbidities (heart failure, coronary artery disease, diabetes, immunocompromise)
- Older age with limited home support or functional impairment
- Worsening respiratory acidosis (pH <7.35 despite treatment)
- New hypoxemia (SpO2 <90% on room air) or hypercapnia
- Inability to cope at home (living alone, no caregiver, poor access to follow-up)

### 8.5.4 Discharge Planning
- Ensure patient can use inhaler devices correctly (demonstrate and observe)
- Review and optimize maintenance therapy (step up if >=2 exacerbations/year)
- 5-day course of prednisone (40 mg daily)
- Prescribe antibiotics if indicated (complete 5-day course)
- Arrange follow-up within 1 week (critical for reducing readmissions)
- Refer for pulmonary rehabilitation (post-exacerbation rehab within 4 weeks)
- Vaccination review and administration
- Written action plan for recognizing and managing future exacerbations
- Smoking cessation intervention if applicable

## 8.6 COPD-Asthma Overlap

Some patients have features of both COPD and asthma (ACO - Asthma-COPD Overlap). This is important because:
- These patients have worse outcomes than either disease alone
- They require ICS-containing therapy (as for asthma)
- Management should incorporate elements of both conditions

**Features suggesting ACO:**
- Persistent airflow limitation (post-BD FEV1/FVC <0.70) with significant bronchodilator reversibility (FEV1 increase >400 mL and >15% from baseline)
- Elevated FeNO (>=50 ppb) or blood eosinophils (>=300/μL)
- History of asthma or atopy (childhood asthma, allergic rhinitis, eczema)
- Smoking history
- Symptom variability (day-to-day or seasonal variation)
- Good response to ICS trial

**Management:** Generally requires ICS-containing therapy (similar to asthma) plus long-acting bronchodilators. Avoid LABA monotherapy. Consider phenotype-guided therapy (elevated eosinophils may predict ICS response).

## 8.7 Stable COPD: Key Management Summary Table

| GOLD Group | Initial Therapy | Step-Up (If Inadequate) | Escalation |
|-----------|----------------|------------------------|------------|
| **A** | LAMA monotherapy | LAMA + LABA (dual bronchodilation) | Add ICS if BEC >=300 and exacerbations |
| **B** | LAMA + LABA | Evaluate adherence/technique; consider triple if BEC >=300 | Triple therapy if exacerbations develop |
| **E** | LAMA + LABA (+ ICS if BEC >=300) | Triple therapy if not already on it | Add roflumilast (if FEV1 <50% + chronic bronchitis); add azithromycin (if former smoker + frequent exacerbations); consider alpha-1 augmentation if deficient; evaluate for bronchoscopic/surgical interventions |

## 8.8 Red Flags

> **EMERGENCY INDICATORS - SEEK IMMEDIATE CARE:**
> - Severe dyspnea at rest with prominent accessory muscle use, tripod position, or inability to speak
> - Altered mental status (confusion, somnolence, agitation) suggesting hypercapnic encephalopathy
> - Respiratory acidosis with pH <7.25 despite supplemental oxygen and medical therapy
> - SpO2 <85% on room air despite supplemental oxygen (or target SpO2 not achievable)
> - Hemodynamic instability (hypotension SBP <90, arrhythmia)
> - Pneumothorax (sudden worsening, unilateral decreased breath sounds, pleuritic chest pain)
> - Massive hemoptysis
> - Signs of acute cor pulmonale (worsening peripheral edema, JVD, right upper quadrant pain from hepatic congestion)

## 8.9 Prognosis and End-of-Life Considerations

- **BODE index** (BMI, airflow Obstruction, Dyspnea, Exercise capacity) predicts mortality better than FEV1 alone
- Median survival after hospitalization for hypercapnic exacerbation: ~2 years
- Advance care planning should be discussed early and revisited regularly
- Palliative care consultation for frequent hospitalizations, severe symptoms despite optimal therapy, or prognostic awareness discussions
- Opioids (low-dose morphine or oxycodone) may be used for refractory dyspnea in end-stage COPD (evidence supports safety and efficacy)
- Benzodiazepines for anxiety associated with dyspnea (use cautiously due to respiratory depression)
- Hospice referral when life expectancy <6 months (frequent hospitalizations, weight loss, dependence on others for ADLs, progressive functional decline)

# Chapter 9: Pneumonia

## 9.1 Overview

Pneumonia is an infection of the lung parenchyma caused by bacteria, viruses, fungi, or other microorganisms. It is classified by setting of acquisition: community-acquired pneumonia (CAP), hospital-acquired pneumonia (HAP), and ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP). CAP is the leading cause of infectious disease-related hospitalization and mortality in developed countries, affecting approximately 4-5 million adults annually in the US, with 1 million requiring hospitalization. Despite advances in antimicrobial therapy and vaccines, pneumonia remains a significant cause of morbidity and mortality, particularly in the elderly and immunocompromised. The IDSA/ATS 2019 guidelines provide the current evidence-based framework for diagnosis and management [^25^].

**Pathophysiology:** Pathogens reach the lower respiratory tract via microaspiration (most common), inhalation, or hematogenous spread. The normal respiratory defense mechanisms (cough reflex, mucociliary escalator, innate and adaptive immunity) are overwhelmed, leading to inflammatory infiltration of alveoli and interstitium. Alveolar filling with inflammatory exudate impairs gas exchange, causing hypoxemia. Bacterial pneumonia typically causes lobar or segmental consolidation, while viral and atypical pathogens produce more diffuse interstitial or bronchopneumonic patterns.

## 9.2 Classification

### 9.2.1 Community-Acquired Pneumonia (CAP)
- Defined as pneumonia acquired outside the hospital or within 48 hours of admission in a person who does not meet criteria for healthcare-associated pneumonia
- Common pathogens:
  - **Bacterial:** *Streptococcus pneumoniae* (most common bacterial cause, 15-30%), *Haemophilus influenzae* (5-15%), *Moraxella catarrhalis* (3-5%), *Staphylococcus aureus* (3-10%, often post-influenza), *Legionella pneumophila* (2-10%, outbreaks in summer), *Mycoplasma pneumoniae* (5-20%, more common in young adults), *Chlamydophila pneumoniae* (5-10%), *Klebsiella pneumoniae* (3-5%, especially in alcoholics)
  - **Viral:** Influenza A/B (most common viral cause), SARS-CoV-2, RSV, rhinovirus, parainfluenza, human metapneumovirus, adenovirus
  - **Mixed:** Viral-bacterial coinfection occurs in 10-30% of hospitalized patients
- Incidence increases with age; highest in elderly (>65 years) and infants

### 9.2.2 Hospital-Acquired Pneumonia (HAP)
- Occurs >=48 hours after hospital admission, not incubating at admission
- Higher prevalence of multidrug-resistant (MDR) organisms due to hospital environment and antibiotic exposure
- Common pathogens: *S. aureus* (including MRSA, 20-40%), *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* (15-20%), *Klebsiella pneumoniae*, *E. coli*, *Acinetobacter baumannii*, *Enterobacter* species
- Mortality rate: 15-30% (higher with MDR organisms and VAP)

### 9.2.3 Ventilator-Associated Pneumonia (VAP)
- Pneumonia occurring >48 hours after endotracheal intubation
- Highest mortality among pneumonias (up to 50% depending on pathogen and patient factors)
- Risk factors: prolonged intubation, supine positioning, enteral nutrition, inadequate oral care, previous antibiotic exposure, reintubation
- Common pathogens similar to HAP but with higher rates of MDR organisms

## 9.3 Severity Assessment [^25^]

Accurate severity assessment is critical for determining appropriate site of care (outpatient vs. hospital ward vs. ICU) and guiding treatment intensity.

### 9.3.1 CURB-65 Score

| Component | Points | Notes |
|-----------|--------|-------|
| Confusion (new onset, defined as AMT <8/10 or new disorientation) | 1 | Indicates severe sepsis or metabolic derangement |
| Urea >7 mmol/L (BUN >19 mg/dL or 20 mg/dL) | 1 | Marker of prerenal azotemia from sepsis |
| Respiratory rate >=30 breaths/min | 1 | Significant respiratory compromise |
| Blood pressure (SBP <90 mmHg or DBP <=60 mmHg) | 1 | Septic shock or cardiovascular compromise |
| Age >=65 years | 1 | Age-related increase in mortality |

| Score | Risk | Mortality | Management |
|-------|------|-----------|------------|
| 0-1 | Low risk | <3% | Outpatient treatment |
| 2 | Moderate risk | 3-15% | Short hospitalization or closely supervised outpatient with early follow-up |
| 3-5 | High risk | 15-40% | Hospital admission; ICU if score 3-5 |

> **CURB-65 >=3 is one of the major criteria for ICU admission** (along with septic shock requiring vasopressors and respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation).

### 9.3.2 PSI (Pneumonia Severity Index) / PORT Score
- More comprehensive than CURB-65; uses 20 variables including demographics, comorbidities (neoplasm, CHF, cerebrovascular disease, renal disease, liver disease), vital signs, physical exam findings, and laboratory values
- Better at identifying low-risk patients suitable for outpatient management than CURB-65
- **PSI Class I-II:** Outpatient treatment (low risk; <1.5% mortality)
- **PSI Class III:** Brief hospitalization or observation unit (intermediate risk; mortality 2-10%)
- **PSI Class IV-V:** Hospital admission (high risk; mortality >10%)
- Limitation: More complex to calculate; tends to overestimate severity in young patients with comorbidities

### 9.3.3 SMART-COP (for Severe CAP / ICU Need Prediction)
Useful for identifying patients who will need intensive respiratory or vasopressor support.

| Component | Points |
|-----------|--------|
| Systolic BP <90 mmHg | 2 |
| Multilobar infiltrates on CXR | 1 |
| Albumin <3.5 g/dL | 1 |
| Respiratory rate >=30 (age <50) or >=25 (age >=50) | 1 |
| Tachycardia >=125 bpm | 1 |
| Confusion | 1 |
| Oxygen: PaO2 <70 mmHg or SpO2 <=93% or PaO2/FiO2 <333 | 2 |
| pH <7.35 | 2 |

| Score | Risk | Predicted Need for IRVS |
|-------|------|------------------------|
| 0-2 | Low risk | <7% |
| 3-4 | Moderate risk | 20-30% |
| >=5 | High risk | >60% |

IRVS = Intensive Respiratory or Vasopressor Support

### 9.3.4 IDSA/ATS Major and Minor Criteria for Severe CAP (ICU Admission)

**Major Criteria (either one mandates ICU admission):**
1. Septic shock requiring vasopressors
2. Respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation

**Minor Criteria (>=3 indicates severe CAP requiring ICU or high-level monitoring):**
1. Respiratory rate >=30/min
2. PaO2/FiO2 ratio <=250
3. Multilobar infiltrates
4. Confusion/disorientation
5. Uremia (BUN >=20 mg/dL)
6. Leukopenia (WBC <4,000 due to infection)
7. Thrombocytopenia (platelets <100,000)
8. Hypothermia (core temp <36 C)
9. Hypotension requiring aggressive fluid resuscitation

### 9.4.1 Routine Testing
| Test | Outpatient | Inpatient (Ward) | ICU |

| CBC with differential | Consider | Yes | Yes |
| BMP/creatinine | Consider | Yes | Yes |

- ICU admission

## 9.5 Treatment of CAP [^25^]

### 9.5.1 Outpatient Treatment

| Patient Profile | Recommended Regimen | Duration |
|----------------|---------------------|----------|
| **No comorbidities, no MRSA/Pseudomonas risk** | Amoxicillin 1 g three times daily OR Doxycycline 100 mg BID OR Macrolide (azithromycin 500 mg day 1, then 250 mg daily for 4 days) | 5 days |
| **With comorbidities** (chronic heart disease, chronic lung disease, CKD, DM, liver disease, malignancy, alcoholism, asplenia) | Amoxicillin-clavulanate 875/125 mg BID OR Cephalosporin (cefuroxime axetil 500 mg BID) + macrolide OR Respiratory fluoroquinolone (levofloxacin 750 mg daily or moxifloxacin 400 mg daily) | 5 days |

> **Macrolide caution:** Use only if local pneumococcal resistance <25% AND no cardiac risks (QT prolongation, arrhythmias, elderly on other QT-prolonging drugs). Macrolide monotherapy as first-line has declined due to increasing pneumococcal resistance (up to 30-40% in some regions). Amoxicillin remains excellent first-line therapy due to superior pneumococcal coverage.

### 9.5.2 Inpatient Treatment (Non-severe)

| Regimen | Details | Duration |
|---------|---------|----------|
| **Beta-lactam + macrolide** | Ceftriaxone 1-2 g daily + azithromycin 500 mg daily | 5-7 days |
| **Respiratory fluoroquinolone monotherapy** | Levofloxacin 750 mg daily or moxifloxacin 400 mg daily | 5-7 days |
| **Beta-lactam + doxycycline** | Ceftriaxone + doxycycline 100 mg BID | 5-7 days |

Beta-lactam alternatives: Ampicillin-sulbactam 1.5-3 g q6h, cefotaxime 1-2 g q8h, ceftaroline 600 mg q12h (broad-spectrum including MRSA)

### 9.5.3 Inpatient Treatment (Severe / ICU)

| Regimen | Details |
|---------|---------|
| **Beta-lactam + macrolide** | Ceftriaxone 2 g daily + azithromycin 500 mg daily |
| **Beta-lactam + fluoroquinolone** | Ceftriaxone + levofloxacin 750 mg daily |

> **Beta-lactam should provide pneumococcal coverage including DRSP** (drug-resistant *S. pneumoniae*). Ceftriaxone, cefotaxime, and ampicillin-sulbactam are preferred.

**If MRSA risk factors** (prior MRSA respiratory infection, recent hospitalization with IV antibiotics, post-influenza necrotizing pneumonia):
- Add vancomycin (target trough 15-20 mcg/mL) or linezolid 600 mg IV/PO BID
- Obtain nasal MRSA PCR to guide de-escalation

**If Pseudomonas risk factors** (structural lung disease [bronchiectasis, COPD], prior Pseudomonas isolation, recent hospitalization with broad-spectrum antibiotics, corticosteroid use):
- Add antipseudomonal agent: Piperacillin-tazobactam 4.5 g q6h, cefepime 2 g q8h, meropenem 1 g q8h, or aztreonam 2 g q8h
- Obtain cultures to guide de-escalation

**Empiric atypical coverage** (legionella, mycoplasma, chlamydophila) should be included in all hospitalized patients with CAP (hence macrolide or fluoroquinolone).

### 9.5.4 Duration of Therapy
- **Minimum 5 days** for uncomplicated CAP (IDSA/ATS 2019 guideline change from 7-10 days)
- Patient must be **afebrile for 48-72 hours AND clinically stable** before discontinuation
- **Clinical stability criteria** (all must be met):
  - Temperature <=37.8 C (100 F) for 48 hours
  - Heart rate <=100 bpm
  - Respiratory rate <=24/min
  - Systolic BP >=90 mmHg
  - SpO2 >=90% (or PaO2 >=60 mmHg) on room air
  - Ability to take oral medications
  - Normal mental status
- May extend to 7-10+ days for: slow clinical response, complicated infection (lung abscess, empyema), MRSA or Pseudomonas, Legionella, meningitis, endocarditis
- Procalcitonin-guided discontinuation may allow shorter courses in patients who meet clinical stability criteria

## 9.6 HAP/VAP Treatment

| Principle | Details |
|-----------|---------|
| Empiric coverage | Broad-spectrum covering MRSA and Pseudomonas if risk factors present |
| MRSA coverage | Vancomycin (trough 15-20 mcg/mL) or linezolid 600 mg BID |
| Pseudomonas coverage | Piperacillin-tazobactam, cefepime, ceftazidime, meropenem, imipenem, or aztreonam |
| Double antipseudomonal coverage | Recommended only if high risk for MDR (previous colonization, unit prevalence >10% MDR, structural lung disease, recent broad-spectrum antibiotics) |
| Legionella coverage | Add macrolide or fluoroquinolone if risk factors |
| De-escalation | Based on culture results within 48-72 hours; narrow to most appropriate agent |
| Duration | 7-day course is standard (shorter acceptable if clinically improving and not Pseudomonas/ MRSA); longer courses for Pseudomonas, MRSA, complicated infections, or immunocompromised |
| **Prevention** | Head of bed elevation 30-45 degrees, oral chlorhexidine care, subglottic suctioning endotracheal tubes, minimizing sedation and duration of ventilation, daily sedation interruption and spontaneous breathing trials |

## 9.7 Aspiration Pneumonia

- Risk factors: Altered consciousness (stroke, seizures, alcohol intoxication), dysphagia (neuromuscular disease, esophageal disorders), poor dentition, supine positioning, enteral feeding
- Typically involves anaerobes (mouth flora) and gram-negative enterics
- **Community-acquired aspiration:** Usually responds to standard CAP antibiotics (amoxicillin-clavulanate, carbapenems, or respiratory fluoroquinolone + metronidazole)
- **Hospital-acquired aspiration:** Broader coverage needed including gram-negatives and MRSA
- **Chemical pneumonitis** (aspiration of gastric contents): Sterile inflammatory response; antibiotics NOT indicated unless secondary infection develops
- **Lung abscess:** Anaerobic coverage with amoxicillin-clavulanate, clindamycin, or carbapenem; prolonged course 4-8 weeks; drainage if large or refractory

## 9.8 COVID-19 and Influenza Pneumonia

| Virus | Treatment | Notes |
|-------|-----------|-------|
| **Influenza A/B** | Oseltamivir 75 mg BID x 5 days; baloxavir marboxil 40-80 mg single dose | Start within 48 hours of symptom onset (or later if hospitalized/severe); reduces duration by ~1 day; reduces complications in high-risk patients |
| **SARS-CoV-2** | See Chapter 10 | Nirmatrelvir-ritonavir (Paxlovid), remdesivir, or molnupiravir for outpatients at high risk |

- **Coinfection:** Bacterial coinfection occurs in 10-30% of influenza and 5-10% of COVID-19; maintain low threshold for adding antibiotics in severe viral pneumonia
- Post-influenza *S. aureus* (including MRSA) and *S. pneumoniae* are particularly virulent; necrotizing pneumonia and empyema are more common

## 9.9 Prevention

| Intervention | Details |
|-------------|---------|
| **Pneumococcal vaccination** | PCV20 (Prevnar 20) once, OR PCV15 (Vaxneuvance) followed by PPSV23 (Pneumovax 23) 1 year later; all adults >=65; adults 19-64 with risk conditions (chronic lung/heart/liver disease, DM, alcoholism, smoking, asplenia, immunocompromise, CSF leak, cochlear implant) |
| **Influenza vaccination** | Annually for all adults; particularly important for patients with chronic lung disease, elderly, immunocompromised |
| **Smoking cessation** | Reduces risk of CAP by 30-50% within 5 years of quitting |
| **Aspiration precautions** | Elevate head of bed 30-45 degrees in at-risk patients; modified diets for dysphagia; oral care in institutionalized patients |
| **Hand hygiene** | Reduces HAP/VAP transmission |
| **Dental hygiene** | Reduces aspiration pneumonia risk in elderly |

## 9.10 Red Flags (Indicating Severe Pneumonia or ICU Admission)

> **CRITERIA FOR ICU ADMISSION (per IDSA/ATS, >=3 minor criteria or any major criterion):**
> - Septic shock requiring vasopressors (MAJOR)
> - Respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation (MAJOR)
> - Respiratory rate >=30/min (minor)
> - Multilobar infiltrates on imaging (minor)
> - Confusion/disorientation (minor)
> - SBP <90 mmHg requiring aggressive fluid resuscitation (minor)
> - PaO2/FiO2 ratio <250 (minor)
> - Leukopenia (WBC <4,000/μL) due to infection (minor)
> - Thrombocytopenia (platelets <100,000/μL) (minor)
> - Hypothermia (core temperature <36 C) (minor)
> - Uremia (BUN >=20 mg/dL) (minor)

## 9.11 Follow-Up
- Reassess within 48-72 hours for outpatients (by phone or visit)
- Chest X-ray should show improvement within 2-4 weeks; if no improvement by 4-6 weeks, evaluate for complications (abscess, empyema, obstruction, non-infectious cause)
- Repeat CXR at 4-6 weeks in smokers and those >50 years to document resolution and exclude underlying malignancy (lung cancer may present as post-obstructive pneumonia)
- In patients with delayed resolution, consider: wrong diagnosis, resistant organism, unusual pathogen (TB, fungal), empyema, abscess, foreign body, malignancy, immunocompromise

# Chapter 10: COVID-19

## 10.1 Overview

COVID-19 is caused by SARS-CoV-2, a betacoronavirus first identified in Wuhan, China in December 2019. The disease has evolved through multiple waves driven by viral variants, with the Omicron lineage and its subvariants (BA.1 through JN.1, KP.2, KP.3, XBB, and subsequent lineages) becoming dominant globally. These newer variants demonstrate enhanced transmissibility, greater immune evasion due to extensive spike protein mutations, and generally reduced intrinsic virulence compared to earlier variants (Alpha, Delta), though severe disease still occurs, particularly in high-risk individuals. While population immunity from vaccination and prior infection has reduced the overall severity of disease, COVID-19 remains a significant cause of morbidity and mortality, with the US recording hundreds of deaths weekly. Current guidance focuses on outpatient treatment for high-risk individuals, inpatient management for severe disease, and recognition and management of post-acute sequelae (long COVID) [^22^] [^24^].

**Virology:** SARS-CoV-2 is an enveloped, positive-sense, single-stranded RNA virus. The spike (S) protein mediates attachment to ACE2 receptors on host cells, with TMPRSS2 facilitating viral entry. Viral variants are characterized by mutations in the spike protein, particularly in the receptor-binding domain (RBD), which affect transmissibility, immune evasion, and tropism.

## 10.2 Diagnosis

### 10.2.1 Testing
- **NAAT (PCR):** Gold standard; higher sensitivity (detects lower viral loads); detects viral RNA for longer period (may remain positive for weeks after infectious period); preferred for high-risk patients needing treatment decisions, hospitalized patients, and when clinical suspicion is high but antigen test is negative
- **Rapid antigen tests:** Detect viral proteins; lower sensitivity than PCR (especially early in infection and with newer variants due to mutations in target proteins); positive result is reliable (specificity >95%), but negative result does NOT exclude infection; best used when viral loads are highest (first 3-5 days of symptoms); serial testing improves sensitivity
- **Serology:** Detects antibodies (IgM, IgG); NOT useful for acute diagnosis (takes 1-3 weeks to develop); may indicate prior infection or vaccination; neutralizing antibody tests have limited clinical utility

### 10.2.2 Clinical Features by Severity

| Severity | Features | Typical Management |
|----------|----------|-------------------|
| **Asymptomatic** | Positive test; no symptoms | Isolation per local guidelines; monitor for symptom development |
| **Mild** | Upper respiratory symptoms (sore throat, rhinorrhea, nasal congestion), low-grade fever, mild cough, headache, myalgias, fatigue, dysgeusia/anosmia (less common with Omicron) | Supportive care at home; antiviral if high risk |
| **Moderate** | Lower respiratory involvement (dyspnea on exertion, persistent cough, chest discomfort), SpO2 >=94% on room air | Outpatient if stable; close monitoring; antiviral if high risk; consider hospitalization if risk factors |
| **Severe** | SpO2 <94% on room air, respiratory rate >=30/min, lung infiltrates >50% on imaging within 24-48 hours | Hospitalization; supplemental oxygen; dexamethasone if requiring oxygen |
| **Critical** | Respiratory failure (requiring mechanical ventilation or high-flow nasal cannula), shock, multi-organ dysfunction, encephalopathy | ICU admission; organ support; comprehensive management protocol |

### 10.2.3 Risk Factors for Severe Disease
Risk factors should guide treatment decisions and level of monitoring:

| Category | Specific Risk Factors |
|----------|---------------------|
| **Age** | >=65 years (risk increases progressively with age; >85 has highest risk) |
| **Cardiovascular** | Coronary artery disease, heart failure, cardiomyopathy, congenital heart disease, hypertension |
| **Pulmonary** | COPD, moderate-severe asthma, interstitial lung disease, cystic fibrosis, pulmonary hypertension |
| **Metabolic** | Diabetes mellitus (especially HbA1c >7%), obesity (BMI >=30; higher BMI = higher risk), metabolic syndrome |
| **Renal** | Chronic kidney disease (any stage), dialysis dependence |
| **Immunologic** | Immunocompromised state (solid organ or stem cell transplant, active cancer on chemotherapy, HIV with low CD4, congenital immunodeficiency, high-dose corticosteroids >=20 mg prednisone equivalent for >=14 days, immunosuppressive biologics) |
| **Neurologic** | Dementia, history of stroke, neuromuscular disorders |
| **Hematologic** | Sickle cell disease, thalassemia |
| **Other** | Pregnancy (especially third trimester), smoking (current or former), liver disease (cirrhosis, NAFLD), physical inactivity, alcohol use disorder |

## 10.3 Outpatient Treatment [^22^] [^24^]

### 10.3.1 Treatment Candidates
- Adults and adolescents (>=12 years, >=40 kg) who are at **high risk for progression to severe COVID-19**
- Must start treatment within 5 days of symptom onset (for nirmatrelvir-ritonavir, molnupiravir) or within 7 days (for remdesivir)
- Shared decision-making recommended given evolving evidence on effectiveness with newer variants and immune populations
- Treatment is most beneficial for: unvaccinated or incompletely vaccinated individuals, immunocompromised patients, those with multiple risk factors, and during periods when circulating variants may be more virulent

### 10.3.2 Treatment Options

| Treatment | Population | Route | Duration | Timing | Efficacy | Key Considerations |
|-----------|-----------|-------|----------|--------|----------|-------------------|
| **Nirmatrelvir/ritonavir (Paxlovid)** | Adults and children >=12 years and >=40 kg | Oral | 5 days | Within 5 days of symptom onset | ~88% reduction in hospitalization/death (EPIC-HR trial in unvaccinated, Delta era); real-world effectiveness lower with Omicron but still significant in high-risk | **First-line**; extensive drug-drug interactions via CYP3A inhibition; requires renal dose adjustment; taste disturbance common |
| **Remdesivir (Veklury)** | Adults and children >=28 days and >=3 kg | IV infusion | 3 days | Within 7 days of symptom onset | ~87% reduction in hospitalization/death (PINETREE trial); requires infusion center | Second-line; preferred if significant drug interactions with Paxlovid; 30-120 minute IV infusion each day |
| **Molnupiravir** | Adults only | Oral | 5 days | Within 5 days of symptom onset | ~30% reduction in hospitalization/death (MOVe-OUT trial); lowest efficacy of three options | Third-line; mutagenic mechanism theoretically could affect fetal development; avoid in pregnancy and breastfeeding; not recommended if other options available |

### 10.3.3 Paxlovid (Nirmatrelvir/Ritonavir) Detailed Guidance [^22^]

**Standard Dosing:**
- 300 mg nirmatrelvir (two 150 mg tablets) + 100 mg ritonavir (one 100 mg tablet) orally twice daily for 5 days
- Take with or without food; swallow tablets whole (do not crush)

**Renal Dose Adjustment:**
- eGFR >=60 mL/min: Standard dose (300/100 mg BID)
- eGFR 30-59 mL/min: 150 mg nirmatrelvir + 100 mg ritonavir BID (reduced nirmatrelvir dose)
- eGFR <30 mL/min: Not recommended unless no alternatives and specialist consultation obtained
- **Severe hepatic impairment (Child-Pugh C):** Not recommended

**Critical Drug Interactions:**
Ritonavir is a strong CYP3A4 inhibitor and CYP2D6 inhibitor; nirmatrelvir is a CYP3A4 substrate and P-glycoprotein inhibitor. The interaction potential is extensive:

- **Contraindicated (do NOT co-administer):** Alfuzosin, colchicine (in renal/hepatic impairment), dronedarone, eplerenone, ergot derivatives, flibanserin, lomitapide, lovastatin, simvastatin, midazolam (oral), triazolam, sildenafil/tadalafil (for pulmonary arterial hypertension), rifampin, St. John's wort, voriconazole (with ritonavir), propafenone, quetiapine (increased QT risk), lurasidone, pimozide
- **Require dose adjustment/holding:** Immunosuppressants (tacrolimus, cyclosporine, sirolimus - may need to hold 3-5 days before starting), anticoagulants (warfarin - monitor INR closely; apixaban/rivaroxaban - generally hold or reduce dose), anticonvulsants (phenytoin, carbamazepine - may need to switch), statins (hold simvastatin/lovastatin; atorvastatin/rosuvastatin may continue at lower dose), hormonal contraceptives (backup method needed), corticosteroids (dexamethasone, prednisone - increased levels), antipsychotics, antiarrhythmics (amiodarone - reduce dose; flecainide - avoid), PDE-5 inhibitors (for erectile dysfunction - hold during treatment)
- **Always check drug interactions** using the Liverpool COVID-19 drug interactions checker (https://www.covid19-druginteractions.org) before prescribing

**Paxlovid Rebound:**
- Recurrence of COVID-19 symptoms and/or positive viral test within 2-8 days after completing treatment course
- Occurs in approximately 5-10% of treated patients
- Generally mild; no clear evidence that second course is beneficial
- Consider per clinician judgment in high-risk patients with recurrent symptoms
- Not thought to be related to inadequate treatment duration; may reflect host immune response timing
- Does NOT indicate resistance

**Efficacy Context with Omicron and Population Immunity:**
- Real-world effectiveness remains significant but reduced compared to pre-Omicron era due to variant evolution and population immunity
- NICE UK has withdrawn recommendation for certain lower-risk populations where cost-effectiveness is no longer favorable [^24^]
- Still strongly recommended for: high-risk unvaccinated individuals, immunocompromised patients (may benefit regardless of vaccination status), those with multiple risk factors, and during periods of increased community transmission

### 10.3.4 Supportive Care for Mild-Moderate COVID-19
- Rest and hydration
- Acetaminophen or NSAIDs for fever and myalgias (NSAIDs are safe in COVID-19)
- Throat lozenges, saline gargles, honey for sore throat
- Decongestants, antihistamines for nasal symptoms
- Dextromethorphan for cough (limited evidence)
- Continue all chronic medications unless contraindicated
- Monitor SpO2 at home if available (seek care if <94% or declining)

## 10.4 Inpatient Treatment

### 10.4.1 Severe/Critical COVID-19: Evidence-Based Therapies

| Therapy | Indication | Dosing | Evidence | Notes |
|---------|-----------|--------|----------|-------|
| **Dexamethasone** | All patients requiring supplemental oxygen (SpO2 <94% on room air or needing any oxygen delivery device) | 6 mg PO/IV daily x 10 days (or until discharge) | RECOVERY trial (2020): 20% relative reduction in mortality; NNT ~8 for 28-day mortality in ventilated patients | Do NOT give if not requiring oxygen (potential harm in mild disease); first-line for severe/critical |
| **Remdesivir** | Hospitalized patients with SpO2 <94% on room air who do NOT require mechanical ventilation or ECMO | 200 mg IV day 1, then 100 mg IV daily x 4 days (total 5 days) | ACTT-1 trial: Reduced time to clinical recovery by ~4 days; WHO Solidarity trial showed no mortality benefit (conflicting evidence) | Consider in patients with oxygen requirement but not intubated; may stop if discharged before day 5 |
| **Tocilizumab** | Severe/critical disease with elevated inflammatory markers (CRP >=75 mg/L or IL-6 elevated) AND receiving dexamethasone | 8 mg/kg IV single dose (max 800 mg) | REMAP-CAP/RECOVERY: Reduced mortality when added to dexamethasone in severe/critical disease; NNT ~20 | IL-6 receptor antagonist; check for active infection, TB, hepatitis B reactivation risk |
| **Baricitinib** | Alternative to tocilizumab; severe/critical disease | 4 mg PO daily x 14 days or until discharge | ACTT-2 trial: Reduced time to recovery when added to remdesivir; TACTIC-R trial supports use with dexamethasone | JAK inhibitor; thrombosis risk (use with prophylactic anticoagulation); check for active infection |

**Organizing Principles for Inpatient Management:**
- Empiric antibiotics NOT recommended unless strong suspicion of bacterial superinfection (elevated procalcitonin, purulent sputum, focal consolidation on imaging, lack of viral prodrome)
- NIV (BiPAP) or high-flow nasal cannula (HFNC) before intubation if possible (avoid intubation if patient can be managed non-invasively)
- Prone positioning for awake hypoxemic patients (SpO2/FiO2 <200) not on mechanical ventilation - improves oxygenation in approximately 70% of patients
- Lung-protective ventilation strategy if intubated: low tidal volume (6 mL/kg ideal body weight), plateau pressure <30 cm H2O, driving pressure <15 cm H2O
- Prone positioning for ventilated patients with ARDS (PaO2/FiO2 <150): >=16 hours daily
- VTE prophylaxis: Prophylactic-dose heparin for all hospitalized patients unless contraindicated; consider therapeutic anticoagulation in patients with high D-dimer and no bleeding risk (controversial - ATTACC/ACTIV-4a/REMAP-CAP showed modest benefit)

### 10.4.2 WHO Severity-Based Treatment Algorithm

| Severity | Oxygen Status | Treatment |
|----------|--------------|-----------|
| **Mild** | Room air (SpO2 >=94%) | Supportive care; consider antiviral if high risk |
| **Moderate** | SpO2 90-93% on room air | Supplemental oxygen (nasal cannula or simple mask); remdesivir if high risk and within 7 days; dexamethasone if oxygen requirement progresses |
| **Severe** | SpO2 <90% on room air; requiring significant supplemental oxygen | Oxygen; dexamethasone 6 mg daily; remdesivir; tocilizumab or baricitinib if CRP >=75 and progressive |
| **Critical** | Mechanical ventilation or ECMO | ICU care; lung-protective ventilation; proning >=16h/day; dexamethasone; IL-6 inhibitor or JAK inhibitor; ECMO if refractory (CESAR/ECMO criteria) |

## 10.5 Long COVID / Post-Acute Sequelae of COVID-19 (PASC) [^76^] [^77^] [^79^]

### 10.5.1 Definition and Epidemiology
Persistent symptoms beyond 12 weeks after acute SARS-CoV-2 infection that cannot be explained by alternative diagnosis. Affects approximately 10-30% of infected individuals (higher after severe acute illness, hospitalization, or in those with multiple comorbidities; lower after mild infection and in vaccinated individuals). Female sex, middle age, and pre-existing anxiety/depression are risk factors.

### 10.5.2 Common Symptoms

| Symptom Category | Specific Symptoms | Prevalence | Notes |
|-----------------|------------------|------------|-------|
| **General** | Fatigue (most common and disabling), post-exertional symptom exacerbation (PESE), low-grade fever | 35-77% | PESE is hallmark - disproportionate worsening of symptoms after physical, cognitive, or emotional exertion |
| **Respiratory** | Dyspnea, persistent cough, chest tightness, reduced exercise tolerance | 43% at 60 days | May persist for months; pulmonary function tests may show reduced DLCO |
| **Neurologic** | Cognitive impairment ("brain fog" - difficulty with concentration, memory, word-finding), headache, dizziness, sleep disturbances, peripheral neuropathy | 28% cognitive impairment | May reflect microvascular injury, neuroinflammation, or autonomic dysfunction |
| **Autonomic** | POTS (postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome), orthostatic intolerance, palpitations, temperature dysregulation | Common in younger patients | Tachycardia >30 bpm increase upon standing without hypotension |
| **Sensory** | Anosmia/ageusia (loss of smell/taste), photophobia, tinnitus | Up to 46% at 1 month | Often improves over 6-12 months but may persist; hyposmia more common than complete anosmia |
| **Psychiatric** | Anxiety, depression, PTSD, mood lability | 25-30% | May be primary or secondary to disability; social isolation during acute illness may contribute |
| **Musculoskeletal** | Myalgias, arthralgias, muscle weakness | Common | May reflect deconditioning, inflammatory response, or myopathic process |
| **Gastrointestinal** | Nausea, diarrhea, abdominal pain, altered bowel habits | 10-20% | May reflect persistent gut dysbiosis or autonomic dysfunction |

### 10.5.3 Proposed Mechanisms
- Persistent viral reservoirs or reactivation
- Chronic immune dysregulation (autoantibodies, T-cell exhaustion)
- Microvascular endothelial dysfunction
- Mitochondrial dysfunction
- Dysautonomia
- Gut microbiome alterations
- Neuroinflammation
- Tissue fibrosis (pulmonary, cardiac)

### 10.5.4 Management (Symptom-Directed) [^77^] [^79^]

> **No approved disease-modifying therapy for long COVID exists.** Management is multidisciplinary, symptom-based, and supportive. The following approaches are based on clinical experience, pathophysiologic rationale, and emerging evidence.

| Symptom | Management Approach | Evidence Level |
|---------|-------------------|----------------|
| **Fatigue / PESE** | Pacing and energy conservation (most important - avoid "pushing through"); graded return to activity only if no PESE; sleep optimization; address comorbidities (anemia, thyroid, depression); low-dose naltrexone (1.5-4.5 mg) has emerging evidence | Clinical consensus; pacing well-established in ME/CFS literature |
| **Dyspnea** | Breathing exercises (pursed-lip, diaphragmatic); pulmonary rehabilitation if objective impairment; bronchodilators if reactive airways; treat comorbid cardiac/pulmonary conditions | Pulmonary rehabilitation has strongest evidence |
| **Brain fog / Cognitive impairment** | Cognitive rehabilitation; pacing of cognitive activities; address sleep/pain/mood; minimize distractions; compensatory strategies (lists, calendars); no proven pharmacologic therapy; some evidence for methylphenidate in selected cases | Emerging evidence for cognitive rehab |
| **Anosmia / Hyposmia** | Olfactory training (4 scents: rose, eucalyptus, clove, lemon; sniff each for 20-30 seconds, twice daily, for 3-6 months); intranasal corticosteroids may help if concurrent rhinitis; majority recover within 12 months | Moderate evidence for olfactory training |
| **POTS / Orthostatic intolerance** | Hydration (2-3 liters/day); increased salt intake; compression garments (abdominal and lower extremity); physical counter-maneuvers; recumbent or aquatic exercise initially; beta-blockers (propranolol 10-20 mg TID) or ivabradine; fludrocortisone; midodrine | Clinical experience; POTS treatment protocols well-established |
| **Cough** | Airway clearance techniques; breathing exercises; cough suppressants (dextromethorphan, benzonatate) for short-term relief; inhaled corticosteroids if bronchial hyperresponsiveness; gabapentin or amitriptyline for neurogenic cough | Limited evidence specific to long COVID |
| **Pain** | NSAIDs/acetaminophen; physical therapy; neuropathic agents (gabapentin, pregabalin, duloxetine) if neuropathic component; low-dose naltrexone |
| **Anxiety / Depression** | SSRIs/SNRIs; psychological support; CBT; mindfulness-based interventions; peer support groups; graded activity within energy envelope |
| **Sleep disorders** | Sleep hygiene optimization; CBT-I for insomnia; evaluate for OSA; melatonin; low-dose trazodone or mirtazapine if needed |

### 10.5.5 Evaluation Framework for Long COVID
- Comprehensive history focusing on pre-COVID baseline vs. current functional status
- Targeted workup to exclude alternative or contributing diagnoses:
  - CBC (anemia), CMP (renal, hepatic, electrolytes), TSH
  - BNP/NT-proBNP (cardiac involvement), troponin if indicated
  - ECG, consider echocardiogram (myocarditis, pericarditis, LV dysfunction)
  - Chest X-ray; consider CT chest if persistent respiratory symptoms (interstitial changes, organizing pneumonia)
  - Spirometry, DLCO, 6-minute walk test
  - Ferritin, CRP (persistent inflammation), vitamin D, vitamin B12
- Multidisciplinary referral based on predominant symptoms:
  - Primary care (coordinator)
  - Pulmonology (respiratory symptoms)
  - Cardiology (chest pain, palpitations, dyspnea with cardiac features)
  - Neurology (cognitive impairment, headache, neuropathy)
  - Psychiatry/Psychology (mood, anxiety, CBT)
  - Physiatry/Physical therapy (fatigue, deconditioning, POTS)
  - Occupational therapy (cognitive rehab, ADL support)
- Patient support groups and validated assessment tools (e.g., PROMIS, EQ-5D, MFI-20 for fatigue)

### 10.5.6 Prognosis
- Most patients show gradual improvement over 6-12 months
- Complete recovery occurs in approximately 30-50% by 12 months
- Persistent symptoms beyond 12 months may represent chronic condition
- Vaccination after acute infection may reduce long COVID risk or improve symptoms in some patients
- Reinfection increases risk of long COVID (additive or independent)

## 10.6 Prevention
- **Updated COVID-19 vaccines:** Recommended annually for all eligible individuals >=6 months; updated formulations target currently circulating variants
- **High-risk patients:** Should maintain up-to-date vaccination; may benefit from more frequent updates
- **Standard infection control:** Hand hygiene, respiratory etiquette (cover coughs/sneezes), improved ventilation
- **Pre-exposure prophylaxis:** Pemivibart (Pemgarda) authorized for selected immunocompromised patients who may not mount adequate vaccine response; check current FDA/CDC guidance as recommendations evolve
- **Post-exposure prophylaxis:** Not routinely recommended; monitor for symptoms and test

## 10.7 Red Flags

> **SEEK IMMEDIATE CARE:**
> - SpO2 <90% on room air (or <94% and declining despite home oxygen)
> - Severe dyspnea at rest or inability to speak full sentences
> - Chest pain suggestive of acute coronary syndrome (pressure, radiation, diaphoresis)
> - Altered mental status or confusion
> - Cyanosis (blue lips, fingertips, or facial skin)
> - Signs of sepsis (fever with tachycardia >120, hypotension, rigors)
> - New-onset focal neurologic deficits (possible stroke from COVID-19-associated coagulopathy)
> - Severe dehydration or inability to maintain oral intake
> - Oxygen requirement that exceeds home oxygen capacity

## 10.8 Special Populations

| Population | Considerations |
|-----------|----------------|
| **Immunocompromised** | Higher risk of severe disease, prolonged viral shedding, and breakthrough infection; may require extended treatment courses; pre-exposure prophylaxis should be considered; consult infectious disease |
| **Pregnancy** | Increased risk of severe disease (especially third trimester); Paxlovid can be used in pregnancy (benefit outweighs theoretical risk); remdesivir also acceptable; dexamethasone for severe disease is safe |
| **Children** | Generally milder disease; multisystem inflammatory syndrome in children (MIS-C) is rare but serious (4-6 weeks post-infection; fever + multi-organ involvement); treatment primarily supportive |
| **Elderly** | Highest risk of severe disease and mortality; early antiviral treatment strongly recommended even with mild symptoms; watch for atypical presentations (confusion, falls, functional decline without fever) |
| **Nursing home residents** | High transmission risk; early treatment essential; consider empiric treatment during outbreaks; vaccination of staff and residents is critical |

# Chapter 11: Pulmonary Embolism

## 11.1 Overview

Pulmonary embolism (PE) is a potentially life-threatening condition caused by thrombus migration to the pulmonary arterial circulation, most commonly originating from deep vein thrombosis (DVT) in the proximal lower extremities (iliac, femoral, popliteal veins). Less common sources include upper extremity veins (central venous catheters, pacemakers), right heart thrombus, and paradoxical embolism through a patent foramen ovale. PE is the third most common cause of cardiovascular death (after myocardial infarction and stroke), with an estimated annual incidence of 60-70 per 100,000 and case-fatality rate of 7-11% in the first 30 days. Prompt diagnosis and risk stratification are critical for appropriate management. The 2019 ESC Guidelines provide the current evidence-based framework [^30^].

**Pathophysiology:** Obstruction of pulmonary arterial branches increases pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR), leading to elevated right ventricular (RV) afterload. The RV dilates and dysfunction ensues, causing decreased left ventricular (LV) filling through ventricular interdependence (septal bowing impairs LV filling). Systemic hypotension occurs when >50% of pulmonary circulation is obstructed (massive PE). RV ischemia may occur due to increased wall tension and decreased coronary perfusion pressure. Release of vasoactive mediators (serotonin, thromboxane A2) causes additional pulmonary vasoconstriction, worsening ventilation-perfusion mismatch.

**Virchow's Triad (risk factors):**
1. **Stasis:** Immobilization, paralysis, long travel, obesity, pregnancy
2. **Hypercoagulability:** Factor V Leiden, prothrombin G20210A, antithrombin deficiency, protein C/S deficiency, antiphospholipid syndrome, malignancy, oral contraceptives, hormone replacement therapy
3. **Endothelial injury:** Trauma, surgery (especially orthopedic), central venous catheters, prior DVT/PE

## 11.2 Clinical Presentation

PE presentation is highly variable, ranging from asymptomatic (incidental finding on CT) to sudden cardiovascular collapse and death. The classic triad (dyspnea, chest pain, hemoptysis) is present in <20% of cases.

| Symptom/Sign | Frequency | Notes |
|-------------|-----------|-------|
| Dyspnea (sudden onset, unexplained) | 73-82% | Most common symptom; may be exertional or at rest |
| Pleuritic chest pain | 44-65% | Suggests peripheral PE causing pleural irritation/pulmonary infarction |
| Tachycardia (>100 bpm) | 26-60% | Nonspecific but common |
| Tachypnea (>20/min) | 42-70% | Early sign of respiratory compensation |
| Cough | 20-37% | Usually dry; may be productive with hemoptysis |
| Syncope | 6-15% | Indicates massive PE with hemodynamic compromise; high mortality |
| Hemoptysis | 5-13% | Indicates pulmonary infarction (peripheral PE); "red currant jelly" sputum |
| Signs of DVT (unilateral leg swelling, pain, warmth, erythema) | 15-32% | Pain/swelling in calf or thigh; Homan sign is unreliable |
| Hypotension (SBP <90) | 8-15% | Indicates massive PE; mortality >50% without treatment |
| Fever (low-grade) | 20-30% | Usually <38.5 C; if higher, consider infection |
| Diaphoresis | 10-20% | With hemodynamic compromise |
| Loud P2, right-sided S3/S4 | Variable | Signs of pulmonary hypertension/RV strain |
| Jugular venous distension | Variable | RV failure |

> **Wells Criteria Pre-test Probability:** The clinical presentation of PE is notoriously nonspecific. Most patients with suspected PE do NOT have PE (prevalence of PE in ED patients with suspicion is approximately 10-20%). Systematic diagnostic algorithms combining clinical probability assessment, D-dimer, and imaging are essential to avoid missed diagnoses and unnecessary testing.

## 11.3 Diagnostic Algorithm

### 11.3.1 Wells Score for PE [^30^]

| Variable | Points | Clinical Reasoning |
|----------|--------|-------------------|
| Clinical signs/symptoms of DVT (minimum: leg swelling and pain with palpation of deep veins) | 3 | Most important single factor; objective DVT signs strongly suggest PE |
| PE is the #1 diagnosis OR equally likely | 3 | Clinical gestalt; alternative diagnoses less likely |
| Heart rate >100 bpm | 1.5 | Tachycardia reflects hemodynamic stress |
| Immobilization >=3 days OR surgery in previous 4 weeks | 1.5 | Recent surgery (especially orthopedic, abdominal, pelvic) is major risk factor |
| Previous objectively diagnosed DVT/PE | 1.5 | Recurrence risk elevated after first event |
| Hemoptysis | 1 | Suggests pulmonary infarction |
| Malignancy (on treatment, treated within 6 months, or palliative) | 1 | Cancer is a major risk factor and prognostic factor |

| Score | Probability | Next Step | Prevalence of PE |
|-------|------------|-----------|-----------------|
| <=4 | PE unlikely (low-intermediate) | Check D-dimer; if negative, PE ruled out; if positive, proceed to CTPA | ~5-15% |
| >4 | PE likely (intermediate-high) | Proceed directly to CTPA (do not wait for D-dimer) | ~40-60% |

### 11.3.2 Revised Geneva Score (Alternative)

| Variable | Points |
|----------|--------|
| Age >65 years | 1 |
| Previous DVT/PE | 3 |
| Surgery or fracture within 1 month | 2 |
| Active malignancy | 2 |
| Unilateral lower limb pain | 3 |
| Hemoptysis | 2 |
| Heart rate 75-94 bpm | 3 |
| Heart rate >=95 bpm | 5 |
| Pain on lower limb palpation and unilateral edema | 4 |

| Score | Probability |
|-------|------------|
| 0-3 | Low (8% prevalence) |
| 4-10 | Intermediate (29% prevalence) |
| >=11 | High (74% prevalence) |

### 11.3.3 PERC Rule (Pulmonary Embolism Rule-out Criteria) [^30^]

For patients with **low pre-test probability (Wells <=4)**, if ALL 8 criteria are met, PE can be ruled out without D-dimer testing:

1. Age <50 years
2. Heart rate <100 bpm
3. O2 saturation >=95% on room air
4. No unilateral leg swelling (no calf or thigh asymmetry)
5. No hemoptysis
6. No recent surgery or trauma (within 4 weeks)
7. No prior DVT or PE
8. No hormone use (oral contraceptives, estrogen therapy, hormone replacement)

- PERC-negative patients have <2% risk of PE (safe to discharge without further testing)
- Can reduce CTPA utilization by 7-18% in low-risk patients [^30^]
- **Do NOT use PERC if Wells score >4** (PE likely)
- If any PERC criterion is positive, proceed to D-dimer testing

### 11.3.4 D-Dimer Interpretation [^30^]

D-dimer is a fibrin degradation product; elevated in virtually all patients with acute thrombosis but also elevated in many other conditions.

| Clinical Scenario | Recommended Threshold | Interpretation |
|-------------------|----------------------|----------------|
| Standard (age <=50) | <500 ng/mL FEU (<250 ng/mL D-dimer units) | Negative D-dimer effectively rules out PE in low-intermediate probability patients (NPV >99%) |
| Age-adjusted (age >50) | Age x 10 ng/mL FEU (e.g., 70-year-old: <700 ng/mL) | Reduces CTPA use by 11-20% in patients >50 years without sacrificing safety [^30^]; ADJUST-PE trial validated |
| High clinical probability (Wells >4) | D-dimer NOT recommended | Proceed directly to CTPA regardless of D-dimer (high false-negative rate in high probability) |

**Important Limitations of D-Dimer:**
- D-dimer is NOT useful in: hospitalized patients (high false-positive rate), post-surgical patients (elevated for weeks), pregnant patients (elevated in normal pregnancy), patients with cancer (often chronically elevated), patients >80 years (use age-adjusted cutoff), patients with recent infection or inflammation
- A negative D-dimer in low probability effectively excludes PE (negative predictive value >99%)
- A positive D-dimer does NOT diagnose PE (requires imaging confirmation)

### 11.3.5 Imaging

| Modality | Role | Sensitivity/Specificity | Considerations |
|----------|------|------------------------|----------------|
| **CT pulmonary angiography (CTPA)** | Gold standard for diagnosis; direct visualization of filling defects in pulmonary arterial tree | Sensitivity ~95%, Specificity ~95% for main/lobar/segmental PE | Radiation exposure (~2-5 mSv); iodinated contrast (nephrotoxicity, anaphylaxis); detects alternative diagnoses; limited sensitivity for subsegmental PE |
| **V/Q scan** | Alternative when CTPA contraindicated | Sensitivity ~95% for high-probability; high non-diagnostic rate (~50%) | Preferred in pregnancy (especially with normal CXR) and significant renal impairment; limited utility with abnormal baseline CXR (high non-diagnostic rate) |
| **Echocardiography** | Assess RV strain (indicates severity/prognosis); helps risk stratify | RV strain on echo has ~60-70% sensitivity for PE | RV dilation, hypokinesis, McConnell sign (RV free wall akinesis with preserved apex - 77% specific), elevated RVSP, septal bowing into LV; normal echo does NOT exclude PE |
| **Lower extremity ultrasound** | Evaluate for DVT | Sensitivity ~90% for proximal DVT | Positive DVT in setting of suspected PE = treat as PE without CTPA (same disease spectrum); serial testing if initial negative |
| **Chest X-ray** | Exclude other causes; assess for V/Q scan candidacy | Not diagnostic for PE | Westermark sign (regional oligemia), Hampton hump (wedge-shaped peripheral opacity = pulmonary infarction), elevated hemidiaphragm, pleural effusion |
| **MRI pulmonary angiography** | Alternative for selected patients (pregnancy, young patients needing multiple scans) | Emerging data; limited availability | No radiation; no iodinated contrast; gadolinium in pregnancy is controversial |

### 11.3.6 Pregnancy-Specific Considerations
- D-dimer is elevated in normal pregnancy (third trimester levels up to 3-4x non-pregnant), reducing specificity
- Modified Wells score or pregnancy-adapted YEARS algorithm preferred
- **YEARS algorithm for pregnancy:**
  - Clinical signs of DVT (3 points), hemoptysis (2 points), PE most likely diagnosis (2 points)
  - If score <=1 and D-dimer <1000 ng/mL: PE excluded
  - If score >=2 and D-dimer <500 ng/mL: PE excluded
  - Otherwise: CTPA or V/Q scan
- V/Q scan may be preferred over CTPA in pregnancy (especially with normal CXR) to reduce maternal breast radiation; however, CTPA with low-dose protocol and breast shielding is also acceptable
- If CTPA needed, use low-dose protocol (reduced kVp) with breast shielding
- Fetal radiation exposure is very low with both V/Q and CTPA (<1 mGy - well below teratogenic threshold)

### 11.3.7 Diagnostic Algorithm Summary

1. Assess clinical probability (Wells score or revised Geneva)
2. If low probability: Apply PERC rule
   - PERC negative: PE ruled out, no further testing
   - PERC positive: D-dimer testing
     - D-dimer negative: PE ruled out
     - D-dimer positive: CTPA
3. If intermediate probability: D-dimer testing
   - D-dimer negative: PE ruled out
   - D-dimer positive: CTPA
4. If high probability: Proceed directly to CTPA (do not obtain D-dimer)
5. If CTPA contraindicated: V/Q scan or lower extremity ultrasound

## 11.4 Risk Stratification

Risk stratification guides the intensity of treatment and level of monitoring.

| Category | Definition | Features | Management |
|----------|-----------|----------|------------|
| **Massive PE** | Acute PE with sustained hypotension (SBP <90 mmHg for >15 minutes not due to arrhythmia, hypovolemia, sepsis, or LV dysfunction) OR pulselessness OR persistent profound bradycardia (HR <40 with signs of shock) | Cardiogenic shock, syncope, cardiac arrest, end-organ hypoperfusion | Systemic thrombolysis OR surgical/catheter embolectomy; ICU admission; hemodynamic support |
| **Submassive (intermediate-high risk)** | Normotensive PE with RV strain on imaging OR elevated cardiac biomarkers (troponin, BNP) | RV dilation on echo or CTPA (RV/LV ratio >1); elevated troponin; elevated BNP/NT-proBNP | Anticoagulation + close monitoring (ICU or step-down); consider thrombolysis if clinical deterioration |
| **Submassive (intermediate-low risk)** | Normotensive PE with RV dilation on imaging but normal biomarkers | RV/LV ratio >1 on CTPA; normal troponin and BNP | Anticoagulation; inpatient observation |
| **Low risk** | Normotensive PE with no RV strain AND normal cardiac biomarkers | No RV dilation; normal troponin and BNP; PESI Class I-II or sPESI = 0 | Anticoagulation; may consider early discharge or outpatient management (Hestia criteria) |

**RV Strain Criteria:**
- CTPA: RV/LV diameter ratio >1.0 (measured in axial plane at mid-level)
- Echocardiography: RV dilation, RV hypokinesis, septal flattening/D-shaped LV, McConnell sign, TR velocity >2.6 m/s
- Biomarkers: Elevated troponin I or T (myocardial injury from RV ischemia), elevated BNP >500 pg/mL or NT-proBNP >900 pg/mL (RV stretch)

**sPESI (Simplified Pulmonary Embolism Severity Index):**
- Age >80 (1 point), history of cancer (1), history of chronic cardiopulmonary disease (1), heart rate >=110 (1), SBP <100 (1), O2 saturation <90% (1)
- sPESI = 0: Low risk (30-day mortality <1%); sPESI >=1: High risk (30-day mortality up to 10%)

**Hestia Criteria for Outpatient PE Treatment:**
PE patients can be considered for outpatient management if NONE of the following are present:
- Hemodynamically unstable
- Thrombolysis or embolectomy needed
- Active bleeding or high bleeding risk
- Need for supplemental oxygen to maintain SpO2 >90%
- Severe pain requiring IV analgesia
- Acute medical illness requiring hospitalization
- Severe renal impairment (CrCl <30)
- Severe liver impairment
- Pregnant
- Documented history of non-compliance
- Unable to obtain or administer anticoagulation
- Social circumstances precluding outpatient care

## 11.5 Treatment

### 11.5.1 Initial Anticoagulation (All Confirmed PE)

Anticoagulation should be initiated immediately upon diagnostic confirmation (or even empirically in high-probability cases while awaiting imaging, unless absolute contraindication exists).

| Agent | Dosing | Notes |
|-------|--------|-------|
| **Apixaban** | 10 mg BID x 7 days, then 5 mg BID | Preferred DOAC; no initial parenteral required; lower bleeding risk; no food requirement |
| **Rivaroxaban** | 15 mg BID x 21 days, then 20 mg daily | Take with evening meal; no initial parenteral required |
| **Edoxaban** | 60 mg daily (after 5-10 days of parenteral anticoagulation) | Reduce to 30 mg if CrCl 15-50, weight <=60 kg, or on certain P-gp inhibitors |
| **Dabigatran** | 150 mg BID (after 5-10 days of parenteral anticoagulation) | Less preferred due to GI side effects; renal clearance predominant |
| **Enoxaparin** | 1 mg/kg SC q12h (or 1.5 mg/kg daily if CrCl <30) | LMWH; bridging or if DOAC contraindicated; dose-reduce if obesity or renal impairment |
| **Unfractionated heparin IV** | 80 U/kg bolus, then 18 U/kg/hr infusion (titrate to aPTT 1.5-2.5x control or anti-Xa 0.3-0.7) | If thrombolysis or intervention anticipated; severe renal impairment; rapid reversal needed |
| **Fondaparinux** | 5 mg (<50 kg), 7.5 mg (50-100 kg), 10 mg (>100 kg) SC daily | Alternative to LMWH; avoid if CrCl <30; no HIT risk |

**DOACs are preferred over warfarin** for most patients with PE (Class I recommendation) due to:
- Lower major bleeding risk (especially intracranial hemorrhage)
- No INR monitoring required
- Fewer drug-food interactions
- Rapid onset (no bridging needed for apixaban and rivaroxaban)
- Comparable or superior efficacy

**Warfarin remains preferred for:**
- Severe renal impairment (CrCl <15 or dialysis)
- Antiphospholipid syndrome with triple positivity
- Mechanical heart valves
- Patient preference (cost, established routine)
- Some cases of extensive proximal DVT where monitoring is desired

**Duration of Anticoagulation:**

| Clinical Scenario | Duration | Rationale |
|-------------------|----------|-----------|
| Provoked/transient risk factor (surgery, trauma, immobilization, pregnancy, estrogen) | 3 months | Recurrence risk low after transient factor resolves |
| Unprovoked first PE | Minimum 3 months; consider extended/indefinite if low bleeding risk | Recurrence risk ~10% at 1 year, ~30% at 5 years after stopping |
| Unprovoked PE with low bleeding risk | Indefinite anticoagulation preferred | NNT ~25 to prevent one recurrent VTE over 5 years |
| Recurrent VTE (any) | Indefinite anticoagulation | Very high recurrence risk |
| Active cancer | Indefinite (while cancer active or on treatment) | LMWH or DOAC preferred |
| Thrombophilia (antiphospholipid syndrome, homozygous Factor V Leiden, prothrombin G20210A homozygous, combined defects) | Indefinite | High recurrence risk |
| Isolated distal DVT (provoked) | 3 months if proximal extension excluded | Lower recurrence risk |
| Isolated distal DVT (unprovoked) | 3 months; extended if ongoing risk | Individualize |

### 11.5.2 Thrombolysis

**Indications:**
- **Massive PE (hemodynamic compromise):** Systemic thrombolysis is indicated unless contraindicated
- **Submassive PE with deterioration:** Consider if signs of clinical worsening despite anticoagulation (worsening RV function, increasing oxygen requirement, persistent hypotension)

**Contraindications:**
- **Absolute:** Active internal bleeding, recent intracranial hemorrhage, known intracranial tumor/AV malformation, ischemic stroke within 3 months, suspected aortic dissection
- **Relative:** Recent surgery/trauma (<3 weeks), uncontrolled hypertension (SBP >180), pregnancy, bleeding diathesis, CPR >10 minutes, bacterial endocarditis

**Agents:**
- Alteplase (tPA): 100 mg IV over 2 hours (most studied; 10 mg bolus then 90 mg infusion)
- Tenecteplase: Weight-based single IV bolus (30-50 mg based on weight) - preferred for ease of administration and equivalent efficacy
- Streptokinase and urokinase are less commonly used

**Evidence:** Systemic thrombolysis reduces mortality in massive PE (NNT ~7) but increases major bleeding (including intracranial hemorrhage ~2-3%). In submassive PE, thrombolysis prevents hemodynamic decompensation but increases bleeding without clear mortality benefit (PEITHO trial).

### 11.5.3 Embolectomy and Interventions

| Procedure | Indication | Notes |
|-----------|-----------|-------|
| **Catheter-directed therapy (CDT)** | Submassive or massive PE; intermediate-high risk patients | Includes catheter-directed thrombolysis (low-dose tPA infused directly into thrombus) and mechanical/suction embolectomy (AngioVac, FlowTriever); lower systemic bleeding risk than IV thrombolysis; requires specialized centers |
| **Surgical embolectomy** | Massive PE with failed thrombolysis or absolute contraindication to thrombolysis; pre-arrest or arrest | Requires cardiac surgery team and bypass capability; mortality 20-50% depending on patient condition |
| **IVC filter** | Only if anticoagulation is absolutely contraindicated AND acute proximal DVT/PE is present | Does NOT treat PE; prevents future emboli; remove when anticoagulation becomes safe; routine use NOT recommended (evidence of harm) |

### 11.5.4 Follow-Up and Long-Term Complications
- Early follow-up within 1-2 weeks to assess symptoms, bleeding, anticoagulation tolerance
- Consider cardiopulmonary exercise testing and echocardiogram at 3-6 months if persistent dyspnea or exercise limitation
- **Chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension (CTEPH):** Occurs in 2-4% of PE survivors; develops months to years after acute PE; presents with progressive dyspnea, exercise limitation, RV failure; screen with echocardiogram at 3-6 months; curable with pulmonary thromboendarterectomy (PTE) or balloon pulmonary angioplasty (BPA)
- **Post-PE syndrome:** Persistent functional limitation after PE; may include residual dyspnea, reduced exercise capacity, quality of life impairment; pulmonary rehabilitation may help

## 11.6 Special Populations

| Population | Considerations |
|-----------|----------------|
| **Pregnancy** | LMWH is treatment of choice throughout pregnancy and for at least 6 weeks postpartum (minimum 3 months total); DOACs are contraindicated (cross placenta; potential fetal bleeding and developmental effects); warfarin is contraindicated in first trimester (teratogenic) and third trimester (fetal hemorrhage); V/Q scan preferred over CTPA if normal CXR |
| **Cancer** | LMWH preferred for first 6 months (NCCN guidelines); edoxaban and apixaban are acceptable alternatives (SELECT-D and Hokusai-VTE-Cancer trials showed comparable efficacy but higher GI/genitourinary bleeding with edoxaban); consider indefinite anticoagulation while cancer active |
| **Renal impairment** | Adjust DOAC dosing; LMWH may require anti-Xa monitoring if CrCl <30; UFH preferred in severe renal impairment (dialysis, CrCl <15) due to reversibility and shorter half-life |
| **Elderly** | Increased bleeding risk with all agents; careful weight-based dosing; increased fall risk (but falls are rarely the cause of major bleeding on anticoagulation); DOACs generally preferred over warfarin (lower ICH risk) |
| **Obesity** (BMI >40 or weight >120 kg) | Standard DOAC dosing may be insufficient; consider LMWH with anti-Xa monitoring; apixaban and rivaroxaban may be preferred DOACs; evidence evolving for high-dose strategies |
| **Extremes of weight** (<50 kg) | Increased bleeding risk; consider dose reduction; rivaroxaban and apixaban have specific reduced-dose criteria |

## 11.7 Red Flags

> **EMERGENCY INDICATORS - SEEK IMMEDIATE CARE:**
> - Syncope with suspected PE (indicates hemodynamically significant clot burden; high mortality)
> - Hypotension (SBP <90 mmHg sustained) with confirmed or suspected PE
> - Cardiac arrest with suspected PE (consider thrombolysis during CPR if PE is likely cause)
> - RV strain on echocardiogram or CTPA (RV/LV ratio >1) with elevated troponin
> - Active bleeding while on anticoagulation (major GI bleed, intracranial hemorrhage, retroperitoneal bleed)
> - Progressive hypoxemia despite supplemental oxygen
> - Sudden worsening dyspnea in patient with known DVT (suspect extension/embolization)
> - Hemoptysis with massive PE (indicates pulmonary infarction but may also reflect anti-coagulation complication)

## 11.8 Prevention

| Strategy | Indication | Approach |
|----------|-----------|----------|
| **Pharmacologic prophylaxis** | Hospitalized medical patients, post-surgical patients | LMWH, UFH, or fondaparinux; continue until ambulatory |
| **Mechanical prophylaxis** | Contraindication to pharmacologic prophylaxis | Intermittent pneumatic compression devices; graduated compression stockings |
| **Early ambulation** | All post-operative and hospitalized patients | Mobilize as soon as medically safe |
| **Extended prophylaxis post-orthopedic surgery** | Total hip replacement, total knee replacement, hip fracture surgery | LMWH or DOAC for 10-14 days (THA/TKA) or 28-35 days (hip fracture) |
| **Extended prophylaxis in high-risk cancer** | Khorana score >=2 (high-risk ambulatory cancer patients receiving chemotherapy) | LMWH or rivaroxaban 10 mg daily |
| **Travel prophylaxis** | Long-haul flights (>6-8 hours) in high-risk patients | Aisle seat, frequent ambulation, calf exercises, hydration; consider LMWH for very high risk |

# Chapter 12: Dyspnea Assessment

## 12.1 Overview

Dyspnea is a subjective sensation of uncomfortable or difficult breathing. It is one of the most common presenting complaints in emergency medicine and primary care, accounting for 3-4% of all ED visits and up to 25% of ambulatory care visits. The differential diagnosis is broad, spanning cardiac, pulmonary, hematologic, metabolic, neuromuscular, and psychiatric causes. A systematic approach is essential for accurate diagnosis, appropriate management, and safe disposition. This chapter provides a comprehensive framework for the evaluation of acute and chronic dyspnea, with emphasis on rapid differentiation between life-threatening and benign causes [^80^] [^81^] [^82^].

**Definition:** Dyspnea is a subjective experience of breathing discomfort that consists of qualitatively distinct sensations that vary in intensity. The experience derives from interactions among multiple physiological, psychological, social, and environmental factors, and may induce secondary physiological and behavioral responses. (American Thoracic Society definition)

## 12.2 Approach to Acute Dyspnea

### 12.2.1 Initial Assessment (First 60 Seconds - "ABCD")

| Parameter | Assessment | Red Flag Findings | Immediate Action |
|-----------|-----------|-------------------|-----------------|
| **A - Airway** | Patency, ability to speak, stridor | Complete obstruction, inability to speak, altered phonation, severe stridor | Head positioning, jaw thrust, suction, advanced airway if needed |
| **B - Breathing** | Rate, pattern, effort, accessory muscle use, symmetry | RR >30 or <8, tripod position, paradoxical breathing, severe retractions, cyanosis, absent breath sounds unilaterally | Supplemental oxygen, assist ventilation if needed |
| **C - Circulation** | BP, HR, peripheral perfusion (capillary refill, temperature), JVD | SBP <90, HR >130 or <45, cool clammy extremities, mottling, pulsus paradoxus >10 mmHg | IV access, fluids, vasopressors if needed |
| **D - Disability (mental status)** | Alertness, orientation, ability to follow commands | Confusion, agitation, drowsiness, coma | Indicates severe hypoxia, hypercapnia, or shock; prepare for intubation |
| **O2 saturation** | Pulse oximetry on room air | SpO2 <85% (critical), <90% (severe), <94% (concerning) | Supplemental oxygen; target appropriate saturation |

### 12.2.2 History (Targeted)
- **Onset:** Sudden (seconds-minutes: PE, pneumothorax, arrhythmia, anaphylaxis, foreign body) vs. gradual (hours-days: HF, pneumonia, COPD exacerbation, anemia)
- **Triggers:** Exertion, positional change (orthopnea, PND), allergens, cold air, emotional stress, meals
- **Associated symptoms:** Chest pain, cough, fever, wheeze, hemoptysis, palpitations, lower extremity edema
- **Past medical history:** Cardiac disease, lung disease, anemia, thromboembolism, cancer, renal disease
- **Risk factors:** Smoking, occupational exposures, travel, immobilization, surgery, medications (beta-blockers, NSAIDs, chemotherapeutics)
- **Quantify severity:** How far can the patient walk on flat ground? How many flights of stairs? Has this changed recently?

### 12.2.3 Cardiac vs. Pulmonary Dyspnea: Key Differentiating Features

| Feature | Cardiac Dyspnea | Pulmonary Dyspnea |
|---------|----------------|-------------------|
| **Onset** | Gradual (CHF decompensation over days) or sudden (flash pulmonary edema, acute MI, arrhythmia) | Variable; may be gradual (COPD, ILD) or sudden (PE, pneumothorax, asthma, foreign body) |
| **Positional effect** | Orthopnea (dyspnea when supine) is highly specific for CHF; paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnea (waking breathless at night, relieved by sitting) is virtually diagnostic of CHF | Less common except in severe COPD/asthma; pleuritic pain worsens with inspiration |
| **Exertional pattern** | Exertional dyspnea that improves with rest; progressively worsening over time; may have angina equivalent symptoms | Exertional dyspnea with prolonged recovery; may be triggered by specific exposures |
| **Jugular venous distension** | Present in right heart failure (volume overload) | Absent unless cor pulmonale (COPD with right heart failure) |
| **Peripheral edema** | Bilateral, dependent, pitting edema (ankles/sacrum); hepatojugular reflux | Absent (unless cor pulmonale or hypoalbuminemia) |
| **Wheezing** | "Cardiac asthma" (pink frothy sputum from pulmonary edema); may improve rapidly with diuretics and nitrates | True wheezing (polyphonic, expiratory) in asthma/COPD; monophonic wheeze suggests central obstruction |
| **Crackles** | Bilateral basal, fine, late-inspiratory (pulmonary edema); may have diffuse crackles in acute pulmonary edema | Focal, coarse crackles (pneumonia); diffuse fine crackles (ILD, atypical pneumonia); may be absent (COPD, PE) |
| **S3 gallop** | Present in decompensated HFrEF (rapid ventricular filling into stiff/dilated ventricle) | Absent (S3 is pathologic in adults >40 and indicates LV dysfunction) |
| **Heart sounds** | S3, S4, murmurs (valvular disease), pericardial friction rub | Loud P2 (pulmonary hypertension), right-sided S4 (cor pulmonale) |
| **Blood pressure** | Hypertensive (flash pulmonary edema) or hypotensive (cardiogenic shock) | Usually normal unless massive PE, tension pneumothorax, or severe asthma |

## 12.3 Diagnostic Workup

### 12.3.1 Bedside Assessment (Essential for All)

| Test | Findings | Key Indications |
|------|----------|----------------|
| **ECG** | Ischemia (ST changes, T-wave inversion, new Q waves), arrhythmia (AF with RVR, VT, bradyarrhythmia), PE (S1Q3T3 pattern - large S in I, Q and inverted T in III), RV strain (RBBB, right axis deviation, T-wave inversion V1-V4), pericarditis (diffuse ST elevation, PR depression), LVH | All patients with acute dyspnea of unclear cause |
| **Chest X-ray** | Pulmonary edema (batwing/butterfly pattern, cephalization, Kerley B lines, pleural effusions), pneumonia (consolidation, infiltrates), pneumothorax (visceral pleural line, absence of lung markings), pleural effusion (blunting of costophrenic angles), cardiomegaly, ILD pattern | All patients with acute dyspnea (unless clearly musculoskeletal/anxiety and no red flags) |
| **Arterial blood gas (ABG)** | Hypoxemia (PaO2 <80), hypercapnia (PaCO2 >45), respiratory acidosis (pH <7.35), metabolic acidosis with compensatory hyperventilation (low PaCO2), elevated A-a gradient | Severe dyspnea, SpO2 <90%, altered mental status, known COPD, suspected PE |

### 12.3.2 Biomarkers [^80^] [^81^] [^82^] [^84^]

| Biomarker | Cardiac Indication | Pulmonary Indication | Cutoff Values | Interpretation |
|-----------|-------------------|---------------------|---------------|----------------|
| **BNP/NT-proBNP** | Elevated in HF; strongly supports cardiac cause; correlates with severity [^80^] | May be mildly elevated with PE (RV strain), but generally lower than in HF; elevated in COPD exacerbations with cor pulmonale | NT-proBNP <300 pg/mL: Acute HF unlikely (NPV 98%); NT-proBNP >450 (age <50), >900 (age 50-75), >1800 (age >75): Acute HF likely; BNP <100 pg/mL: HF unlikely; BNP >400: HF likely | Age-adjusted cutoffs improve accuracy; obesity falsely lowers BNP; renal failure elevates NT-proBNP without HF |
| **Troponin** | ACS, demand ischemia, myocarditis | PE (RV strain causes micro-injury; troponin elevation indicates submassive/massive PE), severe sepsis, COPD exacerbation with cor pulmonale | Any elevation above 99th percentile is abnormal; dynamic changes suggest ACS | hs-troponin detects more chronic low-level elevations |
| **D-dimer** | May be elevated in acute decompensated HF | PE evaluation; elevated in most hospitalized patients, post-surgical patients, pregnancy | Age-adjusted cutoff (age x 10 ng/mL if >50 years); <500 ng/mL excludes PE in low-intermediate probability | High false-positive rate; NOT useful in high-probability patients |
| **CRP** | Nonspecific elevation possible | Bacterial pneumonia (elevated), viral infection (modestly elevated), COPD exacerbation | No specific diagnostic cutoff; trend may guide antibiotic response | Nonspecific inflammatory marker |
| **Procalcitonin** | Not specific | Bacterial pneumonia (>0.25 ng/mL suggests bacterial; >0.5 ng/mL strongly suggests); guides antibiotic initiation and discontinuation | <0.1 ng/mL: Bacterial infection unlikely; >0.25 ng/mL: Consider antibiotics; >0.5 ng/mL: Antibiotics indicated | Also elevated in severe sepsis, major surgery, trauma |

**BNP/NT-proBNP Interpretation for Dyspnea Differentiation:**

| Clinical Context | NT-proBNP | Interpretation |
|-----------------|-----------|----------------|
| Acute dyspnea, no prior HF | >900 pg/mL | Acute HF likely |
| Acute dyspnea, known HF | >1000 pg/mL above baseline | Decompensated HF |
| Acute dyspnea | <300 pg/mL | Acute HF very unlikely (NPV 98%) |
| Chronic dyspnea | >400 pg/mL | Chronic HF likely |
| NYHA II | Median ~531 ng/L | Mild elevation [^80^] |
| NYHA III | Median ~2,675 ng/L | Moderate elevation [^80^] |
| NYHA IV | Median ~5,724 ng/L | Marked elevation [^80^] |

> **PRIDE Study findings:** Median NT-proBNP in acute CHF: 4,054 pg/mL vs. 131 pg/mL in non-cardiac dyspnea [^84^]. Age-adjusted cutoffs improve diagnostic accuracy in elderly patients.

### 12.3.3 Additional Testing (Guided by Presentation)

| Test | Indication | Expected Findings |
|------|-----------|-------------------|
| **Echocardiography** | Suspected cardiac cause, elevated BNP, new murmur, suspected PE with RV strain, pericardial effusion/tamponade | LV function (EF), wall motion abnormalities (ischemia), valvular disease, RV size/function, pericardial effusion, estimated PA pressure |
| **CT pulmonary angiography (CTPA)** | Suspected PE | Filling defects in pulmonary arteries; may incidentally find other causes (pneumonia, ILD, effusion) |
| **CT chest (non-contrast)** | Pneumothorax, ILD pattern, aortic pathology, bronchiectasis, emphysema, masses | Parenchymal lung disease, aortic dissection, bullae, lymphadenopathy |
| **Spirometry / Pulmonary function tests** | Chronic dyspnea evaluation | Obstructive pattern (COPD, asthma: FEV1/FVC <0.70), restrictive pattern (ILD, obesity, neuromuscular: FVC reduced, FEV1/FVC normal or elevated), diffusion impairment (DLCO reduced in ILD, emphysema, PE) |
| **Cardiac stress testing** | Suspected ischemic cause, intermediate-risk chest pain | Inducible ischemia, fixed perfusion defects |
| **Complete blood count** | Anemia evaluation, infection | Hb <7 g/dL often symptomatic; Hb <10 g/dL may contribute to dyspnea; leukocytosis suggests infection |
| **Thyroid function tests** | Thyroid disease suspected | Hyperthyroidism (tachycardia, tremor, weight loss); hypothyroidism (effusions, myxedema, diastolic HTN) |
| **Renal function panel** | AKI, CKD, acid-base | Elevated creatinine, acidosis |
| **Liver function tests** | Hepatic disease, right heart failure | Elevated LFTs, hypoalbuminemia |
| **BNP/NT-proBNP** | Cardiac vs. non-cardiac dyspnea differentiation | See above |

## 12.4 Common Causes of Dyspnea: Comprehensive Framework

### 12.4.1 Cardiac Causes (Most Common Systemic Cause)

| Cause | Key Features | Diagnostic Test | Prevalence in ED Dyspnea |
|-------|-------------|-----------------|-------------------------|
| **Acute decompensated heart failure** | Orthopnea, JVD, peripheral edema, S3 gallop, pulmonary crackles, CXR showing pulmonary edema | BNP/NT-proBNP, echocardiogram, CXR | ~35-50% |
| **Acute coronary syndrome** | Chest pressure, diaphoresis, nausea, dyspnea may be angina equivalent (especially in women, elderly, diabetics) | Troponin, ECG, coronary angiography/CTA | ~10-15% |
| **Arrhythmias** (AF with RVR, VT, SVT, bradyarrhythmia) | Irregular or rapid pulse, palpitations, sudden onset/offset of symptoms, syncope | ECG, telemetry | ~5-10% |
| **Acute valvular emergency** | New murmur, flash pulmonary edema, hemodynamic collapse (papillary muscle rupture post-MI causing acute MR, chordal rupture causing acute MR, acute AR from endocarditis/dissection) | Echocardiogram (emergent) | ~1-2% |
| **Pericardial tamponade** | Beck's triad, pulsus paradoxus >10 mmHg, elevated JVD, muffled heart sounds | Echocardiogram | <1% |
| **Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy** | Younger patient, syncope with exertion, harsh systolic murmur at LLSB that increases with Valsalva/decreases with squatting, family history | Echocardiogram (asymmetric septal hypertrophy) | <1% |
| **Pulmonary hypertension** | Exertional dyspnea, loud P2, right heart strain on ECG, signs of right heart failure | Echocardiogram (estimated RVSP), right heart catheterization | ~1-2% |
| **Constrictive pericarditis** | Chronic dyspnea, Kussmaul sign (JVD increases with inspiration), pericardial knock, elevated JVD, peripheral edema, ascites | Echocardiogram, cardiac MRI, CT chest | Rare |

### 12.4.2 Pulmonary Causes (Most Common Overall)

| Cause | Key Features | Diagnostic Test | Prevalence in ED Dyspnea |
|-------|-------------|-----------------|-------------------------|
| **COPD exacerbation** | History of COPD, increased sputum, prolonged expiration, wheeze, barrel chest, accessory muscle use, smoking history | Spirometry (if stable), CXR (hyperinflation), ABG | ~20-25% |
| **Asthma exacerbation** | Wheeze, reversible airflow obstruction, allergic triggers, personal/family history of atopy, nocturnal/early morning symptoms | Spirometry with reversibility, FeNO | ~10-15% |
| **Pneumonia** | Fever, cough, purulent sputum, focal crackles, egophony, consolidation on CXR, leukocytosis | CXR, sputum culture, procalcitonin | ~10-15% |
| **Pulmonary embolism** | Pleuritic pain, tachycardia, risk factors for DVT, sudden onset dyspnea, syncope, hemoptysis | Wells score, D-dimer, CTPA | ~5-10% |
| **Pneumothorax** | Sudden unilateral pleuritic pain, decreased breath sounds, hyperresonance, tracheal deviation (tension) | CXR (upright expiratory), CT if uncertain | ~2-5% |
| **ARDS** | Severe hypoxemia refractory to supplemental oxygen, bilateral infiltrates on CXR, non-cardiogenic (normal BNP), risk factor (sepsis, trauma, pneumonia, aspiration) | CXR, ABG (PaO2/FiO2 <300), clinical criteria | ~1-2% |
| **Interstitial lung disease** | Chronic progressive dyspnea, dry cough, inspiratory crackles (Velcro rales), clubbing, restrictive pattern on PFTs | HRCT chest, PFTs (restrictive, reduced DLCO) | ~1-2% |
| **Pleural effusion** | Dullness to percussion, decreased breath sounds, unilateral, may have associated condition (CHF, pneumonia, malignancy, cirrhosis) | CXR, ultrasound, thoracentesis | ~3-5% |
| **Upper airway obstruction** | Stridor (inspiratory wheeze), voice changes, difficulty swallowing, may be acute (angioedema, foreign body, epiglottitis) or chronic (tumor) | Laryngoscopy, CT neck/chest, soft tissue neck X-ray | <1% |
| **Lung cancer** | Smoker, chronic cough, hemoptysis, weight loss, unilateral wheeze, pleural effusion, post-obstructive pneumonia | CT chest, bronchoscopy, tissue biopsy | ~1-2% |

### 12.4.3 Other Causes

| Cause | Key Features | Diagnostic Test | Notes |
|-------|-------------|-----------------|-------|
| **Anemia** | Fatigue, pallor, tachycardia, exertional dyspnea out of proportion to activity; Hb <7 g/dL often symptomatic | CBC, reticulocyte count, iron studies, B12/folate | Common and often overlooked; transfuse if Hb <7-8 with symptoms or cardiac disease |
| **Metabolic acidosis** | Kussmaul respirations (deep, rapid breathing), history of DKA, renal failure, sepsis, salicylate toxicity, lactic acidosis | ABG (low pH, low HCO3, compensatory low PaCO2), BMP, lactate, ketones, serum osmolality | Respiratory compensation for metabolic acidosis causes dyspnea |
| **Anxiety/panic attack** | Paresthesias (perioral and digital), chest tightness, hyperventilation, sense of doom, normal vitals between episodes, reproducible with voluntary hyperventilation | Diagnosis of exclusion after ruling out cardiac/pulmonary causes; GAD-7 screening | Most common non-medical cause; NEVER diagnose anxiety without excluding organic causes first |
| **Deconditioning** | Gradual onset, improves with exercise training, no symptoms at rest, recent prolonged immobilization or illness | Exclude other causes; exercise testing; 6-minute walk test | Diagnosis of exclusion |
| **Obesity hypoventilation syndrome** | BMI >30, daytime somnolence, morning headaches, hypercapnia, polycythemia, pulmonary hypertension | ABG (elevated PaCO2), sleep study (polysomnography), PFTs (restrictive pattern) | Often overlaps with OSA |
| **Neuromuscular disease** | Weakness, difficulty swallowing, weak cough, paradoxical breathing (abdomen moves inward on inspiration), orthopnea | PFTs (restrictive pattern with reduced maximal inspiratory/expiratory pressures), EMG | ALS, myasthenia gravis, Guillain-Barre, muscular dystrophies, diaphragmatic paralysis |
| **Carbon monoxide poisoning** | Headache, confusion, dizziness, nausea, cherry-red skin (late finding), exposure to combustion (faulty heater, garage, fire), multiple people in same environment affected | Carboxyhemoglobin level (co-oximetry on ABG; normal <2%, toxic >15-20%) | Pulse oximetry falsely normal (does not distinguish oxyhemoglobin from carboxyhemoglobin) |
| **Methemoglobinemia** | Cyanosis despite normal PaO2, chocolate-colored blood, drug exposure (benzocaine, dapsone, nitrates, nitrites) | Methemoglobin level (co-oximetry; >1-2% abnormal, >20-30% symptomatic, >70% fatal) | Treat with methylene blue if symptomatic |
| **High altitude illness** | Recent ascent >2500 m, headache, fatigue, insomnia, dyspnea out of proportion to exertion; HAPE presents with cough, pink frothy sputum, severe hypoxemia | Clinical context, SpO2 (very low for altitude), CXR (patchy infiltrates in HAPE) | Descent is definitive treatment; acetazolamide for prevention; dexamethasone for HACE |
| **Pregnancy** | Physiologic dyspnea of pregnancy (progesterone-driven hyperventilation, increased tidal volume); dyspnea out of proportion to gestational age is concerning | Clinical context; exclude PE, anemia, cardiomyopathy, preeclampsia | Normal in pregnancy but should not be severe or progressive |
| **Drugs/toxins** | Beta-blockers (bronchoconstriction), amiodarone (pulmonary toxicity), methotrexate (ILD), bleomycin (ILD), nitrofurantoin (ILD), NSAIDs (aspirin-exacerbated respiratory disease), opioids (respiratory depression) | Drug history, medication reconciliation | Consider drug-induced causes in all patients |

## 12.5 Management Principles by Cause

### 12.5.1 Cardiac Dyspnea

| Condition | Immediate Management | Follow-Up |
|-----------|---------------------|-----------|
| **ADHF** | IV loop diuretics (furosemide), vasodilators if SBP >110 (nitroglycerin), oxygen, NIV if needed, continue GDMT, strict I/O monitoring | Cardiology; daily weights; sodium restriction; GDMT optimization |
| **ACS** | Aspirin, anticoagulation, beta-blocker, nitroglycerin, revascularization per STEMI/NSTEMI protocol | Cardiology; cardiac rehab; secondary prevention |
| **Arrhythmia (AF with RVR)** | Rate control (diltiazem, beta-blocker, amiodarone), anticoagulation, evaluate for cardioversion | Cardiology/electrophysiology; CHA2DS2-VASc assessment |
| **Tamponade** | Urgent pericardiocentesis (echo-guided preferred); IV fluids for preload; avoid diuretics | Cardiology; monitor for reaccumulation; consider surgical window |
| **Hypertensive emergency with flash pulmonary edema** | IV nitroglycerin + IV loop diuretics; gradual BP reduction | HTN specialist; medication optimization |

### 12.5.2 Pulmonary Dyspnea

| Condition | Immediate Management | Follow-Up |
|-----------|---------------------|-----------|
| **COPD/asthma exacerbation** | Bronchodilators (SABA +/- ipratropium), corticosteroids, oxygen (target 88-92% in COPD, 93-95% in asthma), NIV if hypercapnic | PCP/pulmonology within 1 week; pulmonary rehab; vaccination review |
| **Pneumonia** | Appropriate antibiotics, oxygen, supportive care; CURB-65/PSI for disposition | PCP within 48-72 hours; repeat CXR in 4-6 weeks if smoker/elderly |
| **PE** | Anticoagulation (DOAC preferred); thrombolysis if massive; hemodynamic support | Hematology or PCP; consider CTEPH screening at 3-6 months |
| **Pneumothorax (tension)** | Immediate needle decompression (14g angiocatheter, 2nd ICS midclavicular line) then chest tube | Thoracic surgery if recurrent; evaluate for underlying lung disease |
| **Pneumothorax (simple)** | Observation if small (<2 cm) and stable; chest tube if large or symptomatic | Follow-up CXR; high-flow oxygen may accelerate reabsorption |

## 12.6 Red Flags

> **IMMEDIATE INTERVENTION REQUIRED - CALL EMS OR ACTIVATE EMERGENCY PROTOCOLS:**
> - Severe hypoxemia (SpO2 <85% on room air or not improving with supplemental O2)
> - Altered mental status (confusion, agitation, drowsiness, coma)
> - Hemodynamic instability (SBP <90 mmHg, requiring vasopressors, arrhythmia with hemodynamic compromise)
> - Respiratory acidosis with pH <7.25 (indicates impending respiratory failure)
> - Severe respiratory distress (tripoding, unable to speak, paradoxical breathing, respiratory exhaustion, use of accessory muscles)
> - Suspected tension pneumothorax (immediate needle decompression before imaging - do NOT wait for X-ray confirmation)
> - Cardiac tamponade (Beck's triad: hypotension, JVD, muffled heart sounds)
> - Massive PE with hemodynamic compromise (syncope, SBP <90, cardiac arrest)
> - Anaphylaxis with airway compromise (stridor, angioedema, hypotension)
> - Foreign body airway obstruction (complete obstruction)

## 12.7 When to Seek Emergency Care (Patient-Facing)

Seek emergency medical attention immediately if you experience:
- Sudden severe shortness of breath at rest
- Shortness of breath that is getting rapidly worse
- Shortness of breath accompanied by chest pain or pressure
- Blue discoloration of lips, fingertips, or face
- Confusion, fainting, or severe dizziness with shortness of breath
- Coughing up blood
- Swelling in one leg accompanied by sudden shortness of breath
- High-pitched noisy breathing (stridor) or inability to speak
- Shortness of breath after a known trigger (allergen, insect sting) with facial/throat swelling
- Inability to lie flat due to breathing difficulty
- Oxygen saturation below 90% if you have a pulse oximeter at home

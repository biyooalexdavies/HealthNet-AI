# Chapter 1: Vaccination Guidelines

## 1.1 Overview

Vaccination remains one of the most effective preventive interventions in modern medicine. The Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP) publishes annual immunization schedules incorporating new evidence throughout the year. This chapter provides comprehensive guidance on adult vaccination based on the 2024-2025 ACIP schedule, including key updates and special populations. [^274^] [^276^]

**Key 2025 Schedule Changes:**
- Transition from quadrivalent to **trivalent influenza vaccines** (IIV3, aIIV3, HD-IIV3, RIV3) [^456^]
- Updated **COVID-19 vaccine** recommendations with 2024-2025 formulation
- Addition of **21-valent pneumococcal conjugate vaccine (PCV21; CAPVAXIVE)** as an option [^467^]
- Addition of **inactivated polio vaccine (IPV)** to adult tables [^456^]
- Updated **respiratory syncytial virus (RSV)** recommendations [^452^]
- New **mRNA RSV vaccine (mResvia)** added [^456^]

**How to Use the Schedule:**
1. Determine recommended vaccines by age (Table 1)
2. Assess need for additional vaccines by medical condition/indication (Table 2)
3. Review vaccine types, dosing, intervals, and special situations
4. Review contraindications and precautions
5. Review new/updated ACIP guidance (Addendum) [^276^]

## 1.2 Influenza Vaccine

### Overview
Annual influenza vaccination is recommended for all adults 19 years and older. Influenza causes significant morbidity and mortality, particularly in older adults, immunocompromised persons, and those with chronic medical conditions.

### Recommendations

| Population | Recommendation | Evidence Level |
|------------|---------------|----------------|
| All adults 19+ | Annual vaccination | ACIP universal recommendation [^276^] |
| Adults 65+ | HD-IIV3, aIIV3, or RIV3 **preferred** over standard-dose | ACIP preferential recommendation [^276^] |
| Egg allergy | Any age-appropriate influenza vaccine (egg-based or non-egg-based) acceptable | ACIP [^274^] |
| Pregnant women | Inactivated influenza vaccine (not LAIV) | ACIP; safe in any trimester |
| Solid organ transplant (19-64) | HD-IIV3 and aIIV3 acceptable options | ACIP [^274^] |

### Key Clinical Points
- **Trivalent formulation** replaces quadrivalent for the 2024-25 season (B/Yamagata lineage removed due to absence of circulating virus) [^456^]
- Vaccination ideally completed by end of October, but continue vaccinating as long as influenza is circulating
- It takes approximately 2 weeks after vaccination for antibodies to develop
- **LAIV3 contraindication:** Close contacts of severely immunosuppressed persons requiring protected environment should NOT receive live attenuated influenza vaccine [^274^]
- Common adverse effects: soreness at injection site, low-grade fever, mild myalgias (1-2 days)

## 1.3 COVID-19 Vaccine

### Overview
COVID-19 vaccination remains an essential preventive measure. Updated formulations are released annually to address circulating variants. [^274^] [^276^]

### 2024-2025 Recommendations by Population

| Population | Vaccination Schedule |
|------------|---------------------|
| Adults 19-64 (unvaccinated) | 1 dose updated 2024-25 Moderna or Pfizer-BioNTech; OR 2 doses Novavax at 0, 3-8 weeks |
| Adults 19-64 (previously vaccinated) | 1 dose updated 2024-25 vaccine at least 8 weeks after most recent dose |
| Adults 65+ | 2 doses updated 2024-25 vaccine, minimum 2 months apart [^276^] |
| Immunocompromised adults | May receive additional doses beyond routine; 3-dose initial mRNA series, followed by boosters |
| Pregnancy | Recommended during pregnancy; safe at any gestational age [^542^] |

### Key Clinical Points
- Use vaccine from same manufacturer for all doses in initial series when possible
- Common adverse effects: injection site pain, fatigue, headache, myalgia, fever (typically resolve within 1-3 days)
- Rare: myocarditis/pericarditis (higher risk in young males after mRNA vaccines; typically mild and self-limited)
- Contraindications: Severe allergic reaction (e.g., anaphylaxis) to previous dose or component

## 1.4 Tdap/Td (Tetanus, Diphtheria, Pertussis)

### Overview
Tetanus, diphtheria, and pertussis are vaccine-preventable diseases with significant morbidity and mortality. Pertussis (whooping cough) has resurged in adults due to waning immunity, making Tdap vaccination particularly important for preventing infant transmission. [^534^] [^535^]

### Recommendations

| Scenario | Recommendation |
|----------|---------------|
| Primary series (unvaccinated adults) | 3 doses Td-containing vaccine: Tdap, then Td/Tdap at 4 weeks, then Td/Tdap at 6-12 months [^534^] |
| Booster (all adults) | Td or Tdap every 10 years throughout life |
| Adults never received Tdap | Single dose of Tdap regardless of interval since last Td [^274^] |
| Pregnancy | Tdap during **each pregnancy**, 27-36 weeks gestation; if not given during pregnancy, give immediately postpartum [^538^] [^543^] |
| Wound management | Tdap if >5 years since last booster, regardless of gestational age [^546^] |

### Pregnancy Considerations
- Tdap at 27-36 weeks gestation reduces infant pertussis risk by **78%** in babies <2 months old [^543^]
- Earlier vaccination within the 27-36 week window maximizes passive antibody transfer to the newborn
- If tetanus booster is indicated during pregnancy (>10 years since prior Td), give Tdap at 27-36 weeks rather than Td

## 1.5 HPV Vaccine (Gardasil 9)

### Overview
Human papillomavirus (HPV) is the most common sexually transmitted infection and causes cervical, anal, oropharyngeal, vulvar, vaginal, and penile cancers. HPV vaccination prevents new infections but does NOT treat existing infections. [^464^]

### Recommendations

| Population | Recommendation | Schedule |
|------------|---------------|----------|
| Routine | Age 11-12 years (may start at age 9) | 2-dose series if starting before 15th birthday (0, 6-12 months) |
| Catch-up | Ages 13-26 years | 3-dose series (0, 1-2 months, 6 months) |
| Adults 27-45 | **Shared clinical decision-making** | 3-dose series if electing vaccination |
| Immunocompromised (including HIV) | Recommended through age 26; 3-dose series even if initiated at age 9-14 [^274^] |

### Key Clinical Points
- Vaccination not recommended during pregnancy; no intervention needed if inadvertently administered
- No pregnancy test needed before vaccination
- HPV vaccination for all eligible persons regardless of sexual orientation or gender identity
- Target population for adults 27-45: those most likely to benefit (new sexual partners, not previously infected with vaccine types)

## 1.6 Pneumococcal Vaccines

### Overview
Pneumococcal disease remains a significant cause of morbidity and mortality, particularly in older adults and those with certain medical conditions. The 2024-2025 schedule includes four pneumococcal vaccines: PCV15, PCV20, PCV21, and PPSV23. [^461^] [^462^] [^467^]

### Available Vaccines

| Vaccine | Valency | Indications |
|---------|---------|-------------|
| PCV15 (VAXNEUVANCE) | 15-valent conjugate | Adults 19+ with risk conditions |
| PCV20 (Prevnar 20) | 20-valent conjugate | Adults 50+; one-and-done option |
| PCV21 (CAPVAXIVE) | 21-valent conjugate | Adults 50+; one-and-done option [^467^] |
| PPSV23 (Pneumovax 23) | 23-valent polysaccharide | Follows PCV15 when sequential approach used |

### Adult Recommendations

| Population | Recommendation |
|------------|---------------|
| Adults 50+ (never received PCV) | Single dose of PCV15, PCV20, or PCV21. If PCV15 used: follow with PPSV23 at >=1 year (>=8 weeks if immunocompromised, CSF leak, or cochlear implant). If PCV20 or PCV21: no additional vaccine needed [^467^] |
| Adults 19-49 with risk conditions | Single dose of PCV15, PCV20, or PCV21; risk conditions include: chronic heart/lung/liver disease, diabetes, cigarette smoking, alcoholism, immunocompromising conditions, CSF leak, cochlear implant |
| Adults 65+ with prior PCV13 and PPSV23 | Shared clinical decision-making regarding PCV20 or PCV21 (single dose, at least 5 years after last pneumococcal vaccine) [^461^] |

### Clinical Tool
The CDC offers the **PneumoRecs VaxAdvisor** mobile app for patient-specific pneumococcal vaccine guidance. [^462^]

## 1.7 Shingles (Herpes Zoster) Vaccine

### Overview
Herpes zoster (shingles) affects approximately 1 in 3 adults in their lifetime, with risk increasing dramatically with age. Postherpetic neuralgia (PHN) is the most common complication and can cause debilitating chronic pain.

### Recommendations

| Population | Vaccine | Schedule | Efficacy |
|------------|---------|----------|----------|
| All adults 50+ | RZV (Shingrix) | 2 doses, 2-6 months apart | 97% (ages 50-69); 91% (ages 70+) [^465^] |
| Adults 19+ with immunocompromising conditions | RZV (Shingrix) | 2 doses, 1-2 months apart (may extend to 2-6 months if needed) | High efficacy across immunocompromised groups |

### Key Clinical Points
- **No maximum age** for vaccination [^465^]
- Efficacy against PHN: 91% (ages 50+), 89% (ages 70+)
- Should vaccinate even if previously had shingles, received Zostavax, or received varicella vaccine
- No specific waiting period after shingles episode; vaccinate after rash resolves
- Contraindicated during current episode of herpes zoster and during pregnancy
- Common adverse effects: injection site pain (78%), fatigue (46%), myalgia (45%), headache (38%), fever (18%) -- typically resolve within 2-3 days [^465^]
- Very rare risk of Guillain-Barre syndrome reported

## 1.8 Hepatitis A and B Vaccines

### Hepatitis A
- **2-dose series** for adults at risk [^518^]
- Risk groups: travelers to endemic areas, men who have sex with men (MSM), persons who use drugs, persons with chronic liver disease
- Combined HepA-HepB vaccine (Twinrix) available: 3-dose series (0, 1, 6 months)

### Hepatitis B (Updated Universal Recommendation)

**2022-2023 ACIP Update:** All adults aged 19-59 years should receive routine HepB vaccination. This replaces the previous risk-based approach to improve coverage. [^519^] [^523^]

| Population | Recommendation |
|------------|---------------|
| Adults 19-59 years | **Universal vaccination** (catch-up if not previously vaccinated) [^523^] |
| Adults 60+ with risk factors | Recommended |
| Adults 60+ without risk factors | May receive vaccine if desired |

### Hepatitis B Vaccine Options

| Vaccine | Schedule | Notes |
|---------|----------|-------|
| Heplisav-B | 2 doses, 1 month apart | Adults 18+; enhanced immunogenicity |
| Engerix-B | 3 doses (0, 1, 6 months) | Standard option |
| Recombivax HB | 3 doses (0, 1, 6 months) | Standard option |
| Twinrix (HepA+HepB) | 3 doses (0, 1, 6 months) | For those needing both |

### Risk Factors for Adults 60+
- Sexual exposure risks (MSM, multiple partners, STI evaluation)
- Percutaneous/mucosal blood exposure (injection drug use, healthcare workers, dialysis)
- HIV, hepatitis C, chronic liver disease
- Travel to endemic countries, diabetes
- Incarcerated persons [^523^]

### Universal Hepatitis B Screening (2023)
All adults 18+ should be screened at least once with a **triple panel**: HBsAg, anti-HBs, total anti-HBc. Pregnant people should be tested during each pregnancy. [^517^]

## 1.9 MMR and Varicella Vaccines

### MMR (Measles, Mumps, Rubella)

**Evidence of Immunity (at least one of the following):** [^274^] [^541^]
- Documentation of adequate vaccination (2 doses measles/mumps, 1 dose rubella; doses separated by >=28 days)
- Laboratory evidence of immunity
- Laboratory confirmation of disease
- Birth before 1957 (except healthcare personnel)

| Population | Recommendation |
|------------|---------------|
| Adults without evidence of immunity | 1 dose MMR |
| Healthcare personnel, students, travelers without evidence | 2 doses MMR [^274^] |
| Healthcare personnel born 1957+ | Documentation of 2 doses MMR or lab evidence of immunity [^536^] |
| Pregnancy | MMR **contraindicated**; give 1 dose postpartum before discharge if no rubella immunity [^274^] |

### Varicella (Chickenpox)

**Evidence of Immunity:** [^533^] [^540^]
- Documentation of 2 doses of varicella vaccine
- Laboratory evidence of immunity
- Birth in US before 1980 (not for HCP, pregnant women, immunocompromised)
- Diagnosis/verification of varicella or shingles by healthcare provider

| Population | Recommendation |
|------------|---------------|
| Healthcare personnel without evidence of immunity | 2 doses, 4-8 weeks apart [^536^] |
| Adults without evidence of immunity | 2 doses, 4-8 weeks apart |

### Key Clinical Points
- 70-90% of adults who do not remember having varicella actually have antibodies [^540^]
- Serologic testing before vaccination may be cost-effective in adults with uncertain history
- MMR and varicella can be given simultaneously; if not given on same day, separate by at least 28 days

## 1.10 RSV (Respiratory Syncytial Virus) Vaccine

### Overview
RSV causes significant respiratory disease in older adults, with an estimated 60,000-160,000 hospitalizations and 6,000-10,000 deaths annually among adults 65+ in the United States. [^452^] [^515^]

### 2024 Updated Recommendations

| Population | Recommendation |
|------------|---------------|
| Adults 75+ years | RSV vaccination **recommended** (routine) [^452^] |
| Adults 60-74 years at **increased risk** | RSV vaccination recommended for those with: chronic cardiopulmonary disease, diabetes, moderate/severe immune compromise, chronic kidney/liver/hematologic disorders, neurologic/neuromuscular conditions, nursing home residents, frailty [^530^] |
| Pregnancy | RSV vaccination (Abrysvo) at **32-36 weeks gestation** during RSV season [^542^] |

### Available Vaccines
- **Abrysvo** (Pfizer) -- single dose
- **Arexvy** (GSK) -- single dose
- **mResvia** (Moderna) -- single dose [^452^]

### Key Clinical Points
- **Single dose only**; no repeat dosing currently recommended
- Coadministration with other vaccines (influenza, COVID-19) acceptable [^515^]
- Common adverse effects: injection site pain, fatigue, headache, myalgia
- Rare safety signal: potential association with Guillain-Barre syndrome (under investigation)

## 1.13 Immunocompromised Host Vaccination

### General Principles [^545^]

| Category | Guidance |
|----------|----------|
| **Inactivated vaccines** | Generally safe; may have reduced immunogenicity |
| **Live vaccines** | Contraindicated in severely immunocompromised persons |
| **Household contacts** | Should receive all age-appropriate vaccines; prefer inactivated influenza over LAIV |
| **Higher/Additional doses** | May be needed for adequate response |
| **Antibody titers** | May be checked when indicated |

### Timing After Immunosuppressive Therapy

| Therapy | Inactivated Vaccines | Live Vaccines |
|---------|---------------------|---------------|
| Chemotherapy | 3-6 months after completion | After evidence of immune reconstitution |
| Anti-CD20 (e.g., rituximab) | Wait 6-9 months after last dose | After evidence of immune reconstitution |
| High-dose corticosteroids (>2 weeks) | May give during therapy; optimal response 1 month after discontinuation | Defer until off therapy |

### Key Vaccines for Immunocompromised Adults
- **Annual influenza vaccination** (essential)
- **COVID-19:** Additional doses recommended [^274^]
- **Pneumococcal:** May need both PCV and PPSV23 with shorter intervals [^467^]
- **Hepatitis B:** 2-dose (Heplisav-B) or 3-dose series; check anti-HBs titers
- **HPV:** 3-dose series through age 26 [^274^]
- **Shingles:** 2-dose RZV (Shingrix), 1-2 months apart [^465^]

## 1.14 Vaccine Hesitancy Counseling: The CASE Method

### Overview
Vaccine hesitancy is recognized by WHO as one of the top 10 threats to global health. Effective counseling requires a patient-centered approach that builds trust, addresses specific concerns, and respects autonomy. [^510^]

### The CASE Method

| Step | Description | Example Language |
|------|-------------|-----------------|
| **C - Corroboration** | Acknowledge concerns and validate emotions | "It makes sense that you want to make the best decision for your health." |
| **A - About Me** | Share personal experience or institutional trust | "In my practice, I've seen how effective this vaccine is..." |
| **S - Science** | Provide evidence-based information tailored to concerns | Offer specific data addressing the patient's stated worry |
| **E - Explain/Advise** | Give clear recommendation while respecting choice | "Based on your age and medical history, I recommend this vaccine because..." |

### Motivational Interviewing Approach: 4-Step Model [^510^]

**Step 1: Engage -- Establish Trust**
- Use MI spirit: Compassion, Acceptance, Partnership
- Skills: Open-ended questions, reflective listening, affirmation
- **Don't:** Try to correct misinformation with arguments or lectures
- **Do:** Listen to learn; let people express fears without interruption

**Step 2: Understand Views**
- Clarify specific concerns with open questions ("What specifically worries you about this vaccine?")
- Affirm desire to act responsibly ("I can see you really care about making a healthy choice.")
- Use complex reflections and summaries

**Step 3: Provide Information (Ask-Offer-Ask)**
- Ask permission: "Would it be okay if I shared some information about how this vaccine works?"
- Offer targeted, accurate information
- Ask for understanding: "Does that make sense? Do you have questions?"

**Step 4: Respect Autonomy**
- Don't force or pressure for immediate decision
- Strengthen trust relationship
- Move toward planning when patient is ready
- Offer to continue the conversation at next visit

### Evidence Base
Motivational interviewing approaches reduce vaccine hesitancy and enhance intention to vaccinate. [^510^] A confrontational approach typically reinforces resistance.

### Red Flags: Contraindications vs. Precautions

| True Contraindications (Do NOT Vaccinate) | Precautions (May Vaccinate with Caution) |
|------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------|
| Severe allergic reaction to prior dose or vaccine component | Moderate or severe acute illness (defer until recovery) |
| Encephalopathy within 7 days of prior DTP/Tdap/DTP | Guillain-Barre syndrome within 6 weeks of prior tetanus-containing vaccine |
| Severe combined immunodeficiency (for live vaccines) | Pregnancy (for live vaccines) |
| Current pregnancy (MMR, varicella) | Thrombocytopenia (MMR) |

# Chapter 2: Cancer Screening

## 2.1 Overview

Cancer screening aims to detect malignancies at early, treatable stages before symptoms develop. This chapter covers evidence-based screening recommendations for the five most common screening-targeted cancers, with attention to guideline differences between major organizations and disparities in screening outcomes.

---

## 2.2 Breast Cancer Screening

### Overview
Breast cancer is the second most common cancer among women in the United States. Screening mammography reduces breast cancer mortality, but the optimal starting age, interval, and modality remain subjects of guideline evolution. The USPSTF 2024 update lowered the recommended starting age from 50 to 40, reflecting rising incidence in younger women and persistent mortality disparities. [^455^] [^458^]

### USPSTF 2024 Recommendation [^455^] [^453^]

| Parameter | Recommendation |
|-----------|---------------|
| **Population** | All persons assigned female at birth at average risk |
| **Age 40-74** | Biennial screening mammography (**Grade B**) |
| **Age 75+** | Insufficient evidence to assess balance of benefits/harms (**Grade I**) |
| **Dense breasts** | Insufficient evidence for supplemental screening with ultrasound or MRI (**Grade I**) |
| **Modality** | Digital mammography or digital breast tomosynthesis (DBT/3D mammography) |

### Comparison of Major Guideline Recommendations

| Organization | Age to Start | Frequency | Age to Stop | Notes |
|-------------|-------------|-----------|-------------|-------|
| **USPSTF 2024** [^455^] | 40 | Every 2 years | 74 (individualize 75+) | Applies to all persons assigned female at birth |
| **ACS 2023** [^416^] | 45 (option at 40-44) | Annual (45-54); every 1-2 years (55+) | When life expectancy <10 years | Continue in good health |
| **ACOG** | 40 | Every 1-2 years | 75 (shared decision-making) | Offer annual to women 40-49 |
| **NCCN** | 40 | Annual | Upper age based on health status | Strong preference for annual |
| **AAFP** | 50 (individualize 40-49) | Every 2 years | 74 | Evidence update pending |
| **AMA/American College of Radiology** | 40 | Annual | When life expectancy <5-7 years | Strongest screening stance |

### Rationale for Lowering Screening Age to 40 [^458^]
- Rising breast cancer incidence in women under 50
- Black women have **40% higher death rate** than White women despite similar screening rates
- Disparities are widest for women under 50
- Starting at age 40 has potential to reduce mortality disparities

### Digital Breast Tomosynthesis (DBT/3D Mammography)
- DBT has elevated cancer detection compared to conventional digital mammography [^454^]
- Reduces false-positive callbacks
- Both digital mammography and DBT are effective screening modalities [^455^]

### Dense Breast Notification
- FDA requires reporting of breast density to patients (2024)
- Dense breasts: higher cancer risk AND reduced mammography sensitivity
- **Supplemental screening** (MRI, ultrasound) for dense breasts: insufficient evidence per USPSTF [^455^]
- Shared decision-making about supplemental imaging when breasts are dense

### High-Risk Populations
- BRCA1/2 mutation carriers: Annual MRI starting at age 25-30, plus mammography at age 30
- Prior chest radiation (age 10-30): Annual MRI starting 8 years after radiation
- Strong family history: May warrant earlier screening; consider genetic counseling
- Black women: May benefit from annual screening starting at 40 due to higher mortality

### Key Clinical Points
- Ensure equitable follow-up for abnormal mammograms -- disparities exist in timeliness of diagnostic follow-up
- Biennial screening reduces false positives and overdiagnosis compared to annual screening, but may miss more interval cancers in younger women
- Any positive screen requires diagnostic evaluation (diagnostic mammogram, possible ultrasound/biopsy)

## 2.3 Cervical Cancer Screening

### Overview
Cervical cancer screening has dramatically reduced cervical cancer incidence and mortality. The transition to HPV-based testing has improved sensitivity, and primary HPV testing is now a preferred screening modality. [^466^]

### USPSTF 2018 Recommendations [^466^]

| Age Group | Recommended Screening | Interval | Grade |
|-----------|----------------------|----------|-------|
| **<21 years** | **Do NOT screen** | -- | D (harms > benefits) |
| **21-29 years** | Cervical cytology (Pap smear) only | Every 3 years | A |
| **30-65 years** | Option 1: Cytology alone | Every 3 years | A |
| | Option 2: **Primary hrHPV testing** | Every 5 years | A |
| | Option 3: Co-testing (cytology + hrHPV) | Every 5 years | A |
| **>65 years** | **Do NOT screen** if adequate prior screening | -- | D |

### Definition of "Adequate Prior Screening" for Discontinuation at 65
- 3 consecutive negative cytology results, OR
- 2 consecutive negative co-tests or primary HPV tests within the prior 10 years (most recent within 5 years)

### Post-Hysterectomy
- **Do NOT screen** after hysterectomy with cervix removal if no history of CIN2+ or cervical cancer (**Grade D**)
- Continue screening if hysterectomy was performed for CIN2+ or cervical cancer

### Risk Factors
- HPV infection (primary cause)
- Smoking
- Multiple sexual partners, early sexual debut
- Immunosuppression (HIV, transplant)
- Low socioeconomic status
- Limited access to screening

### Key Clinical Points
- Primary HPV testing every 5 years is now widely considered the preferred option for ages 30-65 due to superior sensitivity
- HPV vaccination does NOT eliminate the need for cervical cancer screening
- Abnormal results require appropriate follow-up per ASCCP guidelines
- Transgender men who retain a cervix should continue cervical cancer screening

## 2.4 Colorectal Cancer Screening

### Overview
Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third leading cause of cancer death in the US. The USPSTF 2021 update lowered the recommended starting age from 50 to 45, reflecting a 51% increase in CRC incidence among adults under 55 since the mid-1990s. [^494^]

### USPSTF 2021 Recommendations [^494^]

| Parameter | Recommendation |
|-----------|---------------|
| **Age 45-75** | Screen all adults (**Grade A**) |
| **Age 76-85** | Individual decision based on prior screening, health status, life expectancy (**Grade C**) |
| **>85** | Do not screen |
| **Goal** | Any recommended test is better than no screening |

### Screening Modalities

| Test | Frequency | Sensitivity | Notes |
|------|-----------|-------------|-------|
| **FIT (Fecal Immunochemical Test)** | Annual | Moderate | Stool-based; detects hemoglobin; noninvasive; good adherence |
| **mt-sDNA (Cologuard)** | Every 1-3 years | Higher than FIT | Stool-based; detects DNA alterations and hemoglobin; higher false-positive rate |
| **Colonoscopy** | Every 10 years | Highest (gold standard) | Visual examination of entire colon; requires bowel prep and sedation |
| **CT Colonography** | Every 5 years | Good | Requires bowel prep; radiation exposure; follow colonoscopy if positive |
| **Flexible Sigmoidoscopy** | Every 5 years | Moderate | Limited to distal colon; declining use |
| **FIT + Sigmoidoscopy** | FIT annually + sigmoidoscopy every 10 years | Moderate | Combined approach; rarely used |

### Implementation Notes [^494^]
- Colonoscopy is the most commonly used modality (52.7% in the post-2021 recommendation era)
- FIT and mt-sDNA are noninvasive options that improve adherence, especially in underserved populations
- Any positive stool-based test requires follow-up colonoscopy
- **"Any recommended test is better than no screening"** -- patient preference and access should guide modality selection

### High-Risk Populations
- First-degree relative with CRC or advanced adenoma: Start at age 40, or 10 years before youngest case in family
- Hereditary CRC syndromes (Lynch syndrome, FAP): Specialized screening protocols
- Inflammatory bowel disease: Colonoscopy with surveillance biopsies
- Prior abdominal/pelvic radiation: Earlier screening

### Key Clinical Points
- Average-risk adults with negative colonoscopy do not need repeat screening for 10 years
- The shift to age 45 addresses rising early-onset CRC and reduces racial disparities (Black Americans have higher CRC incidence and mortality)
- Access to follow-up colonoscopy after positive stool-based tests remains an equity issue

## 2.5 Lung Cancer Screening

### Overview
Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer death in the United States. Low-dose CT (LDCT) screening reduces mortality by approximately 20% in high-risk populations. The USPSTF 2021 update expanded eligibility criteria, nearly doubling the eligible population and including more Black patients and women. [^468^] [^473^]

### USPSTF 2021 Recommendations [^468^]

| Parameter | Criteria |
|-----------|----------|
| **Age** | 50-80 years |
| **Smoking history** | 20 pack-years or more |
| **Current status** | Current smoker OR quit within past 15 years |
| **Modality** | Annual low-dose CT (LDCT) |
| **Grade** | B |
| **Discontinue when** | 15 years since quitting; or health problem limiting life expectancy/ability for curative surgery |

### 2023 ACS Update [^474^]
The American Cancer Society removed the "years since quitting" requirement:
- Recommend annual screening for adults 50-80 who have ever smoked with 20+ pack-year history
- **Note:** USPSTF and Medicare have not yet adopted this change

### Pack-Year Calculation
```
Pack-years = (number of cigarettes per day / 20) x number of years smoked
```

### Shared Decision-Making Requirements
- Medicare requires a shared decision-making visit before the first LDCT
- Counseling should cover: benefits (early detection), harms (false positives, radiation, overtesting/overdiagnosis), importance of annual screening, and smoking cessation support

### Lung-RADS Classification for Nodule Management

| Category | Management |
|----------|-----------|
| 0 (Incomplete) | Additional imaging needed |
| 1 (Negative) | Continue annual screening |
| 2 (Benign appearance) | Continue annual screening |
| 3 (Probably benign) | Short-interval LDCT at 6 months |
| 4A (Suspicious) | Short-interval LDCT at 3 months; consider PET-CT |
| 4B (Very suspicious) | Multidisciplinary review; tissue sampling |
| 4X (Suspicious with additional features) | Same as 4A/4B |

### Key Clinical Points [^468^]
- Expanded criteria (from age 55/30 pack-years) nearly doubles the eligible population
- Includes more Black patients and women who tend to smoke fewer cigarettes per day
- Estimated **30,000-60,000 lives saved annually** with full implementation
- Most lung nodules detected on LDCT are NOT cancer
- Smoking cessation support should be offered to ALL current smokers
- Annual adherence is critical for mortality benefit

## 2.6 Prostate Cancer Screening

### Overview
Prostate cancer is the most common non-skin cancer in American men. PSA-based screening can detect prostate cancer early but carries risks of overdiagnosis and overtreatment. Shared decision-making is essential. [^482^] [^469^]

### USPSTF 2018 Recommendations [^482^]

| Age Group | Recommendation | Grade |
|-----------|---------------|-------|
| **55-69 years** | **Shared decision-making** about PSA-based screening | C |
| **70+ years** | **Do not routinely screen** | D |
| **<55 years (average risk)** | No routine screening | -- |

### Benefits of PSA Screening
- May slightly lower chance of death from prostate cancer in some men
- Can detect high-grade, potentially lethal cancers at curable stages

### Harms of PSA Screening
- False-positive results leading to additional testing (biopsy)
- Overdiagnosis of indolent cancers that would never cause symptoms
- Treatment complications: urinary incontinence, erectile dysfunction, bowel dysfunction
- Anxiety associated with screening and false positives

### Shared Decision-Making Model [^469^]
1. **Choice talk:** Introduce the choice; acknowledge that a decision needs to be made
2. **Option talk:** Describe PSA screening, potential benefits, and harms; consider decision aid
3. **Decision talk:** Help patient explore preferences and make an informed decision

### High-Risk Populations [^479^]
- **Black men:** 1.8x more likely to be diagnosed; 2.2x more likely to die from prostate cancer
- Consider earlier screening (age 45) for Black men and those with first-degree family history
- Over 60% of prostate cancer deaths occur in men who never had a PSA test

### Key Clinical Points
- Less than 40% of US men who received a PSA test report having a shared decision-making conversation [^480^]
- Decision aids improve knowledge but have modest effect on screening likelihood
- After the 2012 USPSTF recommendation against routine screening, PSA rates fell 20-40%, and late-stage diagnoses subsequently increased [^479^]
- If screening: PSA every 2 years is reasonable; abnormal result requires urology referral

### Cancer Screening Comparison Table: Organization Guidelines

| Cancer Type | USPSTF | ACS | ACOG/ACR | Notes |
|-------------|--------|-----|----------|-------|
| **Breast** | Biennial 40-74 (B) | Annual 45+ (option 40-44) | Annual 40+ | USPSTF lowered to 40 in 2024 [^455^] [^416^] |
| **Cervical** | Pap 21-29 q3y; HPV 30-65 q5y (A) | Same as USPSTF | Same as USPSTF | Primary HPV testing preferred 30-65 [^466^] |
| **Colorectal** | Age 45-75, any modality (A) | Same | Same | Lowered from 50 in 2021 [^494^] |
| **Lung** | LDCT 50-80, 20 py, current/quit <15y (B) | 50-80, 20 py, ever smoker | Same as USPSTF | Shared decision-making required [^468^] |
| **Prostate** | Shared decision 55-69 (C); no routine 70+ (D) | Shared decision 50+ (informed decision) | Shared decision 55-69 | Less than 40% report SDM [^482^] [^469^] |

# Chapter 3: Cardiovascular Risk Reduction

## 3.1 Overview

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading cause of death worldwide. Preventive strategies include risk assessment, lifestyle modification, pharmacotherapy (statins, antihypertensives), and selective use of aspirin. This chapter integrates current evidence and guidelines for primary cardiovascular prevention.

---

## 3.2 ASCVD Risk Assessment: The PREVENT Equations (2024)

### Overview
The American Heart Association's **PREVENT (Predicting Risk of cardiovascular disease EVENTs)** equations are now the preferred risk calculator, replacing the 2013 Pooled Cohort Equations (PCE). [^470^] [^472^] [^493^]

### Key Features of PREVENT
| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Age range** | 30-79 years |
| **Race variable** | **Race-free** -- removes race as predictor, addressing health equity concerns |
| **Additional variables** | Incorporates kidney function (eGFR) and BMI |
| **Risk timeframes** | Provides both 10-year and 30-year risk estimates |
| **Outcomes** | Total CVD, ASCVD, Heart Failure (separate estimates) |
| **Accuracy** | Recalibrated to contemporary event rates (PCE overestimated by 40-50%) [^493^] |

### Risk Categories (2026 ACC/AHA Guidelines) [^138^] [^470^]

| Category | 10-Year ASCVD Risk | Recommended Action |
|----------|-------------------|-------------------|
| **Low** | <3% | Lifestyle counseling; no pharmacotherapy |
| **Borderline** | 3% to <5% | Moderate-intensity statin may be reasonable after clinician-patient discussion |
| **Intermediate** | 5% to <10% | At least moderate-intensity statin; high-intensity at upper end |
| **High** | >=10% | High-intensity statin for >=50% LDL-C reduction |

### Risk-Enhancing Factors [^493^]
When risk-based decision for statin therapy is uncertain, consider these factors:

| Category | Risk Enhancers |
|----------|---------------|
| **Family history** | Premature ASCVD (male <55, female <65) |
| **Inflammatory conditions** | Lupus, rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis, HIV |
| **Cardiometabolic** | Overweight/obesity, metabolic syndrome, diabetes, CKD, pre-eclampsia |
| **Ancestry** | South Asian, Filipino |
| **Reproductive** | Early menopause, gestational diabetes, preeclampsia |
| **Biomarkers** | Elevated Lp(a), apoB, hsCRP, triglycerides |

### Coronary Artery Calcium (CAC) Scoring
Used to reclassify risk when uncertainty remains after PREVENT + risk enhancers:

| CAC Score | Recommended Action |
|-----------|-------------------|
| **0** | May avoid statin therapy (unless smoker, diabetes, strong family history) |
| **1-99** | Moderate-intensity statin |
| **100-999** | High-intensity statin |
| **>1000** | Very high risk; intensify therapy beyond statins [^138^] |

### How to Calculate ASCVD Risk (Step-by-Step)
1. Obtain fasting lipid panel (total cholesterol, HDL-C, LDL-C, triglycerides)
2. Record systolic blood pressure (on treatment or not)
3. Record presence of diabetes, smoking status, age, sex
4. Obtain eGFR (for PREVENT) and BMI
5. Enter data into PREVENT calculator
6. Determine risk category
7. Consider risk-enhancing factors for borderline/intermediate risk
8. Consider CAC scoring if risk-enhancer assessment is inconclusive

---

## 3.3 Statin Therapy for Primary Prevention

### Overview
Statins are the cornerstone of pharmacologic ASCVD risk reduction. The 2026 ACC/AHA guidelines emphasize an intensity-based approach rather than LDL-C target-based therapy. [^138^] [^493^]

### Statin Intensity Classification

| Intensity | Expected LDL-C Reduction | Examples |
|-----------|-------------------------|----------|
| **High-intensity** | >50% | Atorvastatin 40-80 mg, Rosuvastatin 20-40 mg |
| **Moderate-intensity** | 30-49% | Atorvastatin 10-20 mg, Rosuvastatin 5-10 mg, Simvastatin 20-40 mg, Pravastatin 40-80 mg, Pitavastatin 2-4 mg |
| **Low-intensity** | <30% | Simvastatin 10 mg, Pravastatin 10-20 mg, Lovastatin 20 mg |

### Indications for Statin Therapy

| Patient Category | Statin Recommendation |
|-----------------|----------------------|
| **LDL-C >=190 mg/dL** | High-intensity statin regardless of calculated risk |
| **Age 40-75 with diabetes** | At least moderate-intensity statin |
| **10-year ASCVD risk >=10%** | High-intensity statin |
| **10-year risk 5-9.9%** | Moderate-intensity statin; high-intensity at upper end |
| **10-year risk 3-4.9% with risk enhancers** | Moderate-intensity statin after discussion |

### Add-On Therapy (if LDL-C goal not achieved on maximally tolerated statin) [^138^]
- **Ezetimibe:** First add-on; inhibits cholesterol absorption
- **PCSK9 inhibitor (evolocumab, alirocumab):** For very high-risk patients with persistent elevation
- **Bempedoic acid:** For statin-intolerant patients or additional LDL lowering
- **Icosapent ethyl:** For TG 150-499 mg/dL on statin with additional risk factors

### Special Populations
| Population | Consideration |
|------------|--------------|
| **Age >75** | Individualize based on life expectancy, multimorbidity, frailty, patient preference |
| **Life expectancy <1 year** | May discontinue lipid-lowering therapy |
| **CKD Stage 3+** | Moderate-intensity statin or statin + ezetimibe |
| **HIV** | Statins generally safe; consider drug interactions (avoid simvastatin) |
| **Pregnancy** | Statins contraindicated; discontinue before conception |
| **Liver disease** | Avoid in decompensated cirrhosis; caution with active liver disease |

### Monitoring
- Baseline lipid panel before initiation
- Recheck 4-12 weeks after initiation or dose change
- Then every 3-12 months
- HbA1c if diabetes suspected
- Liver enzymes if clinically indicated (routine monitoring not required)

### Statin Intolerance Management
- Verify true intolerance vs. anticipated side effects
- Try alternative statin (hydrophilic statins: pravastatin, rosuvastatin have lower myopathy risk)
- Try alternate-day dosing
- Add ezetimibe to low-dose statin
- Consider bempedoic acid if completely statin-intolerant

## 3.4 Blood Pressure Management

### Classification (ACC/AHA)

| Category | Systolic (mmHg) | Diastolic (mmHg) |
|----------|----------------|------------------|
| **Normal** | <120 | and <80 |
| **Elevated** | 120-129 | and <80 |
| **Stage 1 Hypertension** | 130-139 | or 80-89 |
| **Stage 2 Hypertension** | >=140 | or >=90 |
| **Hypertensive Crisis** | >=180 | and/or >=120 |

### Treatment Thresholds
- Target **<130/80 mmHg** for most adults
- Start pharmacotherapy at >=130/80 for most patients with 10-year CVD risk >=10%
- Start at >=140/90 for patients with lower 10-year CVD risk

### First-Line Agents
- **Thiazide diuretics** (chlorthalidone, HCTZ)
- **ACE inhibitors**
- **ARBs** (if ACE inhibitor-intolerant)
- **Calcium channel blockers** (dihydropyridines)

### Key Clinical Points
- **Proper measurement technique** is essential: seated quietly for 5 minutes, appropriate cuff size, arm at heart level
- **Out-of-office BP monitoring** recommended (home BP monitoring, ambulatory BP monitoring)
- White coat hypertension (elevated office BP, normal out-of-office) and masked hypertension (normal office, elevated out-of-office) are common
- Two or more readings on at least two occasions are needed for diagnosis
- Lifestyle modifications are first-line for elevated BP and Stage 1 with low CVD risk

## 3.5 Aspirin for Primary Prevention

### Overview
The role of aspirin for primary cardiovascular prevention has been substantially narrowed based on accumulating evidence that bleeding risks often outweigh benefits in populations without established CVD. [^508^] [^513^]

### Landmark Trials

| Trial | Population | Key Findings |
|-------|-----------|-------------|
| **ARRIVE (2018)** [^508^] | Moderate-risk patients | Aspirin did not lower CVD events; increased GI bleeding |
| **ASPREE (2018)** [^508^] | Adults >70 years | No benefit on disability-free survival; increased all-cause mortality |
| **ASCEND (2018)** [^513^] | Diabetics | Reduced serious vascular events but increased major bleeding |

### Meta-Analysis (Zheng 2019) [^508^]
- Aspirin associated with reduced composite CV events (HR 0.89)
- BUT increased major bleeding (HR 1.43)
- **NNT = 265** for CVD benefit; **NNH = 210** for bleeding harm
- Net benefit is minimal or negative for most patients

### Current Guidelines (ACC/AHA) [^508^]

| Population | Recommendation |
|------------|---------------|
| **Adults >70 years** | Do NOT use for primary prevention |
| **Adults with increased bleeding risk** | Do NOT use for primary prevention |
| **Adults 40-70 at higher ASCVD risk WITHOUT increased bleeding risk** | May consider after shared decision-making |
| **Adults with established ASCVD** | Aspirin remains indicated (secondary prevention) |

### Key Clinical Points
- Aspirin remains **strongly indicated for secondary prevention** (established ASCVD, recent MI, stroke, PCI, CABG)
- For primary prevention, shared decision-making is essential
- If used for primary prevention: 81 mg daily is the standard dose
- Bleeding risk factors to consider: prior GI bleed, PUD, concurrent anticoagulants, thrombocytopenia, liver disease, age >70

## 3.6 Lifestyle Counseling for Cardiovascular Risk Reduction

### Diet
- Emphasize vegetables, fruits, whole grains, legumes, nuts, fish, poultry
- Limit saturated fats (<10% of calories), trans fats, sodium (<2,300 mg/day), red meat, sweets, sugar-sweetened beverages
- **Mediterranean diet** and **DASH diet** have the strongest evidence for CVD prevention
- PREDIMED trial: Mediterranean diet with olive oil or nuts reduced major CV events by ~30%

### Physical Activity
- At least **150 minutes/week** moderate-intensity or **75 minutes/week** vigorous [^489^]
- Muscle-strengthening activities 2+ days/week [^492^]
- Any activity is better than none; even light activity offsets some risk

### Weight Management
- BMI goal: 18.5-24.9 kg/m2
- Weight loss of 3-5% produces clinically meaningful improvements in BP, lipids, glucose
- 10% weight loss produces greater benefits
- Waist circumference: Men >40 inches, Women >35 inches = increased metabolic risk

### Smoking Cessation
- **Most important single preventive measure** a patient can take
- Risk of MI decreases within 24 hours of quitting
- At 1 year, excess coronary risk is reduced by 50%
- At 15 years, risk approaches that of never-smokers
- Combination of counseling + pharmacotherapy most effective (see Chapter 5)

### Alcohol
- If consumed: limit to <=1 drink/day for women, <=2 drinks/day for men
- No level of alcohol consumption is safe for cardiovascular protection (contradicting earlier "J-curve" evidence)
- Heavy drinking increases BP, triglycerides, arrhythmia risk, and cardiomyopathy risk

### Sleep
- Screen for obstructive sleep apnea in patients with hypertension resistant to treatment, obesity, loud snoring
- Treat if confirmed (CPAP reduces BP and improves cardiovascular outcomes)

# Chapter 4: Diabetes Prevention

## 4.1 Overview

Type 2 diabetes affects approximately 37 million Americans, with another 96 million having prediabetes. The progression from prediabetes to type 2 diabetes can be prevented or delayed through intensive lifestyle intervention and, in selected cases, metformin pharmacotherapy. The landmark Diabetes Prevention Program (DPP) demonstrated that lifestyle intervention reduces diabetes incidence by 58%. [^485^] [^486^]

---

## 4.2 Prediabetes: Definition and Diagnosis

### Diagnostic Criteria (ADA)

| Test | Prediabetes Range (Impaired) | Diabetes Threshold |
|------|------------------------------|-------------------|
| **Fasting Plasma Glucose (FPG)** | 100-125 mg/dL (IFG) | >=126 mg/dL |
| **2-Hour OGTT** | 140-199 mg/dL (IGT) | >=200 mg/dL |
| **HbA1c** | **5.7-6.4%** | >=6.5% |

### Key Clinical Notes
- Any single abnormal result should be confirmed on a subsequent day
- HbA1c may be unreliable in conditions affecting red blood cell turnover (hemoglobinopathies, anemia, pregnancy)
- IFG and IGT together indicate very high risk for progression to diabetes

### Risk Factors for Prediabetes/Diabetes

| Category | Risk Factors |
|----------|-------------|
| **Weight** | Overweight/obesity (BMI >=25; >=23 in Asian Americans) |
| **Age** | >=35 years |
| **Family history** | First-degree relative with diabetes |
| **Obstetric** | History of gestational diabetes |
| **Physical activity** | Sedentary lifestyle |
| **Race/ethnicity** | African American, Hispanic/Latino, Native American, Asian American, Pacific Islander |
| **Comorbidities** | Hypertension, dyslipidemia, PCOS, obstructive sleep apnea, CVD |

---

## 4.3 Screening for Prediabetes and Diabetes

### ADA Screening Recommendations

| Population | Screening Guidance |
|------------|-------------------|
| **All adults >=35 years** | Screen regardless of risk factors |
| **Adults <35 with risk factors** | Screen |
| **All adults with overweight/obesity** | Screen with one or more risk factors |
| **Women with gestational diabetes** | Screen at 4-12 weeks postpartum; then lifelong screening every 3 years |
| **Normal result** | Rescreen every 3 years |
| **Prediabetes** | Test annually |
| **Overweight/obese children/adolescents** | Start at age 10 or onset of puberty if risk factors present |

### Preferred Screening Tests
- HbA1c (most convenient; no fasting required)
- Fasting plasma glucose
- 2-hour oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT)
- Any test is acceptable; abnormal results should be confirmed

---

## 4.4 Diabetes Prevention Program (DPP): Intensive Lifestyle Intervention

### The Landmark DPP Trial (2002) [^485^] [^486^]

The Diabetes Prevention Program was a major multicenter randomized controlled trial comparing intensive lifestyle intervention, metformin, and placebo in adults at high risk for type 2 diabetes.

| Intervention | Diabetes Risk Reduction |
|-------------|------------------------|
| **Intensive lifestyle intervention** | **58% reduction** (71% in adults >=60 years) |
| **Metformin** | 31% reduction |
| **Placebo** | Baseline |

### DPP Lifestyle Goals
1. **Weight loss of 7%** of body weight
2. **Physical activity of 150+ minutes/week** of moderate-intensity activity

### 10-Year Follow-Up (DPPOS) [^485^]
- DPP participants remained **one-third less likely** to develop diabetes a decade later
- Average delay in diabetes onset: approximately 4 years
- Long-term cardiovascular benefits also observed

### CDC National DPP [^485^] [^486^]
The CDC established a nationwide, evidence-based lifestyle change program based on the DPP:

| Feature | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **Duration** | Year-long structured program |
| **Phase 1 (Months 1-6)** | 16 weekly core sessions |
| **Phase 2 (Months 7-12)** | 6 monthly maintenance sessions |
| **Goals** | 5-7% weight loss; 150 minutes/week physical activity |
| **Delivery** | In-person, online, or combination |
| **Curriculum** | CDC-recognized lifestyle change curriculum |
| **Coverage** | Medicare, many Medicaid programs, most private insurers |

### Referral Criteria for CDC National DPP
- HbA1c: 5.7-6.4%, OR
- FPG: 100-125 mg/dL, OR
- 2-hour OGTT: 140-199 mg/dL, OR
- Prior gestational diabetes

### Real-World Effectiveness [^495^]
- 35.5% of participants achieve 5% weight loss
- Average weight loss: 4.2%
- 41.8% meeting physical activity goal
- Greater attendance = greater weight loss (0.3% per additional session)
- Program completion is the strongest predictor of success

### Practical Counseling Points for Prediabetes
1. Explain what prediabetes means and that progression to diabetes is NOT inevitable
2. Emphasize that modest weight loss (5-7%) and physical activity (150 min/week) are highly effective
3. Refer to CDC-recognized DPP program if available
4. Discuss metformin as an adjunct for high-risk individuals
5. Schedule annual HbA1c monitoring
6. Address cardiovascular risk factors simultaneously (BP, lipids, smoking)

---

## 4.5 Metformin for Diabetes Prevention

### Overview
Metformin is the only medication with evidence for diabetes prevention, though its effect is less robust than lifestyle intervention. It is NOT FDA-approved specifically for diabetes prevention. [^471^]

### ADA Recommendations for Metformin Use in Prediabetes
Metformin should be considered for diabetes prevention, especially in individuals with:

| Criteria | Rationale |
|----------|-----------|
| **BMI >=35 kg/m2** | Higher-risk group; metformin aids weight management |
| **Age <60 years** | Greater absolute benefit in younger adults |
| **Women with prior gestational diabetes** | Very high risk for progression; strong evidence for metformin benefit |
| **Both IFG + IGT** | Very high risk category |

### Metformin Dosing

| Phase | Dose | Notes |
|-------|------|-------|
| **Initiation** | 500 mg daily with evening meal | Start low to minimize GI side effects |
| **Titration Week 1-2** | 500 mg twice daily | Increase if tolerated |
| **Titration Week 3-4** | 850 mg twice daily | Target dose for most patients |
| **Maximum** | 1000 mg twice daily | Not typically needed for prevention |

### Monitoring
- **eGFR** should be >=30 mL/min/1.73m2 for safe use
- **Vitamin B12** levels periodically with long-term use (annual or biennial)
- Discontinue if eGFR falls below 30
- Discontinue 48 hours before iodinated contrast studies; resume when eGFR stable

### Side Effects
- Gastrointestinal: diarrhea, nausea, abdominal discomfort (usually transient; minimized by slow titration and taking with meals)
- Vitamin B12 malabsorption (long-term)
- Metallic taste (rare)
- Lactic acidosis (very rare; risk primarily in severe renal impairment)

### Key Clinical Points
- Metformin is less effective than lifestyle intervention (31% vs. 58% risk reduction)
- Best used as adjunct to lifestyle changes, not replacement
- May provide cardiovascular benefits independent of glycemic control
- Cost-effective; generic formulation is inexpensive
- Consider for patients unable to access or complete DPP programs

# Chapter 5: Smoking Cessation

## 5.1 Overview

Tobacco use remains the leading preventable cause of death globally, responsible for over 8 million deaths annually. Smoking cessation produces immediate and substantial health benefits, with cardiovascular risk reduction occurring within days to weeks. Comprehensive cessation strategies combining behavioral counseling and pharmacotherapy achieve the highest quit rates. [^477^]

### Health Benefits of Quitting

| Timeframe | Health Benefit |
|-----------|---------------|
| 20 minutes | Heart rate and blood pressure decrease |
| 12 hours | Carbon monoxide levels normalize |
| 2-12 weeks | Circulation improves; lung function increases |
| 1-9 months | Coughing and shortness of breath decrease |
| 1 year | Excess coronary heart disease risk reduced by 50% |
| 5 years | Stroke risk reduced to that of non-smoker |
| 10 years | Lung cancer death rate drops to 30-50% of continuing smoker |
| 15 years | Coronary heart disease risk approaches that of never-smoker |

---

## 5.2 The 5As and 5Rs Frameworks

### The 5As (Clinical Intervention Framework)

| Step | Action | Example Language |
|------|--------|-----------------|
| **1. Ask** | Systematically identify tobacco use at every visit | "Do you currently use any tobacco products?" |
| **2. Advise** | Strongly urge all tobacco users to quit in a clear, personalized manner | "As your clinician, I strongly recommend you quit smoking. Quitting is the single most important thing you can do for your health." |
| **3. Assess** | Determine willingness to make a quit attempt within the next 30 days | "Are you ready to set a quit date within the next month?" |
| **4. Assist** | Provide a quit plan including medication + counseling | Offer pharmacotherapy and behavioral support resources |
| **5. Arrange** | Schedule follow-up within the first week after quit date | "I'll call you 3 days after your quit date to see how you're doing." |

### The 5Rs (For Those Not Ready to Quit)

| Step | Action | Counseling Approach |
|------|--------|-------------------|
| **1. Relevance** | Encourage patient to identify personal reasons to quit | "What matters most to you in your life? How might quitting help with that?" |
| **2. Risks** | Ask patient to identify potential negative consequences | "What health concerns worry you most about continuing to smoke?" |
| **3. Rewards** | Ask patient to identify benefits of quitting | "What would be the best things about being smoke-free?" |
| **4. Roadblocks** | Ask patient to identify barriers to quitting | "What has made it hard to quit in the past? Let's problem-solve together." |
| **5. Repetition** | Repeat motivation intervention at every visit | Reinforce that you're ready to help whenever they're ready |

---

## 5.3 Pharmacotherapy for Smoking Cessation

### WHO 2024 Recommendations [^477^]
WHO strongly recommends the following first-line medications for tobacco cessation:

### First-Line Medications

| Medication | Dosing | Key Considerations | Evidence |
|------------|--------|-------------------|----------|
| **Varenicline** | 0.5 mg daily x3 days, then 0.5 mg BID x4 days, then 1 mg BID for 12 weeks | Most effective single agent; take with food and full glass of water; nausea, vivid dreams; reduce dose in severe renal impairment (CrCl <30) | RR 2.27 vs. placebo (Cochrane) [^475^] |
| **Combination NRT** | Patch (long-acting: 21mg/14mg/7mg based on cigarettes/day) + gum/lozenge/inhaler (short-acting for breakthrough cravings) | Patch provides steady-state; short-acting for cravings; combination more effective than single NRT | Strong recommendation, high certainty [^477^] |
| **Bupropion SR** | 150 mg daily x3 days, then 150 mg BID; start 1-2 weeks before quit date | Contraindicated in seizure disorder, eating disorders, concurrent MAOIs; monitor for mood changes; may cause insomnia | Strong recommendation, high certainty [^477^] |
| **Cytisine** | Per package instructions; typically gradual titration | Partial nicotine agonist; strong recommendation, moderate certainty; less widely available in US but accessible globally | Strong recommendation, moderate certainty [^477^] |

### Combination Therapy (for Enhanced Efficacy)

| Combination | Recommendation Level |
|-------------|---------------------|
| **Bupropion + varenicline** | Conditional recommendation when inadequate response to first-line [^477^] |
| **Bupropion + NRT** | Conditional recommendation [^477^] |
| **Varenicline + NRT** | May be considered; evidence growing |

### NRT Product Comparison

| Product | Onset | Duration | Best For |
|---------|-------|----------|----------|
| **Patch** | Slow (hours) | 24 hours | Baseline craving reduction |
| **Gum** | Fast (10-15 min) | 20-30 min | Sudden cravings; oral gratification |
| **Lozenge** | Fast (10-15 min) | 20-30 min | Sudden cravings; discretion |
| **Inhaler** | Fast (minutes) | Variable | Behavioral/hand-to-mouth substitute |
| **Nasal spray** | Very fast (minutes) | Variable | Severe, rapid-onset cravings |

### Varenicline Detailed Protocol
1. **Days 1-3:** 0.5 mg once daily (after eating, with full glass of water)
2. **Days 4-7:** 0.5 mg twice daily
3. **Day 8 through Week 12:** 1 mg twice daily
4. **Set quit date:** Between days 8-14 of treatment
5. **Extension:** May continue for additional 12 weeks if successfully quit
6. **Renal impairment (CrCl 30-49):** Max 0.5 mg twice daily; (CrCl <30): Max 0.5 mg daily

### Contraindications and Precautions

| Medication | Contraindications | Precautions |
|------------|-------------------|-------------|
| **Varenicline** | None absolute | Neuropsychiatric symptoms (monitor); renal impairment |
| **Bupropion SR** | Seizure disorder, anorexia/bulimia, MAOIs in past 14 days, abrupt alcohol/benzodiazepine discontinuation | Insomnia (avoid bedtime dosing), hypertension, psychiatric conditions |
| **NRT** | None absolute | Cardiovascular disease (use with caution); pregnancy (prefer behavioral) |

## 5.4 Behavioral Counseling

### Effective Components [^477^]
- **Multisession counseling** (up to 5 sessions or more) most effective
- **Duration:** At least 10 minutes per session
- **Formats:** Individual, group, and telephone counseling all effective
- **Intensity:** Greater intensity = greater quit rates (dose-response relationship)

### Resources for Behavioral Support

| Resource | Contact | Description |
|----------|---------|-------------|
| **1-800-QUIT-NOW** | 1-800-784-8669 | Free, evidence-based quitline; connects to state program |
| **Smokefree.gov** | smokefree.gov | Free text messaging program, apps, chat support |
| **CDC Tips Campaign** | cdc.gov/tobacco | Real stories from former smokers; motivation tool |
| **Healthcare system programs** | Varies | Many hospitals and health systems offer structured cessation programs |

### Digital Interventions [^477^]

| Modality | Evidence Level | Notes |
|----------|---------------|-------|
| **Text messaging** | Moderate certainty | Effective adjunct; widely accessible |
| **Smartphone apps** | Low certainty | May supplement other interventions; variable quality |
| **Internet-based programs** | Very low certainty | May serve as adjuncts |

## 5.5 E-Cigarettes for Smoking Cessation

### Current Evidence and Guidance [^520^]

| Organization | Position |
|-------------|----------|
| **USPSTF** | Evidence currently **insufficient** to recommend e-cigarettes for smoking cessation |
| **FDA** | No e-cigarette has FDA approval as a smoking cessation therapeutic |
| **Cochrane Review (2024)** | Nicotine e-cigarettes help people stop smoking for at least 6 months; may outperform NRT |
| **UK NHS** | Recommends e-cigarettes as a quit aid |

### Key Clinical Points
- E-cigarettes may help some smokers quit but are **not risk-free**
- **Dual use** (cigarettes + e-cigarettes) is common and substantially less beneficial than complete switching
- Youth initiation with e-cigarettes represents a significant public health concern
- If used for cessation, the goal should be **complete switching**, not dual use
- Heavy metals, volatile organic compounds, and flavoring chemicals may pose long-term risks
- Vaping-associated lung injury (EVALI) has been documented, primarily linked to THC-containing products with vitamin E acetate

### Recommendation
- Counsel patients that FDA-approved pharmacotherapies (varenicline, NRT, bupropion) have stronger evidence bases
- If patient elects to use e-cigarettes for cessation, set a clear plan for complete transition and eventual discontinuation
- Do not recommend e-cigarettes for non-smokers, youth, or pregnant persons

## 5.6 Relapse Prevention

### High-Risk Situations for Relapse
- Negative emotional states (stress, depression, anger)
- Social pressure (being around other smokers)
- Alcohol consumption
- Exposure to smoking cues
- Overconfidence after extended abstinence

### Relapse Prevention Strategies
1. Continue pharmacotherapy for recommended duration (typically 12+ weeks)
2. Identify and plan for trigger situations
3. Build social support for nonsmoking
4. Practice coping skills for cravings (delay, deep breathing, drink water, distract)
5. Schedule follow-up at 1 week, 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, and 1 year
6. Reinforce success; normalize slips; encourage continued effort

# Chapter 6: Nutrition and Exercise

## 6.1 Overview

Dietary patterns and physical activity are foundational determinants of health, influencing risk for cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, obesity, cancer, and all-cause mortality. This chapter synthesizes current guidelines for nutrition and physical activity counseling in clinical practice.

---

## 6.2 US Dietary Guidelines (2020-2025)

### Core Principles

1. Follow a healthy dietary pattern at every life stage
2. Customize and enjoy nutrient-dense food and beverage choices
3. Focus on meeting food group needs with nutrient-dense foods and beverages; stay within calorie limits
4. Limit foods and beverages higher in added sugars, saturated fat, and sodium; limit alcoholic beverages

### Recommended Dietary Pattern (per 2,000-calorie diet)

| Food Group | Recommended Amount | Key Guidance |
|------------|-------------------|--------------|
| **Vegetables** | 2.5 cups/day | Variety of types and colors (dark green, red/orange, legumes, starchy) |
| **Fruits** | 2 cups/day | Whole fruits preferred; limit juice |
| **Grains** | 6 oz/day | At least half whole grains |
| **Dairy** | 3 cups/day | Fat-free or low-fat milk, yogurt, cheese |
| **Protein foods** | 5.5 oz/day | Lean meats, poultry, eggs, seafood, nuts, seeds, soy |
| **Oils** | 27 g/day | Vegetable oils, nuts, avocados |

### Limits

| Nutrient | Limit | Common Sources |
|----------|-------|---------------|
| **Added sugars** | <10% of calories/day | Sugar-sweetened beverages, desserts, candy |
| **Saturated fat** | <10% of calories/day | Red meat, full-fat dairy, butter, coconut oil |
| **Sodium** | <2,300 mg/day | Processed foods, restaurant meals, canned goods |
| **Alcohol** | <=2 drinks/day (men); <=1 drink/day (women) | Beer, wine, spirits |

## 6.3 Mediterranean and DASH Diets

### Mediterranean Diet
The Mediterranean dietary pattern is consistently associated with reduced cardiovascular disease, stroke, and all-cause mortality in prospective cohort studies and randomized trials.

| Component | Emphasis | Limit |
|-----------|----------|-------|
| **Primary fat** | Extra virgin olive oil | Butter, lard |
| **Protein** | Fish, seafood, poultry, legumes | Red meat (rarely) |
| **Grains** | Whole grains | Refined grains |
| **Fruits/vegetables** | Abundant (7-10 servings/day) | -- |
| **Nuts/seeds** | Daily (handful) | -- |
| **Dairy** | Moderate (fermented: yogurt, cheese) | -- |
| **Wine** | Moderate with meals (optional) | Excess |
| **Sweets** | Minimal | Sugar, desserts |

**Evidence:** PREDIMED trial demonstrated approximately **30% reduction** in major cardiovascular events with Mediterranean diet supplemented with olive oil or nuts.

### DASH Diet (Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension)
Originally developed to lower blood pressure, the DASH diet also reduces LDL cholesterol and cardiovascular risk.

| Component | Emphasis | Limit |
|-----------|----------|-------|
| **Fruits/vegetables** | 8-10 servings/day | -- |
| **Whole grains** | 6-8 servings/day | Refined grains |
| **Low-fat dairy** | 2-3 servings/day | Full-fat dairy |
| **Lean protein** | Poultry, fish, plant-based | Red meat, processed meat |
| **Nuts/seeds/legumes** | 4-5 servings/week | -- |
| **Fats/oils** | Healthy fats (2-3 servings) | Saturated fat |
| **Sodium** | 1,500-2,300 mg/day | Processed foods |
| **Sweets** | <=5 servings/week | -- |

**Evidence:** DASH diet can lower systolic BP by 8-14 mmHg -- comparable to single antihypertensive medication.

### Practical Comparison

| Feature | Mediterranean | DASH | Both |
|---------|--------------|------|------|
| **Primary benefit** | CVD prevention, longevity | BP reduction, CVD prevention | Anti-inflammatory |
| **Key distinguishing element** | Olive oil as primary fat | Sodium restriction emphasis | Plant-forward |
| **Alcohol** | Moderate wine optional | Not emphasized | If consumed, moderation |
| **Evidence strength** | Strong (RCTs) | Strong (RCTs) | Both guideline-recommended |

## 6.4 Physical Activity Guidelines

### Adults (18-64 years) [^487^] [^489^] [^492^]

| Parameter | Recommendation |
|-----------|---------------|
| **Aerobic activity** | 150-300 minutes/week moderate-intensity, OR 75-150 minutes/week vigorous-intensity, OR equivalent combination |
| **Muscle-strengthening** | All major muscle groups on **2+ days/week** [^492^] |
| **Additional benefits** | >300 minutes/week moderate activity provides additional health benefits |
| **Sedentary behavior** | Reduce sitting time; any activity is better than none |

### Older Adults (65+ years)
- Same as adults PLUS **balance training** to prevent falls
- Balance activities on **3+ days/week**
- Adjust intensity based on fitness level and chronic conditions

### Children/Adolescents (6-17 years)
- **>=60 minutes/day** moderate-to-vigorous physical activity
- Vigorous activity on **>=3 days/week**
- Muscle- and bone-strengthening on **>=3 days/week**

### Intensity Assessment

| Intensity | Talk Test | METs | Examples |
|-----------|-----------|------|----------|
| **Light** | Can sing easily | <3 | Slow walking, light household tasks |
| **Moderate** | Can talk but not sing | 3-5.9 | Brisk walking, water aerobics, cycling <10 mph, dancing, doubles tennis [^489^] |
| **Vigorous** | Cannot maintain conversation | >=6 | Running, swimming laps, singles tennis, basketball [^489^] |

### Key Messages for Patient Counseling [^489^]
- **Some activity is better than none** -- start where you are
- Activity can be accumulated in short bouts throughout the day (10+ minutes each)
- Reduce sedentary time; even light activity offsets some risk
- Choose activities you enjoy -- adherence is the most important factor
- Include both aerobic and resistance training for optimal health benefits

### Exercise Prescription Framework

| FITT Component | Recommendation |
|----------------|---------------|
| **F - Frequency** | Moderate aerobic: 5 days/week; Vigorous: 3 days/week; Resistance: 2-3 days/week |
| **I - Intensity** | Moderate (RPE 5-6/10) or vigorous (RPE 7-8/10) |
| **T - Time** | 30-60 minutes/session moderate; 20-30 minutes vigorous |
| **T - Type** | Walking, swimming, cycling, dancing, resistance training, yoga |

## 6.5 Obesity Screening and Counseling

### Screening

| Measure | Classification | Risk Threshold |
|---------|---------------|----------------|
| **BMI** | Underweight: <18.5; Normal: 18.5-24.9; Overweight: 25-29.9; Obesity Class I: 30-34.9; Class II: 35-39.9; Class III: >=40 | Screen annually |
| **Waist circumference** | Increased risk: Men >40 inches, Women >35 inches | Measure with BMI >=25 |

### Behavioral Counseling (USPSTF)
- Adults with **overweight or obesity**: Offer or refer to intensive behavioral interventions (**Grade B**)
- Programs should be **6-12 months** with **>=12 sessions**
- Components: behavioral management, physical activity, dietary changes
- Goal: **5-10% weight loss** over 6 months

### Pharmacotherapy for Obesity
Consider for BMI >=30, or >=27 with weight-related comorbidity:

| Medication | Mechanism | Key Considerations |
|------------|-----------|-------------------|
| **Semaglutide 2.4 mg (Wegovy)** | GLP-1 receptor agonist | Weekly injection; 15-20% weight loss; GI side effects; thyroid C-cell tumor risk (contraindicated in MEN2, MTC) |
| **Liraglutide 3.0 mg (Saxenda)** | GLP-1 receptor agonist | Daily injection; 8-10% weight loss |
| **Tirzepatide (Zepbound)** | Dual GIP/GLP-1 agonist | Weekly injection; 20-25% weight loss; most effective |
| **Orlistat** | Lipase inhibitor | 5-10% weight loss; GI side effects; reduce fat-soluble vitamins |
| **Phentermine/topiramate ER (Qsymia)** | Sympathomimetic + anticonvulsant | 7-10% weight loss; teratogenic; CNS side effects |
| **Naltrexone/bupropion (Contrave)** | Opioid antagonist + antidepressant | 5-8% weight loss; monitor BP; contraindicated in seizure disorder |

### Bariatric Surgery
- Indicated for BMI >=40, or >=35 with obesity-related comorbidities
- Most effective long-term treatment for severe obesity (25-35% sustained weight loss)
- Types: Roux-en-Y gastric bypass, sleeve gastrectomy, adjustable gastric banding
- Requires multidisciplinary evaluation and lifelong follow-up

### Key Clinical Points
- Weight stigma is a significant barrier to care; use person-first language ("person with obesity" not "obese patient")
- Set realistic goals; 5-10% weight loss produces clinically meaningful metabolic improvements
- Focus on health behaviors rather than weight alone
- Address underlying contributors: medications, sleep disorders, mental health, food insecurity

## 6.6 Alcohol Screening and Counseling

### USPSTF Recommendation [^511^] [^514^]
- **Screen adults >=18 years for unhealthy alcohol use** (**Grade B**)
- Provide brief behavioral counseling for those engaged in risky/hazardous drinking

### Screening Tools

| Tool | Items | Time | Notes |
|------|-------|------|-------|
| **AUDIT-C** | 3 questions | 1-2 minutes | Best accuracy for brief screening; score >=4 (men) or >=3 (women) is positive |
| **SASQ (Single Question)** | 1 question | <1 minute | "How many times in the past year have you had >=5 (men) or >=4 (women/adults 65+) drinks in a day?" |
| **Full AUDIT** | 10 questions | 2-5 minutes | Comprehensive assessment; identifies alcohol use disorders |

### AUDIT-C Questions
1. How often did you have a drink containing alcohol in the past year?
2. How many drinks did you have on a typical day when you were drinking?
3. How often did you have 6 or more drinks on one occasion?

### Risky Drinking Thresholds [^511^]

| Population | Daily Limit | Weekly Limit |
|------------|------------|--------------|
| **Women** | >3 drinks/day | >7 drinks/week |
| **Men (21-64)** | >4 drinks/day | >14 drinks/week |
| **Adults 65+** | >3 drinks/day | >7 drinks/week |
| **Pregnant women** | **No alcohol** | **No alcohol** |

### Standard Drink Definition
- 12 oz beer (5% alcohol)
- 5 oz wine (12% alcohol)
- 1.5 oz distilled spirits (40% alcohol)

### Brief Intervention (6-15 minutes) [^511^]
Effective brief counseling includes:
1. **Feedback** -- Share screening results and health risks
2. **Advice** -- Recommend reducing or stopping alcohol use
3. **Goal-setting** -- Establish patient-centered drinking goals
4. **Follow-up** -- Schedule return visit to assess progress

### Key Clinical Points
- Brief counseling is effective for reducing drinking in adults with risky/hazardous use
- Patients with alcohol use disorders (AUD) require more intensive treatment referral
- Alcohol interacts with numerous medications (warfarin, benzodiazepines, opioids, acetaminophen)
- Alcohol increases risk for liver disease, certain cancers (breast, esophageal, liver, colorectal), hypertension, trauma
- No level of alcohol consumption is completely safe; risk increases with quantity

# Chapter 7: Mental Health Screening

## 7.1 Overview

Mental health conditions are among the most common causes of disability worldwide. Depression, anxiety, and substance use disorders frequently present in primary care but often go unrecognized. Systematic screening facilitates early identification and treatment, improving outcomes and quality of life. This chapter covers validated screening tools and evidence-based recommendations.

---

## 7.2 Depression Screening

### USPSTF Recommendation
- Screen all adults for depression, including pregnant and postpartum persons (**Grade B**)
- Screening must be coupled with adequate systems for accurate diagnosis, treatment, and follow-up
- Screening positive requires confirmatory diagnostic assessment

### PHQ-2 (Initial Screen)

The PHQ-2 is a brief two-item screen for depression. A positive result triggers administration of the full PHQ-9.

**Questions** (Over the last 2 weeks, how often have you been bothered by):
1. Little interest or pleasure in doing things?
2. Feeling down, depressed, or hopeless?

**Scoring:** 0-3 for each item
- 0 = Not at all
- 1 = Several days
- 2 = More than half the days
- 3 = Nearly every day

**Interpretation:**
| Total Score | Action |
|-------------|--------|
| **0-2** | Depression unlikely; no further action needed |
| **>=3** | **Positive screen** -- administer PHQ-9 |

### PHQ-9 (Full Assessment)

The PHQ-9 is a 9-item instrument corresponding to DSM-5 criteria for major depressive disorder.

**Scoring:** Same 0-3 scale as PHQ-2; total range 0-27

| Score | Severity | Action |
|-------|----------|--------|
| **0-4** | Minimal/none | None; may repeat screen periodically |
| **5-9** | Mild depression | Watchful waiting; consider counseling |
| **10-14** | Moderate depression | Treatment plan; consider medication or therapy |
| **15-19** | Moderately severe depression | Active treatment with medication and/or therapy |
| **20-27** | Severe depression | Prompt treatment; consider specialist referral |

### Critical Item: Question 9 (Suicide Risk)

> **"Over the last 2 weeks, how often have you been bothered by thoughts that you would be better off dead, or of hurting yourself?"**

- **Any positive response (>0)** requires immediate suicide risk assessment (see Section 7.4)
- Document assessment and safety plan
- Do NOT dismiss or normalize suicidal ideation

### Perinatal Depression Screening [^503^]

| Timing | Tool | Cutoff |
|--------|------|--------|
| First prenatal visit | PHQ-9 or EPDS | EPDS >=10 positive |
| Each trimester | PHQ-9 or EPDS | -- |
| 6-week postpartum visit | PHQ-9 or EPDS | -- |
| 6 and 12 months postpartum | PHQ-9 or EPDS | -- |

- EPDS (Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale): 10-item questionnaire validated in pregnancy and postpartum
- Screen fathers/partners when possible (lower cutoff recommended due to different symptom presentation)

### Key Clinical Points
- PHQ-9 score change of 5 points represents clinically meaningful change
- Screening is only beneficial with systems to ensure diagnosis and treatment
- Treat to target: aim for PHQ-9 <5 (remission)
- Follow-up PHQ-9 at 4-6 weeks after treatment initiation
- Consider medical causes of depression (hypothyroidism, B12 deficiency, medication side effects)

---

## 7.3 Anxiety Screening

### USPSTF 2023 Recommendation [^505^]
- Screen all adults **<65 years** for anxiety disorders, including pregnant and postpartum persons (**Grade B**)
- Applies to asymptomatic adults without previously diagnosed anxiety disorder

### GAD-2 (Initial Screen)

**Questions** (Over the last 2 weeks, how often have you been bothered by):
1. Feeling nervous, anxious, or on edge?
2. Not being able to stop or control worrying?

**Scoring:** 0-3 per item (same as PHQ); total 0-6

| Total Score | Action |
|-------------|--------|
| **0-2** | Anxiety unlikely |
| **>=3** | **Positive screen** -- administer GAD-7 |

### GAD-7 (Full Assessment)

The GAD-7 is a 7-item validated tool for assessing anxiety disorder severity.

**Scoring:** 0-3 per item; total range 0-21

| Score | Severity | Action |
|-------|----------|--------|
| **0-4** | Minimal anxiety | None |
| **5-9** | Mild anxiety | Watchful waiting; counseling may help |
| **10-14** | Moderate anxiety | Treatment plan; consider therapy and/or medication |
| **15-21** | Severe anxiety | Active treatment; consider specialist referral |

### Key Clinical Points
- GAD-7 validated in pregnant persons; GAD-2 sensitivity is 1.0 at cutoff >=1 [^505^]
- Screen for both depression and anxiety simultaneously (high comorbidity)
- Consider medical causes: hyperthyroidism, caffeine/stimulant use, medication side effects (steroids, bronchodilators)
- Other anxiety disorders (panic, social anxiety, specific phobias) may require additional screening

### Geriatric Considerations [^505^]
- Geriatric Anxiety Scale (GAS) and Geriatric Anxiety Inventory (GAI) validated for older adults
- Anxiety in older adults often presents with somatic symptoms
- Be alert to overlap with depression and cognitive impairment

---

## 7.4 Suicide Risk Screening

### Overview
Suicide is a leading cause of death in the United States. Screening in primary care can identify at-risk individuals and facilitate intervention. The Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale (C-SSRS) is the most widely validated screening tool. [^509^]

### C-SSRS Screening Version for Primary Care

The C-SSRS asks about the following constructs (within the past month):

1. **Wish to be dead** (passive ideation)
2. **Non-specific active suicidal thoughts**
3. **Active ideation with any methods** (not plan)
4. **Active ideation with intent** (some intent to act)
5. **Active ideation with specific plan**
6. **Preparatory acts or behavior** (e.g., obtaining means, writing goodbye letters)

Also assess history of:
- Aborted or interrupted attempts
- Actual suicide attempts (ever)

### Interpretation and Risk Stratification

| Risk Level | C-SSRS Findings | Management |
|------------|----------------|------------|
| **Low** | No ideation or plan | Outpatient management; routine follow-up |
| **Moderate** | Ideation present but no plan or intent | Close follow-up within 1-2 weeks; safety planning; consider referral |
| **High** | Plan, intent, or recent attempt | **Immediate psychiatric evaluation**; do not leave patient alone; reduce access to lethal means |

### Safety Planning Intervention

A structured safety plan should include:

1. **Recognize warning signs** -- Identify personal triggers and warning signs
2. **Employ internal coping strategies** -- Activities the person can do independently (walking, music, exercise)
3. **Socialize with others who may offer support** -- Places to go with other people (library, coffee shop)
4. **Contact family members or friends who may help** -- Names and phone numbers
5. **Contact mental health professionals or agencies** -- Crisis hotlines, therapist, emergency services
6. **Reduce access to lethal means** -- Remove or lock firearms, medications, other means

### Emergency Resources

| Resource | Contact |
|----------|---------|
| **988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline** | Call or text 988 |
| **Crisis Text Line** | Text HOME to 741741 |
| **Emergency Services** | 911 (if imminent risk) |

### Key Clinical Points [^509^]
- C-SSRS has 67% sensitivity and 76% specificity for suicidal behaviors
- Validated in children as young as 5, veterans with PTSD, psychiatry outpatients
- Recommended by FDA for clinical trials; adopted by CDC
- **Positive screen on any tool requires comprehensive suicide risk assessment**
- Do NOT promise confidentiality if patient is at imminent risk
- Document all assessments and safety planning thoroughly
- Lethal means counseling (firearm removal, medication lockboxes) is an evidence-based intervention

## 7.5 PTSD Screening

### PC-PTSD-5

A 5-item screen for primary care settings:

"In your life, have you ever had any experience that was so frightening, horrible, or upsetting that, in the past month you:"

1. Have had nightmares about it or thought about it when you didn't want to?
2. Tried hard not to think about it or went out of your way to avoid situations that reminded you of it?
3. Were constantly on guard, watchful, or easily startled?
4. Felt numb or detached from others, activities, or your surroundings?
5. Felt guilty or unable to stop blaming yourself or others for the event?

**Interpretation:**
- **0-2 positive responses:** Low probability of PTSD
- **3+ positive responses:** High probability of PTSD; needs further evaluation

### Key Clinical Points
- Screen if patient reports trauma history or presents with symptoms (hypervigilance, avoidance, flashbacks, emotional numbing)
- High prevalence in veterans, first responders, survivors of violence/assault
- Evidence-based treatments: trauma-focused psychotherapy (PE, CPT, EMDR), SSRIs
- Co-occurs frequently with depression and substance use

## 7.6 Substance Use Screening

### Alcohol Screening
See Chapter 6, Section 6.6 for detailed alcohol screening with AUDIT-C and SASQ.

### Drug Use Screening

**USPSTF Recommendations:**
- Screen adults >=18 for unhealthy drug use when services for accurate diagnosis and treatment are available (**Grade B**)
- Evidence insufficient for adolescents

### DAST-10 (Drug Abuse Screening Test)

A 10-item validated tool for screening drug use (excluding alcohol and tobacco):

| Score | Interpretation |
|-------|---------------|
| **0** | No risk |
| **1-2** | Low risk |
| **3-5** | Moderate risk |
| **6-8** | Substantial risk |
| **9-10** | Severe risk |

### Single Question Screen
> "How many times in the past year have you used an illegal drug or used a prescription medication for nonmedical reasons?"

- Any use beyond the prescribed amount or indication warrants further assessment

### Key Clinical Points
- Screen for substance use in a non-judgmental manner
- Ask specifically about prescription drug misuse (opioids, benzodiazepines, stimulants)
- Screen all patients prescribed controlled substances
- High comorbidity between substance use and mental health disorders
- Brief intervention is effective for patients with risky use
- Patients with substance use disorders require referral to specialized treatment

### Screening Summary Table

| Condition | Primary Tool | Positive Threshold | Follow-up Tool |
|-----------|-------------|-------------------|----------------|
| **Depression** | PHQ-2 | >=3 | PHQ-9 |
| **Anxiety** | GAD-2 | >=3 | GAD-7 |

| **PTSD** | PC-PTSD-5 | >=3 positive | Full clinical evaluation |
| **Alcohol misuse** | AUDIT-C or SASQ | AUDIT-C >=4 (M), >=3 (F); SASQ >=1 | Full AUDIT |
| **Drug misuse** | DAST-10 or single question | DAST-10 >=3 | Full clinical assessment |

## 8.2 LGBTQ+ Health

### Health Disparities
LGBTQ+ individuals experience significant health disparities driven by minority stress, discrimination, and healthcare access barriers:

| Domain | Key Disparities |
|--------|----------------|
| **Mental health** | Higher rates of depression, anxiety, suicidal ideation (especially LGBTQ+ youth) |

| **HPV vaccination** | All eligible persons regardless of sexual orientation or gender identity |

### Key Clinical Points
- Create an inclusive clinical environment (intake forms, visible signage)
- Train all staff on LGBTQ+-competent care
- Do not make assumptions about sexual orientation, gender identity, or sexual behavior
- Screen for intimate partner violence (affects all populations, including LGBTQ+)
- Coordinate with specialty care for gender-affirming hormone therapy

## 8.3 PrEP Counseling (Pre-Exposure Prophylaxis for HIV)

### Overview
PrEP is a highly effective biomedical prevention strategy for HIV-negative individuals at substantial risk. When taken as prescribed, PrEP reduces HIV acquisition risk by approximately 99% for sexual transmission. [^498^] [^501^]

### CDC PrEP Indications [^498^]

PrEP is recommended for HIV-negative persons who meet any of the following criteria:

| Route of Exposure | Risk Criteria |
|-------------------|--------------|
| **Sexual (anal or vaginal)** | In past 6 months: sexual partner with HIV (especially unknown/detectable viral load); OR inconsistent condom use with partner whose HIV status is unknown; OR any bacterial STI diagnosis |
| **Injection drug use** | In past 6 months: injection partner with HIV; OR sharing needles/syringes |
| **Other** | Ongoing sexual relationship with HIV-positive partner attempting conception |

### PrEP Options (2024) [^497^] [^498^]

| Option | Regimen | Efficacy | Key Considerations |
|--------|---------|----------|-------------------|
| **TDF/FTC (Truvada)** | Daily oral | ~99% (sexual) | Approved for all risk groups; generic available; monitor renal function |
| **TAF/FTC (Descovy)** | Daily oral | ~99% (sexual) | NOT approved for receptive vaginal sex; better renal/bone safety profile |
| **Cabotegravir (Apretude)** | IM injection every 2 months | ~99% (sexual) | Long-acting; high adherence; injection site reactions; requires loading dose |
| **Lenacapavir** | SC injection every 6 months | >95% (PURPOSE trials) | Longest-acting option; investigational access |

### Baseline Assessment Before PrEP Initiation

| Test | Rationale |
|------|-----------|
| **HIV testing** (Ag/Ab + HIV RNA) | Confirm HIV-negative status |
| **Renal function** (CrCl) | Tenofovir is renally cleared; baseline required |
| **Hepatitis B serology** | TDF/TAF active against HBV; reactivation risk if discontinuing |
| **Pregnancy test** | For persons of childbearing potential |
| **STI screening** (GC/CT, syphilis) | High comorbidity; baseline and ongoing |
| **Hepatitis C antibody** | Screen at baseline |

### Follow-Up Monitoring

| Parameter | Frequency |
|-----------|-----------|
| **HIV testing** | Every 3 months (oral PrEP); prior to each injection (CAB) |
| **Renal function** | Every 6 months (more frequent if age >50 or eGFR <90) |
| **STI screening** | Every 3-6 months |
| **Pregnancy test** | As clinically indicated |
| **Adherence counseling** | Every visit |

### Key Clinical Points
- PrEP does NOT protect against other STIs; condom use still recommended
- Daily adherence is critical for oral PrEP; cabotegravir may be preferred for those with adherence challenges
- Significant cost and insurance access barriers exist despite preventive coverage mandates
- On-demand ("2-1-1") TDF/FTC dosing for MSM (2 pills 2-24 hours before sex, 1 pill 24 hours after, 1 pill 48 hours after) is endorsed by WHO and effective for anal sex
- Coordinate with sexual health services and HIV testing programs

## 8.4 Racial and Ethnic Disparities in Preventive Care

### Overview
Significant disparities exist in preventive care access, screening rates, and outcomes across racial and ethnic groups. Addressing these disparities requires both individual clinical action and systemic advocacy.

### Disparities by Cancer Type

| Cancer Type | Disparity | Actionable Steps |
|-------------|-----------|-----------------|
| **Breast** | Black women: 40% higher death rate despite similar screening rates [^455^]; later stage at diagnosis | Ensure equitable follow-up for abnormal mammograms; consider annual screening starting at 40 |
| **Colorectal** | Black Americans: higher incidence and mortality; lower screening rates | Starting at 45 addresses some disparity; promote FIT as accessible alternative to colonoscopy |
| **Prostate** | Black men: 1.8x diagnosis rate, 2.2x death rate [^479^]; over 60% of deaths in unscreened men | Consider earlier screening (age 45); ensure shared decision-making |
| **Cervical** | Hispanic/Latina women: highest incidence rates; immigrant women face access barriers | Culturally tailored outreach; address language barriers; community health worker programs |
| **Lung** | Black patients less likely to be screened; higher mortality | LDCT expansion includes more Black patients; targeted outreach |

### Systemic Approaches to Address Disparities

| Strategy | Description |
|----------|-------------|
| **Culturally tailored outreach** | Adapt messaging and materials to community language, values, and preferences |
| **Navigation programs** | Patient navigators guide patients through screening, follow-up, and treatment |
| **Community health workers** | Trusted community members provide education and linkage to care |
| **Address structural barriers** | Transportation assistance, insurance enrollment, extended clinic hours |
| **Implicit bias training** | For all healthcare team members |
| **Telehealth integration** | Expand access for counseling and follow-up |
| **Sliding fee/financial assistance** | Reduce cost barriers for uninsured/underinsured |

### Rural Health Considerations

| Challenge | Strategy |
|-----------|----------|
| Fewer providers per capita | Telehealth; mobile screening units |
| Longer travel distances | Mobile mammography; FIT mailing programs |
| Limited specialty care | Teleradiology; e-consultation |
| Higher tobacco use and obesity | Targeted behavioral interventions |
| Later stage at diagnosis | Community-based screening events; patient navigation |

### Key Clinical Points
- Disparities are driven by structural racism, not biological differences
- Individual clinicians can mitigate disparities through equitable care practices
- Screening rates alone do not explain all disparities -- follow-up and treatment quality matter
- Advocate for policies that address social determinants of health

## 8.5 Health Literacy

### Overview
Health literacy -- the ability to obtain, process, and understand basic health information -- is essential for informed decision-making about preventive care. Approximately 36% of American adults have limited health literacy.

### Principles for Health-Literate Communication

| Principle | Application |
|-----------|------------|
| **Teach-back method** | Ask patient to explain back in their own words; confirms understanding |
| **Reading level** | Provide materials at 5th-6th grade reading level |
| **Visual aids** | Use pictures, diagrams, and multimedia resources |
| **Interpreter services** | Offer professional interpreters for non-English speakers; avoid family members |
| **Simplify language** | Avoid medical jargon; explain concepts in plain language |
| **Confirm understanding** | Before procedures, confirm understanding of risks and benefits |

### Universal Precautions Approach

| Element | Action |
|---------|--------|
| **Spoken communication** | Slow down; use plain language; limit to 3-5 key points per visit |
| **Written materials** | Short sentences; large font; relevant pictures; actionable steps |
| **Numeracy** | Use visual aids for numbers; avoid probabilities; use frequencies ("1 in 10") rather than percentages |
| **Navigation** | Clear wayfinding signage; appointment reminders in multiple formats |

### Key Clinical Points
- Limited health literacy is common across all education and income levels
- Shame often prevents patients from admitting they don't understand
- Teach-back is the most effective single intervention to ensure comprehension
- Health literacy is a stronger predictor of health status than age, income, employment status, education, or race
- Document health literacy status and communication preferences in the medical record

# Chapter 9: Occupational and Environmental Health

## 9.1 Overview

Occupational and environmental health encompasses the prevention, diagnosis, and management of conditions related to workplace exposures and environmental hazards. Clinicians play a critical role in identifying work-related and environment-related illness, providing anticipatory guidance, and advocating for patient protection.

## 9.2 Pre-Employment and Periodic Health Examinations

### Components of Occupational Health Evaluations

| Component | Content |
|-----------|---------|
| **Medical history** | Comprehensive medical history with attention to conditions relevant to job requirements |
| **Physical examination** | Targeted to job-specific requirements (e.g., musculoskeletal for physically demanding work) |

## 9.3 OSHA Requirements and Medical Surveillance

### Key OSHA Standards

| Standard (29 CFR) | Scope | Medical Requirements |
|-------------------|-------|---------------------|
| **1910.1030 (Bloodborne Pathogens)** | Healthcare and other at-risk workers | Hepatitis B vaccination offered; post-exposure follow-up protocol |
| **1910.134 (Respiratory Protection)** | Workers required to use respirators | Medical clearance evaluation; annual fit testing |
| **1910.1200 (Hazard Communication)** | All workplaces with hazardous chemicals | Right to know about chemical exposures; Safety Data Sheets (SDS) |
| **1910.132 (PPE)** | Workplaces requiring personal protective equipment | Employer-provided PPE at no cost to employee |
| **1904 (Recordkeeping)** | Most employers | Log of work-related injuries/illnesses (OSHA 300) |

### Medical Surveillance Programs

| Exposure | Program Components |
|----------|-------------------|
| **Asbestos** | Baseline and periodic chest X-ray, spirometry; removal from exposure if criteria exceeded |
| **Lead** | Baseline and periodic blood lead levels; medical removal protection |
| **Noise** | Baseline and annual audiometry; hearing conservation program at 85 dB action level |
| **Respiratory hazards** | Baseline and periodic spirometry; fit testing |
| **Cadmium, chromium, benzene** | Exposure-specific medical surveillance protocols |

### Key Clinical Points
- Clinicians providing occupational health services must understand both clinical and regulatory obligations
- Work-relatedness determination affects workers' compensation eligibility
- OSHA requires employers to provide medical surveillance at no cost to employees
- Physician opinions in OSHA contexts carry regulatory weight

## 9.4 Workers' Compensation

### Principles

| Element | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| **No-fault system** | Employee does not need to prove employer negligence; employer is liable for work-related injuries/illnesses |
| **Coverage** | Medical treatment and wage replacement for work-related conditions |
| **Physician role** | Determine causation (work-relatedness); establish treatment plan; set work restrictions; determine maximum medical improvement (MMI) |
| **Work restrictions** | Temporary or permanent limitations on work activities based on functional capacity |
| **Return-to-work** | Planning with appropriate restrictions; light duty when available |

### Return-to-Work Planning

| Phase | Approach |
|-------|----------|
| **Acute phase** | Light duty assignments during recovery; modified hours |
| **Recovery phase** | Graduated return-to-work programs; progressive increase in duties |
| **Chronic phase** | Functional capacity evaluations for permanent restrictions; ergonomic modifications |

### Key Clinical Points
- Document work-relatedness clearly and early
- Communicate work restrictions specifically (lifting limits, standing limits, etc.)
- Advocate for return-to-work; prolonged absence worsens outcomes
- Be aware of state-specific workers' compensation laws and requirements
- Maintain neutrality; avoid becoming an advocate for either employer or employee

## 9.5 Lead Screening and Management

### Overview
Lead toxicity remains a significant public health concern, particularly for children in older housing and adults with occupational exposures. There is no safe level of lead exposure. [^496^] [^500^] [^502^]

### Pediatric Screening

| Guideline | Recommendation |
|-----------|---------------|
| **CDC/AAP** | **Universal blood lead testing** at ages 12 and 24 months for Medicaid-enrolled children and children in high-prevalence areas |
| **USPSTF** | Evidence insufficient for routine screening of asymptomatic children at average risk (**Grade I**) |
| **Additional testing** | Test all children 36-72 months who have not been previously screened |

### Pediatric Risk Factors

| Category | Risk Indicators |
|----------|----------------|
| **Housing** | Built before 1960; chipped/peeling paint; recent renovation |
| **Behavioral** | Pica (eating non-food items) |
| **Social** | Sibling/playmate with elevated lead level |
| **Parental** | Parent exposed through work/hobbies (stained glass, pottery, firearms, construction) |
| **Environmental** | Living near lead-based industry |
| **Demographic** | Recent immigrant, refugee, internationally adopted child |

### Blood Lead Level Interpretation and Management

| Blood Lead Level | Action |
|-----------------|--------|
| **<3.5 ug/dL** | Current CDC reference level; reduce ongoing exposures |
| **3.5-9 ug/dL** | Identify and reduce exposure sources; follow-up testing in 1-3 months |
| **10-19 ug/dL** | Environmental investigation; nutritional counseling (iron, calcium); frequent follow-up |
| **20-44 ug/dL** | Environmental case management; chelation if symptomatic; comprehensive evaluation |
| **>=45 ug/dL** | **Chelation therapy indicated** |
| **>=70 ug/dL** | **Medical emergency** -- hospitalize for immediate chelation |

### Pregnant Women [^500^] [^506^]

| Parameter | Recommendation |
|-----------|---------------|
| **Screening** | Risk assessment at earliest prenatal contact; blood lead test if any risk factors present |
| **>=5 ug/dL confirmed** | Environmental health assessment |
| **>=10 ug/dL** | Remove from occupational lead exposure |
| **>=40 ug/dL** | Discontinue breastfeeding |
| **>=45 ug/dL** | Chelation therapy (use pregnancy-compatible agent) |

### Key Clinical Points
- The CDC reference level has been progressively lowered (currently 3.5 ug/dL); any detectable lead may cause harm
- Iron deficiency increases lead absorption; ensure adequate iron and calcium intake
- Lead exposure in pregnancy affects fetal neurodevelopment
- Occupational lead exposure (battery manufacturing, construction, smelting, firing ranges) requires regular monitoring
- Chelation therapy: EDTA for levels 45-69; dimercaprol + EDTA for >=70 or symptomatic encephalopathy

## 9.6 Radon Exposure

### Overview
Radon is a colorless, odorless radioactive gas produced by the natural decay of uranium in soil. It is the **second leading cause of lung cancer** after smoking and the leading cause among non-smokers.

### Key Facts
- Enters homes through cracks in foundation, gaps around pipes, construction joints
- Concentrated in basements and lower building levels
- Risk varies by geographic region (dependent on underlying geology)
- Smokers exposed to radon have exponentially higher lung cancer risk

### Screening and Action Levels

| Parameter | Guidance |
|-----------|----------|
| **Testing** | Test all homes below the third floor (DIY kits or professional measurement) |
| **EPA action level** | **4 pCi/L** (picocuries per liter) |
| **Action** | Fix home if radon level >=4 pCi/L |
| **Retesting** | Every 2 years; after any structural modifications |

### Mitigation Strategies

| Method | Description |
|--------|-------------|
| **Sub-slab depressurization** | Most common and effective method; ventilates radon from beneath foundation |
| **Sealing cracks** | Supplementary; alone insufficient for high levels |
| **Increased ventilation** | Supplementary approach |
| **Professional remediation** | Recommended for levels >=4 pCi/L; typically $800-$2,500 |

### Clinical Counseling Points
- Ask patients about home radon testing; recommend testing if not done
- Emphasize that radon is a fixable problem
- Higher priority for patients who smoke or have family history of lung cancer
- Information: epa.gov/radon

## 9.7 Air Quality and Health

### Health Effects of Air Pollutants

| Pollutant | Sources | Health Effects |
|-----------|---------|---------------|
| **PM2.5 (fine particles)** | Vehicles, industry, fires, cooking | Cardiovascular disease, respiratory disease, lung cancer; penetrates deep into lungs |
| **PM10 (coarse particles)** | Dust, pollen, road debris | Respiratory irritation; asthma exacerbation |
| **Ground-level ozone** | Vehicle exhaust, industrial emissions (photochemical reaction) | Asthma exacerbation, decreased lung function, airway inflammation |
| **Nitrogen dioxide (NO2)** | Vehicle exhaust, power plants | Respiratory irritation; asthma trigger |
| **Sulfur dioxide (SO2)** | Coal-fired power plants, industry | Bronchospasm; asthma exacerbation |
| **Carbon monoxide (CO)** | Combustion (vehicles, heaters, stoves) | Headache, dizziness, confusion; fatal at high levels |

### Air Quality Index (AQI) Clinical Guidance

| AQI Level | Health Concern | Guidance for At-Risk Patients |
|-----------|---------------|------------------------------|
| **0-50 (Green)** | Good | Normal outdoor activities |
| **51-100 (Yellow)** | Moderate | Unusually sensitive individuals: consider limiting prolonged outdoor exertion |
| **101-150 (Orange)** | Unhealthy for sensitive groups | Children, elderly, those with heart/lung disease: limit outdoor activities |
| **151-200 (Red)** | Unhealthy | Everyone: limit outdoor exertion; sensitive groups: avoid outdoor activities |
| **201-300 (Purple)** | Very unhealthy | Everyone: avoid outdoor exertion |
| **301+ (Maroon)** | Hazardous | Everyone: remain indoors; emergency conditions |

### Clinical Recommendations
- Monitor AQI for patients with asthma, COPD, heart disease, and older adults
- Advise at-risk patients to limit outdoor activity when AQI >100
- Recommend HEPA air filters for indoor use, especially bedroom
- Advise against outdoor exercise during high ozone days (typically hot, sunny afternoons)
- Encourage use of N95 masks during wildfire smoke events
- Advocate for pollution reduction policies at community level

## 9.8 Climate Health Impacts

### Overview
Climate change represents an escalating threat to public health through multiple pathways. Healthcare providers have a responsibility to understand these impacts, counsel patients, and advocate for mitigation and adaptation. [^537^]

### Health Impacts of Climate Change

| Category | Impact | Clinical Manifestations |
|----------|--------|------------------------|
| **Heat-related illness** | More frequent/severe heat waves | Heat exhaustion, heat stroke; exacerbation of cardiac and renal disease |
| **Respiratory disease** | Worsening air quality; increased pollen/allergens; wildfire smoke | Asthma exacerbations; COPD flares; allergic rhinitis |
| **Vector-borne disease** | Expanded geographic range of vectors | Lyme disease, West Nile virus, dengue, malaria in new regions |
| **Waterborne disease** | Flooding, compromised water systems | Gastrointestinal illness; waterborne pathogen outbreaks |
| **Mental health** | Climate anxiety, displacement trauma, disaster-related PTSD | Anxiety, depression, acute stress disorder, PTSD |
| **Food security** | Crop failures, disrupted distribution | Malnutrition; foodborne illness from unsafe food handling |
| **Extreme weather** | Hurricanes, floods, wildfires | Traumatic injuries; deaths; displacement; interruption of care |

### CDC BRACE Framework [^537^]
The Building Resilience Against Climate Effects framework:

1. **Anticipate** climate impacts and assess vulnerabilities
2. **Project** disease burden
3. **Assess** public health interventions
4. **Develop and implement** climate and health adaptation plan
5. **Evaluate** impact and improve quality

### Clinical Recommendations
- Screen vulnerable patients (elderly, chronic disease, outdoor workers) for heat and air quality risks
- Provide anticipatory guidance for extreme weather events (medication refills, emergency plans, cooling centers)
- Incorporate climate considerations into chronic disease management (heat- and ozone-sensitive medications)
- Support community resilience planning
- Advocate for climate mitigation and adaptation policies at institutional and community levels

### Key Climate Health Counseling Points

| Patient Population | Guidance |
|-------------------|----------|
| **Elderly patients** | Check cooling systems before summer; identify nearby cooling centers; review medication heat sensitivity |
| **Patients with asthma/COPD** | Monitor AQI and pollen counts; keep rescue inhalers accessible; have wildfire smoke action plan |
| **Outdoor workers** | Heat safety protocols; hydration; work-rest cycles; early symptom recognition |
| **Patients in flood-prone areas** | Emergency medication supply; waterproof medical records; evacuation plan |
| **All patients** | Discuss climate anxiety when present; validate concerns; provide coping strategies |

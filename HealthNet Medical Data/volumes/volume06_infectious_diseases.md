# Chapter 1: COVID-19

## 1.1 Overview

COVID-19 is caused by SARS-CoV-2, a single-stranded RNA betacoronavirus first identified in December 2019. As of 2024-2025, the disease has transitioned from pandemic to endemic status in most regions, with ongoing circulation of evolving Omicron sublineages. Clinical presentation ranges from asymptomatic infection to severe pneumonia, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), multisystem inflammatory syndrome (MIS-C/MIS-A), and death.

**Key Epidemiologic Facts:**
- Transmission occurs primarily via respiratory droplets and aerosols
- Incubation period: median 3-5 days (range 1-14 days)
- Infectious period: highest 1-2 days before symptom onset through first 5-7 days of illness
- Reinfection is common due to immune evasion by evolving variants

---

## 1.2 Current Variants (2024-2025)

SARS-CoV-2 continues to evolve through antigenic drift, with Omicron sublineages predominating globally:

| Variant/Lineage | Key Features | Clinical Impact |
|---|---|---|
| **JN.1** | Dominant global lineage from late 2023 into 2024 | Enhanced immune evasion; similar severity to prior Omicron subvariants |
| **KP.2, KP.3 (FLiRT variants)** | Mutations at F456L and R346T in spike protein | Increased prevalence through 2024; enhanced transmissibility |
| **XBB-derived variants** | Continued incremental antigenic drift | Ongoing circulation; accounted for in updated vaccines |

**Clinical Implications:** Variants demonstrate enhanced immune evasion, leading to more frequent reinfection, but severity in vaccinated populations remains generally mild-to-moderate for healthy individuals. Updated vaccines targeting recent lineages are released annually.

---

## 1.3 Diagnosis

### Molecular Testing (NAAT/PCR)
- **Gold standard:** Detects viral RNA with high sensitivity and specificity
- Can detect infection earlier and for longer than antigen tests
- Recommended for: symptomatic high-risk individuals, confirmation of negative antigen tests in symptomatic patients, pre-procedure screening
- Turnaround time varies from hours to days depending on laboratory

### Rapid Antigen Testing
- Detects viral nucleocapsid protein
- **Highest sensitivity:** When viral load peaks (typically first 5-7 days of symptoms)
- **Advantages:** Results in 15-30 minutes; widely available; lower cost
- **Limitations:** Lower sensitivity than PCR, especially in asymptomatic or early infection
- **Strategy:** If symptoms present but antigen test negative, confirm with PCR; serial antigen testing (48 hours apart) improves sensitivity

### Diagnostic Strategy
| Clinical Scenario | Recommended Test |
|---|---|
| Symptomatic, high-risk, needs treatment decision | NAAT (preferred) or antigen with confirmation if negative |
| Symptomatic, low-risk, community surge | Antigen test; confirm with NAAT if negative |
| Asymptomatic exposure | Antigen test at day 5; NAAT if high-risk |
| Pre-procedure screening | NAAT preferred |

---

## 1.4 Treatment of Outpatients

### High-Risk Criteria for Antiviral Treatment
Per CDC and IDSA guidelines, the following conditions increase risk of severe disease and warrant treatment [^22^]:

- Age >=65 years
- Chronic lung, heart, kidney, or liver disease
- Diabetes mellitus
- Immunocompromised state
- Obesity (BMI >=30, or >=25 in some guidelines)
- Pregnancy
- Sickle cell disease
- Neurodevelopmental disorders

### Outpatient Antiviral Options

| Treatment | Population | Route | Duration | Time Window | Key Considerations |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Nirmatrelvir-Ritonavir (Paxlovid)** | Adults; children >=12 years and >=40 kg | Oral | 5 days | <=5 days from onset | **Preferred first-line**; significant drug-drug interactions; renal dose adjustment |
| **Remdesivir (Veklury)** | Adults; children >=28 days and >=3 kg | IV | 3 days | <=7 days from onset | Requires infusion; preferred when Paxlovid contraindicated |
| **Molnupiravir (Lagevrio)** | Adults only | Oral | 5 days | <=5 days from onset | Lower efficacy; avoid in pregnancy; contraception required |

### Nirmatrelvir-Ritonavir (Paxlovid) - Detailed

**Efficacy:** Reduced risk of hospitalization and death by approximately 87% in unvaccinated high-risk outpatients; real-world studies confirm effectiveness in the Omicron era [^22^].

**Standard Dosing:** 300mg nirmatrelvir + 100mg ritonavir orally twice daily for 5 days.

**Renal Dose Adjustment:**
| eGFR | Dosing |
|---|---|
| >=60 mL/min | 300mg nirmatrelvir + 100mg ritonavir BID |
| 30-59 mL/min | 150mg nirmatrelvir + 100mg ritonavir BID |
| <30 mL/min | Not recommended |

**Drug Interactions (Critical):**
Ritonavir is a strong CYP3A inhibitor. Significant interactions include:
- **Contraindicated:** Some antiarrhythmics, ergot derivatives, certain sedatives (triazolam, midazolam oral), simvastatin, lovastatin, rifampin, St. John's Wort
- **Require dose adjustment/hold:** Most statins, immunosuppressants (tacrolimus, cyclosporine), anticoagulants (rivaroxaban, apixaban), some anticonvulsants
- **Always perform comprehensive medication review before prescribing**

### Remdesivir - Second-Line Preferred
- 87% reduction in hospitalization/death in high-risk outpatients
- Requires IV infusion over 3 consecutive days (30-120 minutes each)
- Check liver function and PT before initiation
- Preferred option when Paxlovid is contraindicated due to drug interactions

### Molnupiravir - Alternative Only
- Lower efficacy in clinical trials; limited evidence in vaccinated individuals
- Use only when Paxlovid and remdesivir are unavailable or inappropriate
- Avoid in pregnancy; women of childbearing potential must use contraception

## 1.5 Long COVID (Post-COVID-19 Condition)

### Definition
Post-COVID-19 condition (long COVID) is broadly defined as symptoms persisting >=3 months after acute SARS-CoV-2 infection, lasting at least 2 months, without alternative explanation (WHO definition) [^263^].

### Epidemiology
- Approximately 15% of people still have symptoms at 12 months per global estimates
- More common after severe acute illness but can occur after mild infection
- **Risk factors:** Female sex, older age, pre-existing conditions, severe acute infection

### Common Manifestations and Management

| Symptom Domain | Management Approach | Evidence Level |
|---|---|---|
| **Fatigue** (most common) | Rehabilitation therapy, graded exercise, energy conservation; rule out other causes | Moderate |
| **Cognitive impairment ("brain fog")** | Screen objectively; treat sleep disorders, mood, pain; neuropsychological referral | Moderate |
| **Dyspnea/exercise intolerance** | Respiratory rehabilitation, inspiratory muscle training | Moderate |
| **Anosmia/dysgeusia** | Olfactory training (strongly recommended, Grade A); intranasal corticosteroids may be considered [^79^] | High |
| **POTS** | Non-pharmacologic first (fluids 2-3L/day, NaCl 10-12g/day, compression stockings); beta-blockers, ivabradine, midodrine, fludrocortisone | Moderate |
| **Headache** | Symptomatic treatment; migraine prophylaxis if migraine-like; monitor for medication overuse | Low-Moderate |
| **Psychiatric symptoms** | SSRIs for depression/anxiety; psychiatric referral if severe | Moderate |
| **Musculoskeletal pain** | NSAIDs, physical therapy, graded activity | Low |

### Key Points
- Vaccination reduces (but does not eliminate) risk of long COVID
- Each reinfection carries risk of developing or worsening long COVID
- No single diagnostic test; exclusion of alternative etiologies is essential
- Multidisciplinary care is often needed

---

## 1.6 Vaccination (2024-2025)

Per CDC ACIP recommendations [^274^][^295^][^298^]:

| Population | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| **Adults 19-64 (not immunocompromised)** | 1 dose of 2024-2025 vaccine (Moderna, Pfizer, or Novavax); unvaccinated may need 2 doses |
| **Adults >=65 years** | 2 doses of 2024-2025 vaccine, 6 months apart (minimum 2 months) |
| **Immunocompromised** | 3-dose initial series; additional booster doses per shared clinical decision |
| **Pregnancy** | Recommended; any trimester |

**Key Notes:**
- 2024-2025 vaccines are updated formulations targeting XBB.1.5 or JN.1 lineages
- Multiple platforms available: mRNA (Moderna, Pfizer), protein subunit (Novavax)
- Vaccination is based on shared clinical decision-making for most adults
- Highest benefit for those at increased risk of severe COVID-19

---

## 1.7 Red Flags - Emergency Features

**[EMERGENCY]** The following require immediate emergency department evaluation:

- Oxygen saturation <94% on room air
- Severe dyspnea, respiratory rate >=30/min
- Chest pain or hemoptysis
- Altered mental status or new confusion
- Signs of septic shock (hypotension, poor perfusion, cold extremities)
- Cyanosis
- Signs of MIS-A in adults (multi-organ involvement after acute infection)

# Chapter 2: Influenza and Other Respiratory Viruses

## 2.1 Overview

Influenza is an acute respiratory illness caused by influenza A and B viruses. Seasonal epidemics occur annually, typically October through May in the Northern Hemisphere. Complications include primary influenza pneumonia, secondary bacterial pneumonia (most commonly *S. aureus* or *S. pneumoniae*), myocarditis, encephalitis, and exacerbation of chronic cardiopulmonary conditions. RSV causes significant morbidity in infants and older adults.

---

## 2.2 Diagnosis

| Test Type | Sensitivity | Specificity | Turnaround | Best Use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **RT-PCR** | >95% | >99% | 1-24 hours | Gold standard; hospitalized patients |
| **Rapid antigen (RIDT)** | 50-70% | 90-95% | 10-15 minutes | Point-of-care; community outbreaks |
| **Clinical diagnosis** | Variable | Variable | Immediate | During community outbreaks when testing unavailable |

**Symptom Clues for Influenza:** Abrupt onset of fever, myalgia, headache, dry cough, sore throat; gastrointestinal symptoms more common in children than adults.

---

## 2.3 Antiviral Treatment

Per CDC recommendations [^259^][^260^]:

| Antiviral | Age | Route | Duration | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Oseltamivir (Tamiflu)** | >=14 days | Oral | 5 days (BID) | Generic; renal dose adjustment; most widely used |
| **Zanamivir (Relenza)** | >=7 years | Inhaled | 5 days (BID) | Avoid in asthma/COPD; allergy to lactose/milk protein |
| **Peramivir (Rapivab)** | >=6 months | IV (single dose) | Single dose | For patients unable to take oral meds; renal adjustment |
| **Baloxavir (Xofluza)** | >=5 years | Oral | **Single dose** | Convenient; not recommended in pregnancy/breastfeeding or complicated illness |

### Treatment Principles
- **Start as early as possible** - ideally within 48 hours of symptom onset
- Treatment initiated >48 hours may still benefit severely ill or immunocompromised patients
- **Empiric treatment should not wait for laboratory confirmation in high-risk patients**

### Oseltamivir Dosing
| Population | Dose |
|---|---|
| Adults | 75 mg orally BID x 5 days |
| Children >1 year (weight-based) | <=15 kg: 30 mg BID; >15-23 kg: 45 mg BID; >23-40 kg: 60 mg BID; >40 kg: 75 mg BID |
| Renal impairment (CrCl 10-30) | 75 mg once daily x 5 days |
| Post-exposure prophylaxis | 75 mg once daily x 7-10 days |

### Post-Exposure Prophylaxis
- **Oseltamivir:** 75 mg once daily for 7 days after last exposure
- **Zanamivir:** 10 mg once daily for 7 days
- **Baloxavir:** Single dose for post-exposure prophylaxis (approved >=5 years)
- Outbreak control in institutions: minimum 2 weeks, continue until 1 week after last known case

---

## 2.4 High-Risk Groups

Priority groups for treatment AND vaccination [^259^]:

- Adults >=65 years
- Children <2 years (highest risk <6 months)
- Chronic pulmonary, cardiovascular (excluding isolated hypertension), renal, hepatic, neurologic, metabolic, or hematologic conditions
- Immunosuppressed persons
- Pregnant women and women up to 2 weeks postpartum
- Persons <19 years receiving long-term aspirin therapy
- Residents of nursing homes and long-term care facilities
- American Indians and Alaska Natives
- Morbid obesity (BMI >=40)

---

## 2.5 Respiratory Syncytial Virus (RSV)

### Clinical Features
- Leading cause of bronchiolitis and pneumonia in infants; increasingly recognized in older adults
- Incubation: 4-6 days; contagious for 3-8 days (longer in immunocompromised)
- Symptoms: Rhinorrhea, cough, wheezing, fever; progression to respiratory distress in severe cases

### RSV in Older Adults
- Causes substantial morbidity and mortality in adults >=60 years
- Can exacerbate COPD, asthma, and heart failure
- New vaccines available for adults >=60

### RSV Prevention
| Product | Population | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Nirsevimab (Beyfortus)** | Infants (seasonal) | Monoclonal antibody; single dose for first RSV season |
| **RSVpreF (Abrysvo)** | Adults >=60; pregnancy (32-36 weeks) | Shared clinical decision for 60-74; recommended >=75 |
| **RSVpreF (Abrysvo)** | Pregnancy | Administered 32-36 weeks gestation to protect infant |

---

## 2.6 Vaccination

- **Annual vaccination recommended** for all persons >=6 months without contraindications
- **High-dose or adjuvanted vaccines** (HD-IIV3, aIIV3, RIIV) **preferred** for adults >=65 years
- **Contraindications:** Severe allergic reaction to prior influenza vaccine or component (egg allergy is NOT a contraindication - may receive any age-appropriate vaccine); history of Guillain-Barre syndrome within 6 weeks of prior influenza vaccine (relative contraindication)
- **LAIV (live attenuated):** Not for immunocompromised, pregnant, children 2-4 years with asthma/wheezing, or close contacts of severely immunocompromised

---

## 2.7 Complications of Influenza

| Complication | Key Features | Management |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary influenza pneumonia** | Rapid progression, bilateral infiltrates, hypoxemia | Supportive care; antivirals; often requires ICU |
| **Secondary bacterial pneumonia** | Fever returns after initial improvement; *S. aureus* (including MRSA) or *S. pneumoniae* | Add empiric antibiotics; cover MRSA if risk |
| **Myocarditis** | Chest pain, arrhythmias, heart failure, elevated troponins | Supportive; cardiology referral |
| **Encephalopathy/Guillain-Barre** | Altered mental status, seizures, ascending paralysis | Neurology referral; supportive |

---

## 2.8 Red Flags

**[EMERGENCY]**
- Dyspnea or hypoxia
- Altered mental status
- Severe dehydration
- Persistent high fever >3 days
- Chest pain
- Severe weakness in children
- Recurrent fever after initial improvement (possible secondary bacterial infection)

# Chapter 3: Tuberculosis

## 3.1 Overview

Tuberculosis (TB) is caused by *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*, an acid-fast bacillus. It remains one of the world's deadliest infectious diseases. Approximately 25% of the world's population has latent TB infection (LTBI), with a 5-10% lifetime risk of progression to active disease. The risk is highest in the first 2 years after infection and in immunocompromised individuals.

**Pathophysiology:** Inhalation of droplet nuclei leads to infection of alveolar macrophages. Most immunocompetent individuals contain the infection (latent TB), while those with impaired immunity may develop progressive primary disease or reactivation.

---

## 3.2 Latent TB vs Active TB Disease

| Feature | Latent TB Infection (LTBI) | Active TB Disease |
|---|---|---|
| **Symptoms** | Asymptomatic | Cough >=2-3 weeks, hemoptysis, fever, night sweats, weight loss, fatigue |
| **Chest X-ray** | Usually normal or calcified granuloma | Infiltrates, cavities, nodules, lymphadenopathy |
| **Sputum smear** | Negative | May be positive |
| **Sputum culture** | Negative | Positive |
| **NAAT** | Negative | Positive |
| **Infectious** | No | Yes (if pulmonary) |
| **Treatment** | LTBI regimen (3-4 months preferred) | Full TB treatment (4-6 months) |

---

## 3.3 Diagnosis

### Tuberculin Skin Test (TST/Mantoux)
- Intradermal injection of purified protein derivative (PPD); read induration at 48-72 hours
- **Interpretation cutoffs:**

| Induration | Population |
|---|---|
| >=5 mm | HIV-positive, recent TB contacts, fibrotic changes on CXR, organ transplant recipients, immunosuppressed |
| >=10 mm | Recent immigrants from high-prevalence countries, injection drug users, residents/employees of congregate settings, mycobacteriology lab personnel, persons with risk-increasing clinical conditions, children <4 years |
| >=15 mm | Persons with no known risk factors |

- **Limitations:** False positives from BCG vaccination or non-tuberculous mycobacteria; requires return visit; operator-dependent interpretation

### Interferon-Gamma Release Assays (IGRA)
- Blood tests (T-SPOT.TB, QuantiFERON-TB Gold Plus) measuring IFN-gamma response to *M. tuberculosis*-specific antigens (ESAT-6, CFP-10)
- **Not affected by BCG vaccination**; only one visit required
- **Preferred in:** BCG-vaccinated individuals, those unlikely to return for TST reading, immunocompromised patients [^302^]
- **Limitations:** More expensive; processing requirements; less data in very young children; indeterminate results in immunosuppression

### TST vs IGRA Comparative Performance [^302^]
| Parameter | TST | IGRA |
|---|---|---|
| Sensitivity | Moderate | Higher (especially T-SPOT.TB) |
| Specificity | Lower (BCG cross-reactivity) | Higher |
| Immunocompromised | Less reliable | Preferred |
| Pediatric | Higher sensitivity, lower specificity | Variable performance |
| Cost | Lower | Higher |
| Visit requirement | Two visits | One visit |

---

## 3.4 LTBI Treatment Regimens

Per CDC 2024-2025 guidelines, **short-course rifamycin-based 3- or 4-month regimens are preferentially recommended** over 6- or 9-month isoniazid monotherapy due to higher completion rates [^262^]:

| Regimen | Duration | Drugs | Frequency | Key Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **3HP** | 3 months | Isoniazid + Rifapentine | Once weekly | Age >=2 years; DOT preferred; not for pregnancy or HIV on interacting ART |
| **4R** | 4 months | Rifampin | Daily | All ages HIV-negative; good for INH-resistant contacts |
| **3HR** | 3 months | Isoniazid + Rifampin | Daily | All ages HIV-negative; avoid with some ART |
| **6H** | 6 months | Isoniazid | Daily | Alternative when rifamycins contraindicated |
| **9H** | 9 months | Isoniazid | Daily | Least preferred due to duration and adherence |

**Essential Monitoring:**
- All patients on isoniazid should receive pyridoxine (vitamin B6) 25-50 mg daily to prevent peripheral neuropathy
- Rifamycin drug-drug interactions are common; review ALL medications (warfarin, oral contraceptives, azole antifungals, many antiretrovirals)
- Women using hormonal contraception should add barrier method during rifamycin therapy
- Baseline and periodic liver function testing; counsel patients on hepatotoxicity symptoms

---

## 3.5 Active TB Treatment (Drug-Susceptible)

### Standard RIPE Therapy
| Phase | Duration | Regimen |
|---|---|---|
| **Intensive phase** | 2 months | Isoniazid (H) + Rifampin (R) + Pyrazinamide (Z) + Ethambutol (E) |
| **Continuation phase** | 4 months | Isoniazid (H) + Rifampin (R) |
| **Total** | **6 months** | For most pulmonary TB (drug-susceptible) |

- **DOT (directly observed therapy)** strongly recommended to ensure adherence
- Pyridoxine 25-50 mg daily with isoniazid
- Monthly clinical monitoring; sputum smear/culture at baseline and monthly until conversion

### Updated 4-Month Regimen [^261^]
- A 4-month regimen of isoniazid + rifapentine + moxifloxacin + pyrazinamide is now conditionally recommended for adults >=12 years with drug-susceptible pulmonary TB
- Nonsevere TB in children: 4 months vs 6 months of standard therapy both acceptable

### Extrapulmonary TB
| Site | Duration | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Lymph node TB | 6 months | Standard regimen |
| Pleural TB | 6 months | Add corticosteroids if significant symptoms |
| Bone/joint TB | 6-9 months | Surgical debridement may be needed |
| CNS TB (meningitis) | 9-12 months | Include pyrazinamide + ethionamide; corticosteroids recommended |
| Miliary TB | 6-9 months | Often requires ICU care |

---

## 3.6 Drug-Resistant TB [^261^]

### Rifampin-Resistant, Fluoroquinolone-Susceptible
- **BPaLM regimen:** Bedaquiline + Pretomanid + Linezolid + Moxifloxacin for **6 months**
- Strong recommendation over 15-month or longer regimens
- Represents a paradigm shift in treatment shortening

### Rifampin-Resistant, Fluoroquinolone-Resistant
- **BPaL regimen:** Bedaquiline + Pretomanid + Linezolid for 6 months
- Linezolid preferred at 600 mg daily; therapeutic drug monitoring recommended (trough <2 mcg/mL)

### Monitoring for BPaLM Regimens
- Baseline and monthly: ECG (QTc prolongation risk), CBC, liver enzymes, bilirubin
- Monitor for linezolid toxicity (myelosuppression, peripheral neuropathy)
- Sputum culture monthly until negative

---

## 3.7 Screening in High-Risk Populations

TB screening is recommended for:
- Persons with HIV infection
- Recent immigrants from high-prevalence countries
- Residents/employees of correctional facilities, homeless shelters, long-term care
- Healthcare workers
- Contacts of active TB cases
- Persons initiating immunosuppressive therapy (especially anti-TNF agents, corticosteroids)
- Persons with silicosis, diabetes, chronic kidney disease on dialysis
- Persons with fibrotic changes on chest imaging consistent with prior TB

---

## 3.8 Red Flags

**[EMERGENCY]**
- Hemoptysis (massive or recurrent)
- Severe respiratory distress
- Altered mental status (possible CNS TB)
- Military TB pattern on imaging
- Suspected drug resistance or treatment failure
- Pregnant women with suspected active TB (urgent treatment needed)

# Chapter 4: HIV/AIDS

## 4.1 Overview

Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) targets CD4 T-lymphocytes through the CD4 receptor and CCR5/CXCR4 co-receptors, leading to progressive immunodeficiency if untreated. With modern antiretroviral therapy (ART), HIV is a manageable chronic condition with near-normal life expectancy. An estimated 39 million people are living with HIV globally.

---

## 4.2 Screening and Diagnosis

### 4th Generation HIV Test [^296^]
- **Standard of care:** Detects HIV-1/2 antibodies AND p24 antigen simultaneously
- **Window period:** p24 antigen detectable as early as 14 days post-exposure
- **Advantage over 3rd generation:** Identifies approximately 28% more infections; can differentiate HIV-1 from HIV-2
- **Positive patterns:**
  - Antibody positive / p24 positive: Established HIV infection
  - Antibody negative / p24 positive: **Acute HIV infection** - requires HIV RNA confirmation
  - Antibody positive / p24 negative: Possible HIV-2 or very late-stage HIV-1
- **Conclusive negative:** Test >3 months after last potential exposure is conclusive

### HIV RNA (Viral Load)
- Detectable as early as 5-10 days post-transmission
- **Initial test of choice** if acute HIV infection suspected (<14 days from exposure)
- Also used for indeterminate 4th generation results

### Screening Recommendations
- **Universal screening:** All adults aged 13-64 years at least once (USPSTF Grade A)
- **Annual screening:** Persons at ongoing risk (MSM, injection drug users, persons with multiple partners)
- **Pregnancy:** Universal screening at first prenatal visit; repeat in third trimester if at high risk
- **Pre-procedure:** Some settings require testing

---

## 4.3 PrEP (Pre-Exposure Prophylaxis)

### Available Regimens [^264^][^265^][^268^][^270^]

| Regimen | Agent | Dosing | Route | Efficacy |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **TDF/FTC** (Truvada) | Tenofovir disoproxil fumarate/emtricitabine | Daily oral | Oral | ~99% risk reduction (sexual transmission) |
| **TAF/FTC** (Descovy) | Tenofovir alafenamide/emtricitabine | Daily oral | Oral | ~99% (sexual transmission only; NOT for receptive vaginal sex) |
| **CAB-LA** (Apretude) | Long-acting cabotegravir | Every 2 months | IM injection | ~99% (sexual transmission) |
| **LEN** | Long-acting lenacapavir | Every 6 months | SC injection | 96-100% (FDA approved June 2025) [^265^] |

### PrEP Selection Guidance
- **All sexually active persons without HIV at substantial risk should be offered PrEP**
- Injectable options (CAB-LA, LEN) preferred for those with adherence concerns
- TDF/FTC is the most widely studied and approved for all populations **including pregnancy/breastfeeding**
- TAF/FTC **NOT recommended** for persons likely to get HIV through receptive vaginal sex
- LEN and CAB-LA approved for pregnancy/breastfeeding (LEN: AI recommendation; CAB: BII recommendation) [^270^]

### PrEP Monitoring
| Parameter | Frequency |
|---|---|
| HIV testing | Every 2-3 months (depending on regimen) |
| Renal function | Every 6 months (for TDF-containing regimens) |
| STI screening | At each follow-up visit |
| Hepatitis B status | At baseline (TDF/TAF treat HBV; do not discontinue if co-infected) |
| Pregnancy test | If applicable |

---

## 4.4 PEP (Post-Exposure Prophylaxis)

Updated 2025 CDC Guidelines [^264^]:

| Parameter | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| **Preferred regimen** | Bictegravir/TAF/FTC (Biktarvy) - single pill, once daily |
| **Alternative preferred** | TDF/FTC + Dolutegravir (DTG) |
| **Duration** | 28 days |
| **Timing** | Start as soon as possible, ideally within 2 hours, **no later than 72 hours** |
| **Completion rates** | >90% with BIC/TAF/FTC (higher than historical rates) |

**Key Points:**
- Efficacy decreases with delayed initiation; do not start >72 hours post-exposure
- BIC/TAF/FTC offers advantages: single tablet, once daily, well-tolerated, fewer drug interactions than older regimens
- Source patient testing should be obtained when possible
- Follow-up HIV testing at baseline, 4-6 weeks, and 3 months post-exposure

---

## 4.5 ART Treatment

### Preferred Initial Regimens

**Integrase strand transfer inhibitors (INSTIs)** are preferred first-line due to rapid viral suppression, high barrier to resistance, and favorable side effect profile:

| Regimen | Components | Advantages | Considerations |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Bictegravir/TAF/FTC** (Biktarvy) | Single tablet, once daily | High efficacy, excellent tolerability, few interactions | Contains TAF |
| **Dolutegravir + TAF/FTC or TDF/FTC** | Two pills | High barrier to resistance, well-studied | Renal considerations with TDF |
| **Dolutegravir/Lamivudine** (Dovato) | Two-drug regimen | Simplified; fewer drugs | Requires baseline viral load <500,000; HBV screening required |

### Treatment Goals
- Viral suppression to **<50 copies/mL** within 3-6 months of therapy initiation
- CD4 count recovery (may take 6-12+ months)
- Lifelong therapy; no current strategy for safe treatment interruption

### Monitoring on ART
| Parameter | Frequency |
|---|---|
| Viral load | Every 3-6 months until suppressed; then every 6 months |
| CD4 count | Every 3-6 months initially; may space once suppressed |
| Resistance testing | At baseline (if treatment-naive); with virologic failure |
| Renal function | Every 3-6 months (more often with TDF) |
| Liver function | Every 3-6 months |

---

## 4.6 U=U (Undetectable = Untransmittable)

- Persons with HIV who maintain **undetectable viral load (<200 copies/mL)** for at least 6 months **do not sexually transmit HIV**
- Supported by evidence from HPTN 052, PARTNER, PARTNER 2, and Opposites Attract studies
- Viral load testing every 3-6 months essential
- **Does NOT apply** to perinatal or bloodborne transmission
- Critical message for patient counseling and reducing HIV stigma

---

## 4.7 Opportunistic Infection Prophylaxis

### Primary Prophylaxis by CD4 Count

| CD4 Count | Risk | Prophylaxis |
|---|---|---|
| **<200 cells/mcL** | PCP (*Pneumocystis jirovecii* pneumonia) | TMP-SMX DS 1 tablet daily or 3x/week |
| **<100 cells/mcL** | Toxoplasmosis | TMP-SMX DS daily (if Toxoplasma IgG positive) |
| **<50 cells/mcL** | Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC) | Azithromycin 1200 mg weekly |
| **<150 cells/mcL** | Histoplasmosis, Coccidioidomycosis | Consider in endemic areas |
| **Any** | TB | Treat LTBI if indicated |

- Discontinue prophylaxis after CD4 rises above threshold for >3-6 months on ART with viral suppression

---

## 4.8 Special Populations

### Pregnancy
- All pregnant women with HIV should **initiate ART regardless of CD4 count**
- Goal: undetectable viral load by delivery
- Preferred: INSTI-based regimens with good placental transfer (e.g., dolutegravir)
- Intravenous zidovudine during labor if viral load >1000 copies/mL near delivery
- **Breastfeeding NOT recommended** in US/Europe settings (formula feeding preferred)

### Pediatrics
- Children <15 kg need liquid formulations
- Dolutegravir preferred for children (palatable, well-tolerated, high barrier to resistance)

### Geriatrics
- Higher pill burden considerations; drug interaction screening critical
- Comorbidity management (cardiovascular, bone health, renal function)

---

## 4.9 Red Flags

**[EMERGENCY]**
- Acute retroviral syndrome (fever, pharyngitis, rash, lymphadenopathy) - high viral load, highly infectious
- Opportunistic infection symptoms in known HIV:
  - Dyspnea, dry cough, hypoxia (PCP)
  - Visual changes, floaters (CMV retinitis)
  - Headache, focal neurologic deficits (toxoplasmosis, PML, cryptococcal meningitis)
- New-onset seizure, altered mental status
- Suspected IRIS (immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome) after ART initiation


---

# Chapter 5: Sexually Transmitted Infections

## 5.1 Overview

Sexually transmitted infections (STIs) represent a significant global health burden with increasing incidence rates in many regions. The emergence of antimicrobial resistance (AMR), particularly in *Neisseria gonorrhoeae*, has made appropriate diagnosis and treatment increasingly complex. This chapter covers the most common bacterial and viral STIs encountered in clinical practice.

---

## 5.2 Chlamydia (*Chlamydia trachomatis*)

### Diagnosis
- **NAAT (nucleic acid amplification test)** - gold standard
- Can test urine (first-catch), vaginal/cervical swab, rectal swab, pharyngeal swab
- **Screening recommendations:** Sexually active women <25 years; older women with risk factors; all pregnant women; MSM annually

### Treatment [^280^]
| Regimen | Details | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **First-line: Doxycycline** | 100 mg orally BID x 7 days | Superior microbiologic cure, especially for rectal infections |
| **Alternative: Azithromycin** | 1 g orally single dose | Preferred in pregnancy; may be used if doxycycline contraindicated |

- **Test of cure:** Recommended at 3 months (reinfection is common)
- Treat all sexual partners from preceding 60 days
- Screen for other STIs (gonorrhea, syphilis, HIV)

### Complications (if untreated)
- Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) in women
- Epididymitis in men
- Reactive arthritis
- Infertility (tubal scarring)

---

## 5.3 Gonorrhea (*Neisseria gonorrhoeae*)

### Diagnosis
- NAAT from urine, cervical/vaginal, rectal, or pharyngeal specimens
- **Culture with antimicrobial susceptibility testing** if treatment failure suspected

### Treatment [^283^]
| Infection Site | Regimen |
|---|---|
| **Uncomplicated urogenital, anorectal, or pharyngeal** | Ceftriaxone 500 mg IM single dose (<150 kg); **1 g IM if >=150 kg** |
| **Concurrent chlamydia treatment** | Doxycycline 100 mg BID x 7 days (if not excluded) |
| **Cephalosporin allergy (IgE-mediated)** | Gentamicin 240 mg IM + Azithromycin 2 g orally (single dose each) |

**Critical Notes:**
- **Always treat for chlamydia concurrently** if not excluded
- **No reliable oral alternatives** for pharyngeal infection; consult infectious disease if cephalosporin allergy
- **Test of cure required** for all pharyngeal infections at 7-14 days (culture preferred)
- Retest at 3 months due to high reinfection rates

### Antimicrobial Resistance (AMR) Concerns
- **Fluoroquinolone resistance** is widespread in the US; fluoroquinolones not recommended since 2007
- **Cephalosporins remain the only reliable class**; monitor MIC trends closely
- Suspected treatment failure: retreat with ceftriaxone, obtain culture with susceptibility testing, report to CDC
- Gentamicin + azithromycin alternative has lower efficacy for pharyngeal infection

---

## 5.4 Syphilis (*Treponema pallidum*)

### Stages and Treatment [^280^]

| Stage | Clinical Features | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary** | Painless chancre at inoculation site; regional lymphadenopathy | Benzathine penicillin G 2.4 million units IM single dose |
| **Secondary** | Diffuse rash (palms/soles), mucous patches, condyloma lata, generalized lymphadenopathy, fever | Benzathine penicillin G 2.4 million units IM single dose |
| **Early latent** | Asymptomatic; <1 year duration | Benzathine penicillin G 2.4 million units IM single dose |
| **Late latent** | Asymptomatic; >1 year or unknown duration | Benzathine penicillin G 2.4 million units IM **weekly x 3 doses** |
| **Tertiary (gummatous)** | Gummas, cardiovascular involvement, neurologic disease | Benzathine penicillin G 2.4 million units IM **weekly x 3 doses** |
| **Neurosyphilis** | Cranial nerve deficits, meningitis, stroke, dementia, tabes dorsalis | Aqueous crystalline penicillin G 3-4 million units IV q4h x 10-14 days; OR procaine penicillin G 2.4 million units IM daily + probenecid 500 mg QID x 10-14 days |
| **Ocular/otosyphilis** | Vision changes, hearing loss, tinnitus, uveitis | Same as neurosyphilis |

### Key Management Points
- **Penicillin is the only therapy with documented efficacy for neurosyphilis and in pregnancy**
- If penicillin-allergic (non-pregnant): Doxycycline 100 mg BID x 14 days (early) or x 28 days (late)
- **Pregnant patients MUST receive penicillin; desensitization if allergic**
- **Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction:** Acute febrile reaction within 24 hours of treatment; NOT an allergy; manage with antipyretics; counsel patients
- Follow RPR/VDRL titers at 6, 12, and 24 months; expect 4-fold decline by 12 months

### Diagnostic Testing
- **Non-treponemal tests:** RPR, VDRL (used for screening and monitoring treatment response)
- **Treponemal tests:** FTA-ABS, TP-PA, EIA/CLIA (confirmatory; remain positive for life)
- Reverse sequence screening (treponemal first) is common in automated laboratories

---

## 5.5 Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (PID)

### Diagnosis
- **Minimum criteria:** Pelvic/abdominal pain **PLUS** one of: cervical motion tenderness, uterine tenderness, or adnexal tenderness
- **Additional criteria:** Fever >38.3 C, abnormal mucopurulent discharge, elevated ESR/CRP, documented cervical infection

### Treatment (2024 Guidelines) [^287^][^285^][^291^]

#### Outpatient Regimen
| Drug | Dose | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| Ceftriaxone | 500 mg IM (1 g if >=150 kg) | Single dose |
| Doxycycline | 100 mg orally BID | 14 days |
| Metronidazole | 500 mg orally BID | 14 days |

- **72-hour follow-up is mandatory** to assess clinical response

#### Inpatient Regimen
- Ceftriaxone 1 g IV daily + Doxycycline 100 mg oral/IV BID + Metronidazole 500 mg oral/IV BID
- Continue IV until 24 hours after clinical improvement, then oral to complete 14 days

#### Admission Criteria
- Surgical emergency cannot be excluded
- Tubo-ovarian abscess
- Pregnancy
- Severe illness, vomiting, temperature >38.5 C
- Unable to tolerate oral therapy
- No clinical response to outpatient therapy

**RCT Evidence:** Metronidazole should be routinely added to ceftriaxone + doxycycline; it reduces endometrial anaerobes, decreases *M. genitalium*, and reduces pelvic tenderness compared to ceftriaxone + doxycycline alone [^287^].

---

## 5.6 Trichomoniasis (*Trichomonas vaginalis*)

### Diagnosis
- **NAAT (preferred)** - highest sensitivity
- Wet mount: 51-65% sensitivity; requires immediate examination
- Culture; rapid antigen tests

### Treatment
| Regimen | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Metronidazole** 2 g orally single dose | Traditional regimen |
| **Metronidazole** 500 mg BID x 7 days | May have higher cure rate than single dose |
| **Tinidazole** 2 g orally single dose | Alternative |

- **Treat sexual partners** simultaneously
- Test of cure not routine except in pregnancy (retest at 3 months)

---

## 5.7 Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV)

### Treatment [^297^]

| Indication | Regimen |
|---|---|
| **First episode (genital)** | Acyclovir 400 mg TID x 7-10 days OR Valacyclovir 1 g BID x 7-10 days |
| **Recurrent episode** | Acyclovir 800 mg BID x 5 days OR Valacyclovir 500 mg BID x 3 days (start within 48 hours) |
| **Suppressive therapy** | Acyclovir 400 mg BID OR Valacyclovir 500 mg-1 g daily OR Famciclovir 250 mg BID |

### Suppressive Therapy Indications
- Frequent recurrences (>=6/year)
- Severe symptoms
- To reduce transmission to discordant partner (reduces but does not eliminate risk)
- Pregnancy: suppressive therapy from 36 weeks to reduce need for cesarean delivery

### Key Points
- HSV-1 is increasingly common cause of genital herpes (oral-genital contact)
- Counsel on lifelong nature of infection, asymptomatic shedding, and transmission risk
- Partner notification; screen for other STIs

---

## 5.8 Human Papillomavirus (HPV) Vaccination [^292^]

| Parameter | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| **Vaccine** | 9-valent (Gardasil 9): types 6, 11, 16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52, 58 |
| **Routine age** | 11-12 years (can start at age 9) |
| **Catch-up** | Through age 26 years for all |
| **Ages 27-45** | Through shared clinical decision-making |
| **Dosing (start <15 years)** | 2 doses (0, 6-12 months) |
| **Dosing (start >=15 years or immunocompromised)** | 3 doses (0, 1-2 months, 6 months) |
| **Also recommended for** | MSM and immunocompromised persons through age 26 |

---

## 5.9 Doxycycline Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (Doxy-PEP)

- Doxycycline 200 mg within 72 hours after sex reduces syphilis, chlamydia, and gonorrhea in MSM and transgender women
- CDC approved Doxy-PEP in 2023 for persons with a history of bacterial STI in the past 12 months
- Does not reduce gonorrhea as effectively as chlamydia/syphilis; does not replace condoms
- Concerns about antimicrobial resistance with widespread use; ongoing monitoring

---

## 5.10 STI Red Flags

**[URGENT]**
- Acute pelvic or testicular pain (possible PID, epididymitis, torsion)
- Vision changes with syphilis (ocular syphilis - emergency)
- Hearing changes with syphilis (otosyphilis - emergency)
- Neurologic symptoms in syphilis (neurosyphilis - emergency)
- Severe genital ulceration
- Pregnant patient with active STI

---

# Chapter 6: Urinary Tract Infections

## 6.1 Overview

UTIs are among the most common bacterial infections encountered in clinical practice. Classification by anatomic location (lower = cystitis; upper = pyelonephritis) and complexity (uncomplicated vs. complicated) guides management. *E. coli* accounts for approximately 80% of uncomplicated community-acquired UTIs.

---

## 6.2 Uncomplicated Cystitis (Acute Simple Cystitis)

### Definition
Non-pregnant, immunocompetent women with acute cystitis and no structural/functional urinary tract abnormalities.

### Treatment (2024 Guidelines) [^266^]

| Antibiotic | Duration | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Nitrofurantoin** | 5 days | **First-line**; excellent *E. coli* activity; use with CrCl >=30 mL/min |
| **Fosfomycin tromethamine** | Single dose | Alternative first-line; excellent for suspected MDR organisms |
| **Pivmecillinam** | 3 days | Where available; good safety profile |
| **TMP-SMX** | 3 days | Only if local *E. coli* resistance <20% and no sulfa allergy |

### Second-Line/Reserve Options
- **Fluoroquinolones** (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin): 3 days - avoid due to collateral damage; reserve when alternatives unsuitable
- **Beta-lactams** (amoxicillin-clavulanate, cephalexin): 5-7 days - if other options unsuitable; higher failure rates than nitrofurantoin

### Key Points [^266^]
- **Nitrofurantoin is the drug of choice** for uncomplicated cystitis based on robust efficacy data and ability to spare systemically active agents
- Fluoroquinolones should not be used if other treatment options exist
- Beta-lactam duration is less certain due to heterogeneity in studies
- Obtain urine culture if: treatment failure expected, recurrent infection, complicated UTI, or atypical organism suspected

---

## 6.3 Complicated Cystitis / Complicated UTI

### Definition
UTIs in men, pregnant women, children, elderly, immunocompromised, catheter-associated, urinary tract abnormalities, recurrent UTIs, or recent instrumentation.

### Treatment Approach
- **Obtain urine culture before antibiotics** when feasible
- Empiric therapy based on local resistance and patient risk factors
- Longer duration typically needed (7-14 days depending on severity)
- **Ceftriaxone 1-2 g IV** recommended empiric choice for patients requiring IV therapy

### Catheter-Associated UTI (CAUTI)
- **Replace or remove urinary catheter** before starting antibiotics when possible
- Do NOT treat asymptomatic bacteriuria in catheterized patients
- Treat symptomatic CAUTI for 7 days (or 10-14 days if delayed response)

---

## 6.4 Pyelonephritis

### Treatment (2024 Guidelines) [^266^]

| Antibiotic | Duration | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Fluoroquinolones** | 5-7 days | Levofloxacin 750 mg daily or ciprofloxacin; highly bioavailable |
| **Dose-optimized beta-lactams** | 7 days | Based on RCTs showing comparable outcomes to longer regimens |
| **TMP-SMX** | 7-14 days | If susceptible; based on culture results |

### IV-to-Oral Transition
- Ceftriaxone 1-2 g IV daily for empiric therapy if hospitalization needed
- Transition to oral when clinically improving (typically 24-48 hours)
- Total duration 7-14 days depending on agent and clinical response

### Admission Criteria
- Hemodynamic instability
- Inability to tolerate oral therapy
- Pregnancy
- Significant comorbidities
- Uncertain social situation or inability to follow up
- Concern for complicated infection or obstruction

---

## 6.5 Recurrent UTIs

### Definition
>=2 episodes in 6 months or >=3 episodes in 1 year.

### Prevention Strategies [^266^]

| Strategy | Evidence Level | Details |
|---|---|---|
| **Cranberry products** | Clear recommendation | 36 mg proanthocyanidins (PACs) daily; reduces recurrences in women |
| **Vaginal estrogen** | Clear recommendation | For postmenopausal women; restores vaginal lactobacilli |
| **Methenamine hippurate** | Clear recommendation | 1 g BID; antimicrobial-sparing option; acidifies urine |
| **Continuous/postcoital antibiotics** | Clinical review | TMP-SMX, nitrofurantoin; effective but resistance concerns |
| **Increased water intake** | Clinical review | Additional 1.5 L daily may help |
| **Probiotics** | Insufficient evidence | Studies heterogeneous; lactobacilli show some promise |
| **D-mannose** | Emerging | 2 g daily; may reduce adhesion of *E. coli* |

---

## 6.6 Prostatitis

### Acute Bacterial Prostatitis
- **Pathogens:** *E. coli* most common; *Enterococcus*, *Pseudomonas*, *Klebsiella*
- **Presentation:** Fever, chills, pelvic/perineal pain, dysuria, urinary retention
- **Treatment:** Fluoroquinolone (ciprofloxacin 500 mg BID, levofloxacin 500 mg daily) or TMP-SMX for **2-4 weeks**
- If severe/systemically ill: IV ceftriaxone or fluoroquinolone initially
- **Avoid nitrofurantoin** (poor prostatic penetration) and fosfomycin (limited data)

### Chronic Bacterial Prostatitis
- Fluoroquinolones or TMP-SMX for **4-6 weeks**
- May require prolonged or suppressive therapy
- Culture-guided therapy preferred

---

## 6.7 Asymptomatic Bacteriuria

**General rule: Do NOT treat** including in elderly, diabetics, and spinal cord injury patients.

### Exceptions Where Treatment IS Indicated
| Population | Rationale |
|---|---|
| **Pregnancy** | Screen at first prenatal visit; treat if positive; nitrofurantoin or amoxicillin-clavulanate preferred |
| **Before urologic procedures** | With mucosal bleeding |
| **Renal transplant recipients** | First 1-2 months post-transplant |

### When NOT to Screen or Treat
- Elderly institutionalized patients (no benefit, may cause harm)
- Patients with spinal cord injury and indwelling catheters
- Diabetics without symptoms
- Screening and treatment not beneficial and may promote resistance

---

## 6.8 UTI Red Flags

**[EMERGENCY/URGENT]**
- Sepsis signs (fever, tachycardia, hypotension)
- Acute kidney injury
- Obstruction (anuria, hydronephrosis)
- Severe flank pain with systemic symptoms
- Pregnant patient with suspected pyelonephritis
- Immunocompromised patient with UTI symptoms

---

# Chapter 7: Skin and Soft Tissue Infections

## 7.1 Overview

Skin and soft tissue infections (SSTIs) encompass a broad spectrum from superficial infections like impetigo to life-threatening necrotizing fasciitis. Prompt recognition of severity and appropriate antimicrobial selection are essential. Community-associated MRSA (CA-MRSA) is now endemic in many communities.

---

## 7.2 Cellulitis and Erysipelas

### Clinical Differentiation
| Feature | Erysipelas | Cellulitis |
|---|---|---|
| **Depth** | Superficial (upper dermis) | Deep (dermis and subcutaneous tissue) |
| **Borders** | Well-demarcated, raised | Poorly demarcated |
| **Primary pathogens** | Group A Streptococcus (*S. pyogenes*) | Mixed streptococcal and staphylococcal |
| **Appearance** | Bright red, shiny, edematous | Diffuse erythema, warmth, swelling |

### Treatment
| Severity | Regimen | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| **Mild (outpatient)** | Cephalexin 500 mg QID OR Dicloxacillin 500 mg QID | 5-7 days |
| **MRSA risk or penicillin allergy** | TMP-SMX DS 1-2 tabs BID OR Doxycycline 100 mg BID | 5-7 days |
| **Severe (inpatient)** | Cefazolin OR Oxacillin/Nafcillin IV; add vancomycin if MRSA concern | Until clinical improvement, then oral |

**Important:** TMP-SMX and doxycycline have limited streptococcal coverage; add amoxicillin if concern for significant streptococcal component.

### Supportive Care
- Elevate affected limb above heart level
- Mark extent of erythema to monitor progression
- Recheck within 24-48 hours if outpatient

---

## 7.3 Impetigo

| Type | Pathogen | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| **Non-bullous** | *S. aureus* or *S. pyogenes* | Mupirocin 2% ointment TID x 5 days (limited lesions); oral cephalexin if extensive |
| **Bullous** | *S. aureus* (exfoliative toxins) | Oral dicloxacillin or cephalexin |

- **Post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis** can occur after impetigo (unlike pharyngitis)
- Maintain good hygiene; consider treating household contacts if recurrent

---

## 7.4 MRSA (Methicillin-Resistant *S. aureus*)

### Risk Factors
- Prior MRSA infection or colonization
- Recent hospitalization or long-term care residence
- Injection drug use
- Close contact with MRSA-infected person
- Athletes, military personnel, prisoners
- Men who have sex with men

### Treatment for Purulent SSTI

| Route | Options | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Oral** | TMP-SMX DS 1-2 tabs BID; Doxycycline 100 mg BID; Clindamycin 300-450 mg TID-QID | Check D-test for clindamycin inducible resistance |
| **IV** | Vancomycin (monitor trough); Daptomycin (not for pneumonia); Linezolid; Ceftaroline | For severe or inpatient infections |

### Decolonization (for recurrent infections)
- Mupirocin nasal ointment BID x 5 days
- Chlorhexidine body wash daily x 5 days
- Treat household members if ongoing transmission
- Launder clothing and bedding in hot water

---

## 7.5 Abscess Management

### Primary Treatment
- **Incision and drainage (I&D) is the primary treatment** for all abscesses
- Obtain cultures when possible (guides resistance surveillance)

### Indications for Adjunctive Antibiotics
- Surrounding cellulitis
- Systemic symptoms (fever, tachycardia)
- Immunocompromise
- Facial, hand, or genital location
- Failed I&D alone
- Multiple lesions
- Extremes of age

### Antibiotic Duration
- 5-10 days after I&D if antibiotics indicated
- Re-evaluate if no improvement within 48 hours

---

## 7.6 Necrotizing Fasciitis [^286^][^293^]

### [EMERGENCY - LIFE-THREATENING]

Necrotizing fasciitis is a surgical emergency with mortality of 20-40% even with optimal treatment.

### Clinical Clues ("Hard Signs")
| Sign | Description |
|---|---|
| **Pain out of proportion** | Severe pain beyond what examination would suggest |
| **Rapid progression** | Rapidly spreading erythema/edema beyond visible margins |
| **Skin changes** | Bullae, skin necrosis, ecchymosis, crepitus (subcutaneous gas) |
| **Systemic toxicity** | Fever, tachycardia, hypotension, altered mental status |

### LRINEC Score (Risk Stratification)
| Parameter | Points |
|---|---|
| WBC (per mm3) | <15: 0; 15-25: 1; >25: 2 |
| Hemoglobin (g/dL) | >13.5: 0; 11-13.5: 1; <11: 2 |
| Sodium (mmol/L) | >=135: 0; <135: 2 |
| Creatinine (mg/dL) | <=1.6: 0; >1.6: 2 |
| Glucose (mg/dL) | <=180: 0; >180: 1 |
| CRP (mg/L) | <150: 0; >=150: 4 |

- **Score >=6:** High risk for necrotizing fasciitis; surgical consultation urgent
- **Score >=8:** Strong predictor of necrotizing fasciitis

### Treatment
1. **Surgical emergency:** Immediate surgical debridement is mandatory and life-saving
2. **Empiric antibiotics (broad spectrum):**

| Component | Options | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **MRSA coverage** | Vancomycin OR Linezolid | MRSA |
| **Gram-negative and anaerobic** | Piperacillin-tazobactam OR Carbapenem | Polymicrobial coverage |
| **Toxin suppression + strep** | Clindamycin | Toxin suppression, GAS coverage |

- **Documented GAS necrotizing fasciitis:** Penicillin + Clindamycin
- **Duration:** Until definitive debridement and clinical improvement, typically 7-14 days; narrow based on cultures
- ICU admission required for hemodynamic monitoring and support

---

## 7.7 Diabetic Foot Infections [^279^]

### Classification (IDSA/IWGDF)
| Severity | Features |
|---|---|
| **Uninfected** | No systemic or local symptoms of infection |
| **Mild** | Superficial, limited to skin/subcutaneous tissue; erythema <2 cm |
| **Moderate** | Erythema >2 cm OR deeper structures (tendon, joint, bone) involved |
| **Severe** | Systemic toxicity or metabolic instability (fever, tachycardia, hypotension, acidosis) |

### Treatment Principles
| Severity | Treatment | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| **Mild** | Oral antibiotics targeting Staph and Strep; cephalexin or amoxicillin-clavulanate | 1-2 weeks |
| **Moderate** | Broad-spectrum oral or IV; cover MRSA if risk factors | 1-3 weeks |
| **Severe** | Broad-spectrum IV; include Pseudomonas if risk factors (warm climate, prior colonization) | 2-4 weeks |

### Key Management Points
- **Wound culture** from debrided tissue (not superficial swab)
- **Probe-to-bone test:** Positive test in diabetic foot ulcer has high positive predictive value for osteomyelitis
- **Osteomyelitis:** Treat 4-6 weeks if bone not resected; 2-5 days post-excision if bone fully resected
- **Wound care:** Debridement, offloading, glycemic control are essential
- **MRSA coverage:** If prior MRSA, high local prevalence, or severe infection
- **Surgical evaluation** for abscess, necrosis, or compartment syndrome

### MRSA Coverage Options
- Vancomycin (IV)
- Linezolid (oral/IV)
- Doxycycline (oral)
- TMP-SMX (oral)

---

## 7.8 SSTI Red Flags

**[EMERGENCY]**
- Necrotizing fasciitis (pain out of proportion, rapid spread, crepitus, bullae, skin necrosis)
- Signs of sepsis from SSTI (fever, tachycardia, hypotension, altered mental status)
- Orbital cellulitis (diplopia, proptosis, vision changes, restricted eye movement)
- Necrotizing infection in perineum (Fournier gangrene)
- Rapidly progressive infection despite antibiotics

---

# Chapter 8: Travel Medicine

## 8.1 Pre-Travel Consultation Framework

### Timing
- Ideally **4-6 weeks before travel** (some vaccines require multiple doses)
- Last-minute travelers can still benefit from partial protection

### Assessment Components
| Domain | Key Questions |
|---|---|
| **Destination** | Country, region, urban vs. rural, altitude |
| **Duration** | Short-term vs. long-term |
| **Activities** | Adventure, medical work, sexual exposure, animal contact |
| **Accommodations** | Hotel vs. camping vs. local homestays |
| **Medical history** | Chronic conditions, immunocompromise, pregnancy, allergies |
| **Medications** | Current meds, drug interactions with prophylaxis |

### Documentation
- International Certificate of Vaccination or Prophylaxis (ICVP) where required
- Medication list with generic names
- Emergency contact information
- Travel health insurance verification

---

## 8.2 Malaria Prophylaxis

### Risk Assessment
- Malaria risk varies by country, region, season, and altitude
- Check CDC Yellow Book or WHO malaria maps for current risk by destination
- *P. falciparum* is most deadly; chloroquine resistance is widespread

### Chemoprophylaxis Options

| Drug | Regimen | Start | Continue After Return | Contraindications/Cautions |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Atovaquone-proguanil (Malarone)** | Daily | 1-2 days before | 7 days | Pregnancy (avoid 1st trimester); CrCl <30 |
| **Doxycycline** | Daily | 1-2 days before | 4 weeks | Photosensitivity; esophagitis; pregnancy; children <8 years |
| **Mefloquine** | Weekly | >=2 weeks before | 4 weeks | Neuropsychiatric history; cardiac conduction abnormalities |
| **Tafenoquine (Arakoda)** | Daily loading then weekly | 3 days before | 1 dose after | **G6PD deficiency screening REQUIRED**; hemolytic anemia; pregnancy |
| **Primaquine** | Daily | 1-2 days before | 7 days | G6PD testing required; only for P. vivax/ovale in some areas |

### Key Points
- **No chemoprophylaxis is 100% effective**; mosquito bite prevention essential (DEET, permethrin-treated clothing, bed nets)
- Standby emergency treatment (artemether-lumefantrine or atovaquone-proguanil) for remote travel >24 hours from medical care
- Advise travelers to seek medical attention promptly for fever during or after travel to endemic areas

---

## 8.3 Traveler's Diarrhea

### Prevention
- **Food and water precautions:** "Boil it, cook it, peel it, or forget it"
- Hand hygiene with soap or alcohol-based sanitizer
- Bismuth subsalicylate (Pepto-Bismol) for short-term prophylaxis if high-risk travel
- **Antibiotic prophylaxis NOT routinely recommended**; consider for immunocompromised or critical travel

### Treatment by Severity
| Severity | Features | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| **Mild** | 1-2 loose stools/day, no fever, no blood | Loperamide, hydration, bismuth subsalicylate |
| **Moderate** | >=3 loose stools/day, distressing symptoms | Azithromycin 500 mg daily x 1-3 days OR Rifaximin 200 mg TID x 3 days; add loperamide if no blood/fever |
| **Severe** | Bloody diarrhea, fever, severe abdominal pain | Azithromycin 500 mg daily x 3 days; **avoid loperamide**; seek medical care |

**Important:** Fluoroquinolone resistance is widespread, especially *Campylobacter* in South/Southeast Asia; **azithromycin is preferred** empirically.

---

## 8.4 Yellow Fever [^272^][^273^]

- **Live attenuated vaccine; single dose provides lifelong protection**
- ICVP valid for life (booster requirement eliminated from IHR in 2016)
- Required for entry to some countries; recommended for travel to endemic/transitional risk areas
- **Contraindications:** Age <6 months, severe immunosuppression, thymus disorder, primary immunodeficiency
- **Precautions:** Age 6-8 months, >=60 years, pregnancy, breastfeeding, asymptomatic HIV with CD4 >200
- **Risks:** Viscerotropic disease (YEL-AVD) and neurotropic disease (YEL-AND) - rare but serious

---

## 8.5 Dengue

- **No routine pre-travel prophylaxis** (except Qdenga/TAK-003 vaccine in some endemic regions for select travelers)
- **Prevention:** Mosquito avoidance (Aedes aegypti is a daytime biter)
- **Clinical:** Fever, severe headache, retro-orbital pain, myalgia, arthralgia, rash ("breakbone fever")
- **Warning signs for severe dengue:** Abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, mucosal bleeding, lethargy, hepatomegaly, rising hematocrit
- **Severe dengue** (plasma leakage, hemorrhage, organ impairment) requires hospitalization

---

## 8.6 Zika

- **Prevention:** Mosquito avoidance; sexual transmission possible
- **Pregnancy:** Avoid travel to endemic areas if possible; if travel necessary, strict mosquito precautions
  - Male partners: use condoms or abstain for 3 months after return
  - Females: wait 2 months before conceiving
- Most infections asymptomatic or mild; Guillain-Barre syndrome is rare complication

---

## 8.7 Travel Vaccinations Summary

| Vaccine | Indication | Dosing | Duration |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hepatitis A** | Most travelers to developing countries | 2 doses (0, 6-12 months) | Lifelong |
| **Hepatitis B** | Long-term travel, healthcare work, adventure travel, sexual exposure | 2 doses (Heplisav-B: 0, 1 month) or 3 doses (0, 1, 6 months) | Lifelong |
| **Typhoid** | Travel to endemic areas with poor sanitation | Oral (4 doses every other day) or IM single dose | Oral: 5 years; IM: 2 years |
| **Japanese Encephalitis** | Rural/farm exposure in endemic Asia >=1 month | 2 doses (0, 28 days) | Booster at 1 year, then every 10 years |
| **Rabies (pre-exposure)** | Adventure travel, remote areas, animal exposure | 3 doses (0, 7, 21 or 28 days) | Simplifies post-exposure management |
| **Meningococcal ACWY** | Hajj/Umrah, meningitis belt of Africa | Single dose | 3-5 years |
| **Polio** | Travel to polio-endemic or outbreak areas | Single booster if completed primary series | Lifetime |
| **MMR** | Travelers without immunity | 1-2 doses | Lifelong |

## 8.8 Travel Medicine Red Flags

**[EMERGENCY]**
- Fever in returned traveler from malaria-endemic area (**treat as malaria until proven otherwise**)
- Severe dengue warning signs in endemic area return
- Jaundice with fever (yellow fever, hepatitis, leptospirosis)
- Altered mental status with fever (malaria, typhoid, meningitis)
- Bloody diarrhea with systemic toxicity

# Chapter 9: Tick-Borne Diseases

## 9.1 Overview

Tick-borne diseases are the most common vector-borne illnesses in the United States, with Lyme disease accounting for the majority. The *Ixodes scapularis* (black-legged/deer tick) is the primary vector in the Northeast, Mid-Atlantic, and Upper Midwest, while *Ixodes pacificus* transmits disease on the West Coast. Co-infection with multiple tick-borne pathogens is possible and should be considered in patients from endemic areas with atypical or severe presentations.

---

## 9.2 Lyme Disease (*Borrelia burgdorferi*) [^277^][^278^]

### Epidemiology
- Most common vector-borne disease in the US
- Endemic areas: Northeast, Mid-Atlantic, Upper Midwest, parts of West Coast
- Also prevalent in Europe and Asia (different *Borrelia* species)
- **Transmission typically requires tick attachment >=36 hours**
- Peak incidence: May through August

### Clinical Stages and Treatment

| Stage | Clinical Features | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| **Early localized** | Erythema migrans (EM): expanding erythematous rash, often target-like or bull's-eye; may have fever, malaise, regional lymphadenopathy | Doxycycline 100 mg BID x 10 days; OR Amoxicillin 500 mg TID x 14 days; OR Cefuroxime 500 mg BID x 14 days |
| **Early disseminated** | Multiple EM lesions, cranial nerve palsy (especially CN VII - facial nerve), meningitis, carditis (AV block) | Doxycycline 100 mg BID x 14-21 days; IV ceftriaxone for severe meningitis/carditis |
| **Late disseminated** | Arthritis (especially knee - large effusion), encephalomyelitis, peripheral neuropathy | Oral doxycycline x 28 days for arthritis; IV ceftriaxone 2 g daily x 2-4 weeks for neurologic |

### Detailed EM Rash Treatment [^277^]

| Population | Preferred Regimen | Alternatives |
|---|---|---|
| **Adults** | Doxycycline 100 mg BID x 10-14 days | Amoxicillin 500 mg TID x 14 days; Cefuroxime 500 mg BID x 14 days |
| **Children** | Doxycycline 4.4 mg/kg/day divided BID (max 100 mg/dose) x 10-14 days | Amoxicillin 50 mg/kg/day divided TID x 14 days |
| **Pregnancy** | Amoxicillin 500 mg TID x 14 days | Cefuroxime 500 mg BID x 14 days |

- **Shorter duration preferred** when different durations shown effective
- Azithromycin is less effective and reserved for true contraindications to first-line agents

### Single-Dose Doxycycline Prophylaxis
- **200 mg single dose** for adults (4.4 mg/kg up to 200 mg for children)
- Must be given **within 72 hours of tick removal**
- **Criteria for prophylaxis:**
  1. Tick identified as *Ixodes* species
  2. Attached >=36 hours
  3. Removed in an endemic area
  4. Prophylaxis can be given within 72 hours of removal
- If criteria not met, observe for rash/symptoms for 30 days

### Post-Treatment Lyme Disease Syndrome (PTLDS)
- 10-20% of patients have persistent symptoms (fatigue, pain, cognitive issues) after appropriate antibiotic treatment
- **No evidence that additional antibiotics confer benefit**
- Manage symptoms with NSAIDs, physical therapy, supportive care
- Refer to rheumatology/neurology as appropriate

### Diagnosis
- **Clinical diagnosis** in endemic areas with classic EM rash (serology not needed)
- **Two-tiered testing** (ELISA followed by Western blot) for non-EM manifestations or non-endemic areas
- Serology may be negative in early infection (first 1-2 weeks)

---

## 9.3 Babesiosis (*Babesia microti*)

### Overview
- Vector: Same *Ixodes* ticks as Lyme disease
- Pathogen: Intraerythrocytic protozoan parasite
- Geographic distribution similar to Lyme but more focal

### Clinical Features
- Fever, chills, sweats, fatigue
- Hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia
- Elevated liver transaminases
- Can be asymptomatic in healthy individuals

### Diagnosis
- **Peripheral blood smear:** Intraerythrocytic parasites (tetrad "Maltese cross" is diagnostic but not always seen)
- **PCR:** More sensitive than smear; preferred for diagnosis
- **Serology:** Supportive; 4-fold rise in acute/convalescent titers

### Treatment

| Severity | Regimen | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| **Mild-moderate** | Atovaquone 750 mg BID + Azithromycin 500 mg day 1, then 250 mg daily | 7-10 days |
| **Severe** | Clindamycin IV + Quinine PO | 7-10 days |

- **Severe criteria:** High-grade parasitemia (>10%), severe hemolysis, organ failure, immunocompromise
- Exchange transfusion for high-grade parasitemia (>10%), severe hemolysis, or organ failure
- **Always test for co-infection with Lyme and anaplasmosis**
- Asymptomatic persons with positive blood smear and low parasitemia may not need treatment if immunocompetent

---

## 9.4 Anaplasmosis (*Anaplasma phagocytophilum*)

### Overview
- Previously called human granulocytic ehrlichiosis (HGE)
- Vector: *Ixodes scapularis*
- Obligate intracellular pathogen infecting neutrophils

### Clinical Features
- Fever, headache, myalgia ("flu-like" but often without respiratory symptoms)
- **Leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, elevated transaminases** (distinguishing from influenza/COVID)
- Morulae may be seen in neutrophils on peripheral smear (sensitive but not specific)

### Diagnosis
- **PCR during acute illness** (most sensitive in first week)
- Serology: 4-fold rise in antibody titer (convalescent titers)
- CBC with differential, LFTs supportive

### Treatment
- **Doxycycline 100 mg BID x 10-14 days** (first-line for all ages)
- **Start empirically if clinical suspicion**; do not delay for laboratory confirmation
- Defervescence typically within 24-48 hours of treatment
- **Children <8 years:** Doxycycline is still first-line for suspected tick-borne rickettsial disease; benefits outweigh risks of tooth staining

---

## 9.5 Ehrlichiosis (*Ehrlichia chaffeensis*, *E. ewingii*)

### Overview
- Vector: Lone star tick (*Amblyomma americanum*)
- Geographic distribution: South-Central, Southeastern, and Eastern US
- Obligate intracellular pathogen infecting monocytes (*E. chaffeensis*) or granulocytes (*E. ewingii*)

### Clinical Features
- Similar to anaplasmosis: fever, headache, myalgia
- Leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, elevated LFTs
- Morulae in monocytes (E. chaffeensis) or granulocytes (E. ewingii)

### Treatment
- **Doxycycline 100 mg BID x 7-14 days** (same as anaplasmosis)
- Start empirically if clinical suspicion; do not delay

---

## 9.6 Rocky Mountain Spotted Fever (RMSF) - *Rickettsia rickettsii*

### [EMERGENCY - POTENTIALLY FATAL]

- Most severe tick-borne rickettsial disease in the US
- Mortality up to 5-10% even with treatment; much higher if untreated
- **Treatment:** Doxycycline 100 mg BID x 5-7 days; **start immediately** on clinical suspicion
- Classic triad: fever, rash (wrists/ankles spreading centripetally), tick exposure history
- **Rash may be late or absent**; do not wait for rash to treat

---

## 9.7 Co-Infection Considerations

*Ixodes* ticks can transmit Lyme, babesiosis, and anaplasmosis simultaneously. Consider co-infection in patients from endemic areas with:
- Severe or relapsing illness despite appropriate Lyme treatment (babesiosis)
- Leukopenia or thrombocytopenia with Lyme disease features (anaplasmosis)
- Hemolytic anemia with Lyme disease features (babesiosis)
- Failure to defervesce within 72 hours of appropriate Lyme therapy

---

## 9.8 Tick-Borne Disease Red Flags

**[EMERGENCY]**
- Suspected RMSF (start doxycycline empirically)
- Severe babesiosis (high parasitemia, hemolysis)
- Lyme carditis with high-grade AV block
- Severe neurologic symptoms (meningitis, encephalitis)
- Sepsis-like presentation from any tick-borne illness

---

# Chapter 10: Other Important Infections

## 10.1 *Clostridioides difficile* Infection (CDI) [^95^]

### Overview
*C. difficile* is the most common cause of infectious diarrhea in healthcare settings. Disruption of normal gut flora (most often by antibiotics) allows *C. difficile* overgrowth and toxin production. The spectrum ranges from mild watery diarrhea to life-threatening pseudomembranous colitis, toxic megacolon, and sepsis.

### Classification
| Category | Criteria |
|---|---|
| **Non-severe** | WBC <15,000 cells/mcL AND creatinine <1.5x baseline |
| **Severe** | WBC >=15,000 cells/mcL OR creatinine >=1.5x baseline |
| **Severe-complicated/Fulminant** | Hypotension, shock, ileus, toxic megacolon |

### Treatment (Updated IDSA/SHEA and ESCMID Guidelines)

| Severity | First-Line | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Initial episode, non-severe** | **Fidaxomicin** 200 mg BID x 10 days | Preferred over vancomycin; lower recurrence |
| **Initial episode, severe** | **Fidaxomicin** 200 mg BID x 10 days | Preferred |
| **Alternative** | **Vancomycin** 125 mg PO QID x 10 days | Acceptable if fidaxomicin unavailable |
| **Severe-complicated** | Vancomycin 500 mg PO/NG QID + Metronidazole 500 mg IV q8h + Surgery consult | If ileus: add vancomycin enemas |
| **First recurrence** | Fidaxomicin (standard or extended-pulsed) | Extended-pulsed: 200 mg BID x 5 days, then daily x 20 days |
| **Multiple recurrences (>=2)** | Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) OR Bezlotoxumab + standard antibiotic | FMT most effective for >=3 recurrences |

### Key Points [^95^]
- **Fidaxomicin is now first-line** for initial episodes (both non-severe and severe) due to superior prevention of recurrence
- **Metronidazole is no longer preferred** (IDSA/SHEA); only for situations where vancomycin/fidaxomicin unavailable
- **Bezlotoxumab** (monoclonal antibody against toxin B) reduces recurrence when added to standard antibiotic therapy
- FMT retains role in multiple recurrences; timing in severe-complicated disease still being studied
- **Avoid antiperistaltic agents** (loperamide, diphenoxylate) if toxic megacolon suspected
- **Surgical consultation** (subtotal colectomy) for fulminant colitis with organ failure

### Prevention
- Antibiotic stewardship: use narrowest spectrum for shortest effective duration
- Contact precautions for infected patients
- Hand hygiene with soap and water (alcohol-based sanitizers do not kill spores)
- Environmental cleaning with sporicidal agents

---

## 10.2 Community-Acquired Pneumonia (CAP) [^25^]

### Common Pathogens
| Category | Organisms |
|---|---|
| **Typical bacteria** | *Streptococcus pneumoniae* (most common), *Haemophilus influenzae*, *Moraxella catarrhalis*, *S. aureus*, *Klebsiella pneumoniae* |
| **Atypical pathogens** | *Mycoplasma pneumoniae*, *Chlamydophila pneumoniae*, *Legionella pneumophila* |
| **Viruses** | Influenza, SARS-CoV-2, RSV, adenovirus, parainfluenza |

### Severity Assessment
| Tool | Components | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **CURB-65** | Confusion, Urea >7 mmol/L, RR >=30, BP (SBP <90 or DBP <=60), Age >=65 | 0-1: outpatient; 2: hospitalize; 3-5: ICU |
| **PSI (Pneumonia Severity Index)** | More complex; age, comorbidities, vitals, labs, imaging | Classes I-II: outpatient; III: observe; IV-V: hospitalize |

### Outpatient Treatment [^25^]
| Patient Type | Regimen |
|---|---|
| **No comorbidities** | Amoxicillin 1 g TID OR Doxycycline 100 mg BID OR Macrolide (if local pneumococcal resistance <25% AND no cardiac risks) |
| **With comorbidities** (heart, lung, liver, kidney disease, diabetes, alcoholism, malignancy) | Amoxicillin-clavulanate 875/125 mg BID OR Cephalosporin + Macrolide OR Doxycycline; OR Respiratory fluoroquinolone monotherapy |

### Inpatient Treatment [^25^]
| Setting | Regimen |
|---|---|
| **Non-severe** | Beta-lactam + Macrolide (azithromycin) OR Respiratory fluoroquinolone monotherapy |
| **Severe/ICU** | Beta-lactam + Macrolide (STRONG recommendation) OR Beta-lactam + Fluoroquinolone |
| **MRSA risk** | Add vancomycin or linezolid; obtain cultures/nasal PCR; de-escalate if negative |
| **Pseudomonas risk** | Add piperacillin-tazobactam, cefepime, or meropenem; obtain cultures |

**Beta-lactam options:** Ceftriaxone 1-2 g daily, ampicillin-sulbactam, cefotaxime, ceftaroline.

### Key Management Points
- **Corticosteroids NOT recommended routinely**; may be considered in refractory septic shock
- **Minimum duration: 5 days**; extend based on clinical response; stop when afebrile 48-72 hours and clinically stable
- **Follow-up imaging NOT routinely recommended** for uncomplicated CAP
- **De-escalation:** Review cultures and susceptibilities within 24-48 hours; narrow spectrum accordingly

---

## 10.3 Bacterial Meningitis [^281^]

### [EMERGENCY - START TREATMENT WITHIN 1 HOUR]

### Empiric Treatment by Age and Risk Factor

| Population | Empiric Regimen |
|---|---|
| **Neonates (0-1 month)** | Ampicillin + Cefotaxime +/- Gentamicin (covers Listeria, GBS, gram-negatives) |
| **1 month - 50 years** | Vancomycin + Ceftriaxone (or Cefotaxime) +/- Ampicillin (if Listeria risk) |
| **>50 years** | Vancomycin + Ceftriaxone + Ampicillin (Listeria coverage) |
| **Immunocompromised** | Vancomycin + Ceftriaxone + Ampicillin +/- Antipseudomonal |
| **Head trauma/surgery/CSF shunt** | Vancomycin + Cefepime or Meropenem |

### Pathogen-Specific Therapy
| Pathogen | Treatment | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| *S. pneumoniae* (PCN-susceptible) | Penicillin G or Ampicillin | 10-14 days |
| *S. pneumoniae* (PCN-resistant) | Ceftriaxone +/- Vancomycin | 10-14 days |
| *N. meningitidis* | Ceftriaxone or Penicillin G | 5-7 days |
| *H. influenzae* | Ceftriaxone or Cefotaxime | 7-10 days |
| *Listeria monocytogenes* | Ampicillin + Gentamicin | >=21 days |
| GBS (*S. agalactiae*) | Ampicillin + Gentamicin | 14-21 days |

### Adjunctive Dexamethasone [^281^]
- **Strong recommendation:** Start with or before first antibiotic dose
- **Dose:** Dexamethasone 0.15 mg/kg IV q6h x 2-4 days (10 mg IV q6h for adults)
- **Specific benefit:** Reduces mortality and neurologic sequelae in pneumococcal meningitis
- **Timing critical:** Must start BEFORE or WITH first antibiotic dose
- **Do NOT use** if meningococcal epidemic is suspected or if antibiotics already started
- Discontinue if pathogen is not *S. pneumoniae*

### General Principles
- Start empiric antibiotics ideally within 1 hour of admission
- Blood cultures before antibiotics if no delay
- LP should be performed promptly but should NOT delay antibiotics if critically ill
- If LP delayed due to CT/concerns, start antibiotics immediately
- CT before LP if: immunocompromise, history of CNS disease, new-onset seizure, papilledema, altered consciousness, focal neurologic deficit

## 10.4 Endocarditis Prophylaxis [^282^][^284^][^300^]

### Current AHA Guidelines
Prophylaxis is suggested **ONLY** for patients at **highest risk of adverse outcome** from viridans group streptococcal infective endocarditis (IE) undergoing **dental procedures** involving manipulation of gingival tissue, periapical region, or perforation of oral mucosa.

### Cardiac Conditions Requiring Prophylaxis
| Condition | Risk Level |
|---|---|
| Prosthetic cardiac valve or prosthetic material used for valve repair | **Highest risk** |
| Previous infective endocarditis | **Highest risk** |
| Unrepaired cyanotic congenital heart disease (CHD), including palliative shunts/conduits | **Highest risk** |
| Completely repaired CHD with prosthetic material within first 6 months of procedure | **Highest risk** |
| Repaired CHD with residual defects at site of prosthetic patch/device | **Highest risk** |
| Cardiac transplant recipients with valvulopathy | **Highest risk** |

### Regimens (Single Dose, 30-60 Minutes Before Procedure) [^300^]

| Patient Situation | Regimen |
|---|---|
| **Standard (oral)** | Amoxicillin 2 g orally |
| **Unable to take oral** | Ampicillin 2 g IV/IM OR Cefazolin/Ceftriaxone 1 g IV/IM |
| **Penicillin-allergic, not anaphylactic (oral)** | Cephalexin 2 g orally OR Azithromycin/Clarithromycin 500 mg orally |
| **Penicillin-allergic, not anaphylactic (IV/IM)** | Cefazolin/Ceftriaxone 1 g IV/IM |
| **Penicillin-anaphylactic** | Azithromycin/Clarithromycin 500 mg orally OR Clindamycin 600 mg IV/IM |

**Note:** Clindamycin is no longer routinely recommended as an alternative due to higher rates of adverse reactions including *C. difficile* infection. Cephalexin is preferred for penicillin-allergic patients who have not had anaphylaxis.

### Procedures NOT Requiring Prophylaxis
- Anesthetic injections through noninfected tissue
- Dental radiographs
- Placement of orthodontic brackets
- Shedding of primary teeth
- Tonsillectomy, respiratory procedures not involving mucosal incision
- GI/GU procedures (prophylaxis not recommended for endocarditis prevention)

## 10.5 Osteomyelitis [^279^]

### Classification
| Type | Characteristics |
|---|---|
| **Hematogenous** | Often affects long bones (children) or vertebral bodies (adults) |
| **Contiguous** | Adjacent to soft tissue infection or hardware; common in diabetic foot |
| **Vascular insufficiency** | Associated with peripheral vascular disease, diabetes |

### Treatment Principles
| Type | Duration | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Acute hematogenous** | 4-6 weeks total | IV x 2-4 weeks, then oral |
| **Chronic/contiguous** | 4-6 weeks minimum | Often requires surgical debridement |
| **Diabetic foot osteomyelitis (bone retained)** | 4-6 weeks | Guided by cultures |
| **Diabetic foot (bone resected)** | 2-5 days post-excision | Short course after definitive surgery |

### Antibiotic Options
| Route | Options |
|---|---|
| **Oral (for chronic/completion)** | Doxycycline +/- metronidazole, TMP-SMX, Amoxicillin-clavulanate (culture-guided) |
| **IV (for acute/severe)** | Flucloxacillin, Ceftriaxone, Ertapenem, Vancomycin (MRSA), Piperacillin-tazobactam |

### Key Management Points
- **Surgical debridement** often necessary for chronic osteomyelitis or when hardware present
- **Obtain tissue cultures** (bone biopsy preferred over superficial swabs)
- **Probe-to-bone test:** Positive test in diabetic foot ulcer has high positive predictive value for osteomyelitis
- **MRI** is most sensitive imaging modality for detecting osteomyelitis
- Consider removing infected hardware when possible

## 10.6 Sepsis and Septic Shock - Empiric Antibiotics

| Source | Empiric Regimen | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Unknown source** | Vancomycin + Piperacillin-tazobactam OR Cefepime | Broad coverage for MRSA, Pseudomonas, anaerobes |
| **Urosepsis** | Ceftriaxone or Piperacillin-tazobactam +/- Vancomycin | Add vancomycin if healthcare-associated or catheter-related |
| **Intra-abdominal** | Piperacillin-tazobactam OR Ceftriaxone + Metronidazole | 4 days if source controlled |
| **Necrotizing SSTI** | Vancomycin + Piperacillin-tazobactam + Clindamycin | Urgent surgical debridement |
| **Meningitis** | Vancomycin + Ceftriaxone + Ampicillin + Dexamethasone | Start within 1 hour |
| **Febrile neutropenia** | Cefepime or Piperacillin-tazobactam or Meropenem + Vancomycin (if indicated) | Until afebrile 7 days, ANC >500 |

### Hour-1 Bundle (Surviving Sepsis Campaign)
1. Measure lactate; remeasure if elevated
2. Obtain blood cultures before antibiotics (do not delay antibiotics)
3. Administer broad-spectrum antibiotics within 1 hour
4. Rapid administration of 30 mL/kg crystalloid for hypotension or lactate >=4 mmol/L
5. Apply vasopressors if hypotensive after fluid resuscitation

---

## 10.7 Red Flags Across Severe Infections

**[EMERGENCY]**
- **Sepsis/Septic shock:** Altered mental status, hypotension (SBP <90), tachycardia >120, poor perfusion, lactate >2
- **Immunocompromised host with infection:** Unusual pathogens, atypical presentations
- **Travel-related fever:** Malaria, typhoid, dengue in returned travelers - urgent evaluation required
- **Necrotizing infection:** Rapid progression, severe pain, skin necrosis, crepitus
- **Meningitis signs:** Altered mental status, photophobia, neck stiffness, petechial rash

# Chapter 11: Antimicrobial Stewardship

## 11.1 Overview

Antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) is a coordinated set of strategies to improve appropriate antimicrobial use, optimize clinical outcomes, minimize adverse effects, and reduce antimicrobial resistance. The CDC estimates that at least 30% of antibiotics prescribed in outpatient settings are unnecessary.

---

## 11.2 Core Principles

### Appropriate Selection
| Principle | Action |
|---|---|
| **Obtain cultures** | Before starting antibiotics when clinically feasible (do NOT delay antibiotics in critically ill patients for cultures) |
| **Use local antibiogram data** | Guide empiric therapy based on institutional/regional resistance patterns |
| **Follow IDSA guidelines** | Evidence-based recommendations for specific infection syndromes |
| **Narrowest appropriate spectrum** | Choose based on suspected pathogens and site of infection |
| **Patient-specific factors** | Consider allergies, renal/hepatic function, pregnancy, drug interactions |

### De-Escalation
- Review culture and susceptibility results within 24-48 hours of availability
- Narrow antibiotic spectrum based on identified pathogen and sensitivities
- Discontinue empiric coverage for resistant organisms if not identified
- CAP guidelines specifically recommend de-escalation of MRSA and Pseudomonas coverage if cultures negative and rapid PCR negative [^25^]

### Appropriate Duration
| Infection | Evidence-Based Duration |
|---|---|
| Uncomplicated cystitis | 3-5 days (nitrofurantoin 5 days, TMP-SMX 3 days, fosfomycin single dose) |
| Pyelonephritis | 5-7 days (fluoroquinolones), 7 days (beta-lactams) |
| Community-acquired pneumonia | Minimum 5 days; stop when afebrile 48-72 hours and clinically stable |
| Cellulitis | 5-7 days |
| Intra-abdominal (source controlled) | 4 days |
| Acute bacterial sinusitis | 5-7 days |
| Acute exacerbation of COPD | 5 days |

### IV-to-Oral Conversion
Switch to oral when ALL of the following are met:
- Clinically improving
- Able to tolerate oral intake
- No GI malabsorption concerns
- Bioavailable oral option available

**Agents with excellent oral bioavailability:**
| Drug | Oral Bioavailability |
|---|---|
| Fluoroquinolones (ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin, moxifloxacin) | >90% |
| TMP-SMX | >90% |
| Metronidazole | >90% |
| Linezolid | 100% |
| Doxycycline | >90% |
| Clindamycin | >90% |
| Azithromycin | >90% |

---

## 11.3 Antibiotic Resistance Patterns (Current Trends)

### Extended-Spectrum Beta-Lactamase (ESBL)-Producing Enterobacteriaceae
- Increasing prevalence globally; no longer limited to healthcare settings
- Community-acquired UTIs with ESBL *E. coli* increasingly common
- **Treatment:** Carbapenems (ertapenem, meropenem) preferred for severe infections
- **Oral options for cystitis:** Fosfomycin, nitrofurantoin may retain activity

### Carbapenem-Resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE)
- Major global health threat; mortality rates up to 50% in bloodstream infections
- **Treatment options:** Ceftazidime-avibactam, Meropenem-vaborbactam, Imipenem-relebactam, Cefiderocol
- Reserve agents: Colistin, polymyxin B, tigecycline (toxicity concerns)
- **Consult infectious disease specialists** for all CRE infections

### MRSA
| Type | Characteristics | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| **CA-MRSA** | SCCmec type IV, PVL-positive; community onset | Oral: TMP-SMX, doxycycline, clindamycin |
| **HA-MRSA** | SCCmec type II, multidrug-resistant; healthcare-associated | IV: Vancomycin, daptomycin, linezolid |

- **Oral options:** TMP-SMX, doxycycline/minocycline, clindamycin (check D-test), linezolid
- **IV options:** Vancomycin (monitor trough levels), daptomycin (not for pneumonia), linezolid, ceftaroline, dalbavancin, oritavancin

### Fluoroquinolone Resistance
- High rates in *E. coli* (>20% in many regions), limiting fluoroquinolone utility for empiric UTI therapy
- **FDA black box warnings:** Tendinopathy/rupture, peripheral neuropathy, CNS effects, QT prolongation, aortic aneurysm/dissection, *C. difficile* risk
- **Reserve fluoroquinolones** for situations where no alternatives exist

### Vancomycin-Resistant Enterococcus (VRE)
- Primarily *E. faecium*; *E. faecalis* less commonly resistant
- Linezolid, daptomycin are primary treatment options
- Fecal microbiota transplantation under investigation for VRE decolonization

---

## 11.4 Biomarker-Guided Therapy

| Biomarker | Role in Stewardship | Evidence Level |
|---|---|---|
| **Procalcitonin** | NOT recommended to decide whether to initiate antibiotics in CAP [^25^]; may guide discontinuation in respiratory infections and sepsis | Moderate |
| **C-reactive protein (CRP)** | Can support clinical judgment but should not replace it | Low-Moderate |
| **SeptiCyte Lab** | Gene expression assay for differentiating bacterial vs viral infection | Emerging |

---

## 11.5 Documentation and Communication

- **Document indication** for every antibiotic prescription
- Record expected duration and plan for de-escalation at time of initiation
- Implement antibiotic "time-outs" at 48-72 hours to reassess need and spectrum
- Communicate allergy histories clearly (distinguish true IgE-mediated allergy from intolerance)
- Penicillin allergy de-labeling programs have high success rates and should be used when appropriate

---

## 11.6 AMS Red Flags

| Issue | Action |
|---|---|
| Prolonged broad-spectrum antibiotic use without de-escalation | Reassess within 48-72 hours |
| Antibiotics prescribed for viral infections | Educate; provide symptomatic care |
| Duplicate gram-negative coverage | Streamline; choose one agent |
| Treatment of asymptomatic bacteriuria | Stop antibiotics (with rare exceptions) |
| Missing culture data | Obtain before or at time of antibiotic initiation |

## 12.1 Overview

Vaccination is one of the most effective public health interventions for preventing infectious diseases. This chapter summarizes the CDC Adult Immunization Schedule 2024-2025 [^274^][^275^][^276^], catch-up guidance, and special population considerations.

## 12.3 Key Vaccine Details

### Pneumococcal (Adults >=65 Years or High-Risk)
| Option | Schedule |
|---|---|
| **Option 1 (Preferred)** | Single dose of PCV20 or PCV21 |
| **Option 2** | PCV15 first, then PPSV23 at least 1 year later |
| **Previously received PPSV23** | PCV20 or PCV21 at least 1 year after PPSV23 |

- PCV20/21 cover 20-21 serotypes; simplifies schedule
- High-risk conditions: immunocompromise, chronic heart/lung/liver disease, diabetes, alcoholism, smoking, CSF leak, cochlear implant

### Zoster (Shingrix) - Recombinant Adjuvanted
- 2 doses (0.5 mL each) given 2-6 months apart
- Recommended for **all adults >=50 years** and **immunocompromised adults >=19 years**
- Efficacy: >90% prevention of shingles and post-herpetic neuralgia
- Even if history of shingles or prior Zostavax vaccination
- Most common adverse effect: injection site reactions

### Hepatitis B (Universal Recommendation)
- **All adults aged 19-59 years** should receive HepB vaccine
- Adults >=60 years: risk-based or upon patient request
- **Heplisav-B:** 2 doses at 0, 1 month (higher seroprotection rates)
- **PreHevbrio:** 3 doses at 0, 1, 6 months
- **Engerix-B/Recombivax:** 3 doses at 0, 1, 6 months

### RSV (Respiratory Syncytial Virus)
- **Adults >=75 years:** 1 dose recommended
- **Adults 60-74 years at increased risk** (chronic cardiopulmonary disease, immunocompromise, diabetes, nursing home residence): shared clinical decision
- **Pregnancy:** Seasonal administration during weeks 32-36 gestation (Abrysvo) to protect infant

### COVID-19 (2024-2025 Vaccines) [^294^][^298^]
- **Moderna (Spikevax):** mRNA vaccine
- **Pfizer-BioNTech (Comirnaty):** mRNA vaccine
- **Novavax:** Protein subunit vaccine; 2-dose primary series if unvaccinated
- Adults >=65: 2 doses of 2024-25 vaccine, 6 months apart (minimum 2 months)
- Immunocompromised: additional doses per shared clinical decision-making

## 12.6 Contraindications and Precautions

### Absolute Contraindications
- Severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis) to a previous dose or vaccine component
- Live vaccines in pregnancy or severe immunosuppression

### Precautions
- Moderate or severe acute illness (defer vaccination)
- History of Guillain-Barre syndrome within 6 weeks of influenza vaccine
- Egg allergy is NOT a contraindication to influenza vaccination

## 12.7 Quick Reference: Emergency Antibiotics

| Condition | First-Line Empiric Regimen | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| **Sepsis (unknown source)** | Vancomycin + Piperacillin-tazobactam or Cefepime | Per source control |
| **Meningitis (adult)** | Vancomycin + Ceftriaxone + Ampicillin + Dexamethasone | 10-21 days (pathogen-dependent) |
| **Necrotizing fasciitis** | Vancomycin + Piperacillin-tazobactam + Clindamycin + Urgent surgery | Until debridement complete |
| **Severe CAP (ICU)** | Ceftriaxone + Azithromycin OR Beta-lactam + Fluoroquinolone | Minimum 5 days |
| **Febrile neutropenia** | Cefepime or Piperacillin-tazobactam or Meropenem + Vancomycin (if indicated) | Until afebrile 7 days, ANC >500 |
| **Severe sepsis/urosepsis** | Ceftriaxone or Piperacillin-tazobactam +/- Vancomycin | 7-14 days |
| **Community-acquired abdominal sepsis** | Piperacillin-tazobactam or Ceftriaxone + Metronidazole | 4 days (if source controlled) |
| **Toxic megacolon/C. difficile** | Vancomycin 500 mg PO/NG QID + Metronidazole IV + Surgery consult | Per clinical response |

## 12.9 Red Flags for Severe Infections - Summary

| System | Red Flags | Differential |
|---|---|---|
| **Respiratory** | O2 sat <94%, RR >=30, cyanosis, altered mental status | Severe CAP, COVID-19, influenza pneumonia |
| **CNS** | Altered mental status, neck stiffness, photophobia, petechial rash, focal deficits | Meningitis, encephalitis, RMSF |
| **Cardiovascular** | Hypotension (SBP <90), tachycardia >120, poor perfusion | Sepsis, endocarditis, myocarditis |
| **GI/Abdominal** | Severe abdominal pain, rigid abdomen, bloody diarrhea with fever, ileus | Necrotizing fasciitis, C. difficile, typhoid |
| **Skin/Soft tissue** | Pain out of proportion, rapid spread, crepitus, bullae, skin necrosis | Necrotizing fasciitis, Fournier gangrene |
| **Genitourinary** | Fever with flank pain, sepsis, AKI, obstruction | Pyelonephritis, urosepsis |
| **Systemic** | Fever in returned traveler, fever in neutropenic patient, fever in asplenic patient | Malaria, typhoid, dengue, VZV, encapsulated bacteria |
